## Core thesis
A podcast-style reflection. Alex set a goal of beating a gym-industry guru (captioned "Badros/Bedro"), out of a chip on his shoulder. When he passed him, the win felt empty. The rival wasn't affected at all, and the vendetta only poisoned Alex. Use anger as fuel if it drives you, but never use it to attack. Most markets aren't winner-take-all, so collaborating with "competitors" usually makes everyone more money.

## Frameworks & models
- **"Everyone roots for you for the American dream until it comes true."** Clients cheered when he slept on the gym floor, then resented him once he succeeded. So you have to set ever-higher goals to drive yourself.
- **Two-sided chip:** it does drive you (he cites Jordan inventing slights), but it didn't make him happier.
- **Non-zero-sum markets:** few markets are winner-take-all. Most businesses can't even handle the demand that exists. Different people want problems solved in different ways and are drawn by different psychographics (his audience is 81% male), so different brands attract different customers. "The ocean's very very big."
- **Association effect** (from persuasion books): people forget whom you criticized but remember how you made them feel, and they attach that negativity to you.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Use anger to spur yourself on. Don't use it to "throw shade." Public attacks burn bridges and your reputation.
- People at the top talk to each other. Deals fall through when the reference call goes badly.
- Call competitors to ask how you can help them grow, and share best practices. [inference: he presents this as better for both well-being and profit]

## Playbook steps
1. Notice when you are inventing an enemy. 2. Keep the drive but drop the public hostility. 3. Contact peers or competitors to swap best practices. 4. Position on your own distinct style (high ticket, hard selling, metrics-focused) instead of attacking others.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Malicious public swipes at bigger players.
- Zero-sum thinking about competitors down the street.
- Constantly speaking negatively about others.

## Examples / case details
- Gym Launch's coaching program grew bigger than the rival's (by Alex's belief; he says he doesn't know their numbers), and the rival was "completely unaffected."
- When a reference call comes in about someone who attacked him, he describes them as "volatile," and the listener usually takes his advice.
- A "competitor" doing about the same volume as Acquisition.com with no Facebook ads, only an outbound team. It stretched his view of how big the game is.
- If he and Leila entered weight loss, they wouldn't need to attack Weight Watchers. They'd attract different people.
