# You're Not Behind: My System for Outworking Everyone

## Core thesis
Work = output, not hours or effort. Most owners spend almost no time on the few inputs that grow a business: getting more customers (traffic and conversion) and making them worth more (price, COGS, buy more, buy more times). Spend 4+ hours a day on the current constraint, and find the time by protecting maker time and buying back chores. Someone with 96 productive hours a month beats someone with 3 a week by about 30×. A live talk followed by audience Q&A.

## Frameworks & models
- **Work = volume × leverage**, and speed = (volume × leverage) / time, or output per unit of time. This talk is about volume because "I'm baffled by how little people actually work when they think they are working."
- **Four growth levers**: more traffic (advertise more and better) · higher conversion (practice sales, improve the offer) · higher price ("grow gonads and decide") / lower COGS · buy more times (reach-outs, exit interviews, prepayments, onboarding, faster results, appreciation events). Every hour must ladder up to one of these.
- **4 hours/day on your primary channel**: ads (scripting, analyzing, writing, recording), outreach, content on every platform, or affiliate support. "Excellence comes from repetition"; do so much that "it would be unreasonable for you to suck."
- **Let some fires burn**: prioritize the true constraint; neglect other problems "for a season."
- **Conversion drills when it's the limiter**: track close rate; record sales (post a recording notice where required); watch game tape of winning calls every morning; practice the script out loud daily; get daily feedback; do a 2-minute mental prep before consults; use a pre-sale questionnaire; qualify and pre-sell on the phone.
- **Skill → leverage**: once you have a skill, keeping it up takes less time than building it (like holding a weight).
- **Maker vs manager time** (from Paul Graham, not credited by name in the video): maker = 4–6-hour uninterrupted blocks, about 14 a week, where one interruption kills the block; manager = 5–15-minute slots, 100+ a week, fill with no gaps. Don't mix the two. Alex: 4 maker days plus 1 stacked manager day (Monday). Leila: the reverse.
- **Buy back time**: food (50 h/mo, ~$800), cleaning (25 h, ~$500), laundry (16 h, ~$200): about $1,500/mo. He says that buys back "96 hours" a month. [inference: his three numbers add up to 91 hours; the conclusion barely changes.] Worth it if your time earns more than ~$15/hr. Use the bought time to work, not for Netflix. Commuting: 4.5 h/wk; an Uber is about $700/mo, and he tweets during rides.
- **One battery**: family, chores and employees all drain the same battery. "You will become more productive through elimination than by adding optimization."
- **Elegant-solution frames**: what would it take to get 2×? What if I had to make it more valuable in 1/10th the time? What would someone 10× smarter do? (He dropped a 400-hour book-launch bonus after 1 hour for a better, cheaper idea.)

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Small businesses should put most of their effort into traffic and conversion: no customers means no one to raise prices on.
- If you keep doing the same things and the business isn't growing, those things are wrong.
- Find 4–5 hours with everything off. Weekends are 104 days a year of open time.
- There are no emergencies except 911 ones. The real emergency is a third year of no growth.
- The constraint changes rarely. Keep fixing it until something else breaks; it narrows (e.g., "need more leads" → "need a second channel").
- Make sure you're solving the right problem (a med spa had an LTV problem, not a leads problem). Often it's easier to "fix the sales process and add a zero to your price" than to cut lead costs.
- Drinking costs two days.

## Playbook steps
1. Name the lever that limits you now. 2. Commit 4 h/day of reps to it (channel work or sales drills). 3. Once it's mastered, train someone and move to the next limiter. 4. Pick one maker project; block 4–5 hours (his was 4–9 a.m.); notifications off, dark room, earplugs. 5. Same bedtime every night, weekends included; use a sleep alarm, not a wake-up alarm (in bed at 9, asleep by 10, up around 4–5). 6. Explain maker vs manager time to your team; batch meetings into manager days. 7. Spend about $1,500/mo buying back food, cleaning and laundry; reinvest the time in the four levers.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Defining work as hours or effort ("participation trophies").
- "No one can outwork me" while doing about 3–4 real hours a week.
- Productivity hacks (Pomodoro, TikTok tips) that no one sticks with.
- Being willing to hire at $50K/yr but refusing $1,500/mo of personal help for "your most valuable employee," you.
- Special-snowflake excuses: "customers don't care."
- Solving problems the hard way when there's an elegant solution.

## Examples / case details
- 4,000 sales consults is an example of volume as a measure of skill.
- Alex and Leila: always talk business and don't judge themselves for it; 1–2 three-to-four-day vacations a year with entrepreneur friends; no honeymoon. He says this isn't a prescription.
- Fitness: get in shape between 18 and 24, then maintenance takes about 10% of the time; works out 3–4×/week in a home gym.
