## Frameworks & models
- **Product / market / offer**: the offer is what bridges product and market. It packages and communicates what you give in exchange for money.
- **Signs of a weak offer**: leads are hard to get, most people don't want to buy, and those who do compare your price to competitors (a price-driven rather than value-driven decision).
- **Offer impact multiplies**: response rate × conversion × premium price.
- **Leverage = output per unit of input**: the same work for a more valuable customer yields more. Example: CRO lifting a $1M e-com store 10% = $100K, and he charges $36K (36%). The same work on a $100M store = $10M, and 36% = $3.6M. Same work, 100× the pay.
- **"Value is about who you find, not what you provide"**: the car worth $5K at a dealer, $500 at a scrapyard and $100K to a collector. The Good Samaritan whose mortgage was paid off by the billionaire he helped push.
- **Four market criteria**: (1) pain / "starving crowd" (the hot dog stand outside the stadium), (2) purchasing power, (3) easy to target (associations, FB groups, lists), (4) growing market. Buffett: "not how hard you row but the boat you're in." "When a great manager meets a bad market, the market wins."
- **Price-to-value discrepancy** (Buffett: price is what you pay, value is what you get). People cancel or don't buy when value drops below price. You can widen the gap by lowering price (commodity death spiral) or by raising value, which has no ceiling while price can only go to zero.
- **Common way to set a price (wrong)**: look at competitors → average → go slightly below → offer a bit more for a bit less → everyone ends up a "non-profit."
- **Virtuous cycle of price**: higher price → more emotional investment → higher perceived value → better results → less demanding → more money per customer to reinvest in fulfillment. Business side: more profit, better self-perception, better service and staff, and sales-team conviction. A lower price runs the cycle in reverse.
- **Price is a component of value**: identical wine rated better when labeled expensive. Price so high the buyer thinks "this can't be the same category," so they decide in a vacuum (Dan Kennedy).
- **Value equation**: dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ (time delay × effort/sacrifice). His dad pitch: triple profit, 10 more hrs/week, 4,000 people have done it, 8-week ROI.
- **Value stacking demo**: a $1,000 book with layers added: bestseller, ~8,000 five-star reviews, a testimonial (4× revenue), autograph (6 exist), personal cell, 4 sales-call reviews, traffic-team funnel audit, hiring network, a 2-hour offer session, a feature on his show (~10M impressions), a 3× money-back guarantee over 12 months, and scarcity (only one).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- "Charge as much as you can without cracking a smile" (Dan Kennedy).
- Aim to charge 10× more for something 100× as valuable.
- Don't compete on price unless you are a true low-cost leader from day one (cost-driven culture, automation tech, offshore labor, Walmart-style budgeting). Almost everyone who cuts price didn't start with that strategy.
- Reveal price early. It costs more per call but improves ROAS, because prospects shocked by price wouldn't close anyway. Judge by back-end, not front-end, metrics.
- For services where the client must do the work, charge enough that it hurts so they show up.
- Say no to bad clients to make room for good ones. Find your top segment who pays 5–10× more and get more of them.
- Don't benchmark against the average competitor: "everyone's broke" (from the P&Ls he sees at Acquisition.com).

## Playbook steps
1. Pick a market: pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing.
2. Narrow to underserved, cash-rich subsegments. Prefer your best customer type.
3. Build value with the value equation (outcome, likelihood, speed, ease).
4. Price well above the market so it reads as a different category.
5. Add bonuses, scarcity, urgency, a guarantee and a name.
6. Make sure the sales team believes in it (conviction comes from the founder's genuine intent to help).

## Mistakes he warns against
- Making an "apples-to-apples" me-too offer, which triggers price comparison.
- Selling to people who can't pay (a friend's resume service for unemployed people couldn't sell a $300 consult).
- Rowing hard in a shrinking market (a newspaper business shrinking ~25% a year while gaining market share).
- Hiding price for fear of scaring people off.
- Cutting price to get more customers.
- Salespeople who fold after one objection because they don't believe in the product.
