## Core thesis
Case study with Paul Fontinelli of Boulder Adventure Park, an indoor family entertainment center in Grand Prairie, TX. The business has a $22M build cost ($8M raised, the rest bank debt), $4.8M/yr revenue, is unprofitable, demand-constrained (peak day at 50% of capacity) and weeks from running out of cash. When you're cash-constrained with no time, make ONE bet: a high-urgency, front-loaded offer that pulls cash forward. Here that's a summer-camp giveaway leading into a "buy 4, get 6 free" offer, pushed through the email list, paid ads and phone follow-up. 90-day result: 3,700 leads and summer camps up 325% year over year, but there weren't enough salespeople to close them.

## Frameworks & models
- **Track inputs, not outputs**: the core arbitrage is LTV:CAC. Inputs (conversion rates, leads, attach rates) drive the outputs he tracked.
- **Monthly nut**: break-even ≈ $550K/mo vs $400K now, so a $150K/mo gap (~$600K over the 4-month peak season). Debt service ≈ $180K/mo.
- **One bet under time pressure**: "you don't have the luxury of time." He chose summer camp over his first idea (a season pass) because parents book summer now and the slow season follows.
- **Four traffic flows into one offer**: email list, general-admission attach (in-park upsell), paid ads (creative using clips from a creator, "Unspeakable"), and phone follow-up.
- **Offer**: the existing tiers were $300 × 3 days/wk, $500 × 5 days/wk, or $2,000 for any day all summer, which is "buy 4, get 10" weeks. Angles: buy 4 get 6 free; buy one month, get the whole summer; buy one month, get two free; "as low as $200/week" (2,000 ÷ 10); early bird. Always give a *reason why* you're making the offer.
- **Giveaway launch structure**: 1st prize = full private summer experience, anchored at $5,000 value ($500/wk × 10). 2nd prize (more than one winner) = $3,000 off, which makes it the $2,000 ticket. 7-day countdown timer, then the drawing, then "you won 2nd prize" with 72 hours to claim ("camp is filling up"), then evergreen buy-4-get-6 offers. Thank-you page: refer other parents for extra entries, and if your referral wins, you win too.
- **Anchoring**: advertise the big number so the real price feels small, and leads self-select for the big package.
- **Launch timing rule**: in giveaways and launches, expect a third to half of all leads in the last 48 hours, whatever the campaign length. In the middle, cost per lead rises and it feels like nothing is working; keep spending.
- **Bezos 10% at 100:1 bet**, with the caveat that most entrepreneurs only have enough money to "play two hands."
- **Scaling Roadmap** (free acquisition.com tool): with 150 employees (~10 non-day-workers), he places Paul between the "prioritize" and "productize" phases.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Cash crisis: generate cash first with the "baddest offer" to your existing list; reinvest the cash into ads.
- Landing page: one banner, cut repeated sections, put a VSL right under the headline, "put trust right next to the action" (parent rating near the button), and answer the #1 fear directly (safety: e.g. "zero falls last year") plus a day-one money-back guarantee.
- Say "free" in the ad. Don't say "everyone wins a scholarship," because it kills the excitement. Say "more than one person can win."
- A 10:1 return play (every $1 in returns $10) should be run "a whole lot of times." Rerun it year-round for birthdays at a smaller budget ($2–3K vs $20K) and scale up.
- "As long as you have lead flow, sales people will always make you more than the price for them." Hiring them is growth spend and very low risk.
- Legal: make sure giveaways are allowed in your state. He says he isn't an attorney.

## Playbook steps
1. Find the monthly nut and the gap. 2. Pick one offer that pulls cash forward. 3. Rework the landing page (VSL, trust, fear-killer, guarantee). 4. Run a 7–10 day giveaway with a countdown (ads + email + texts). 5. Draw the winner, then a 72-hour claim window for 2nd prize. 6. Call every lead. 7. Flip to evergreen bonus-week offers. 8. Rerun the play for other occasions (birthdays).

## Mistakes he warns against
- Tracking outputs only and not knowing input metrics.
- Spreading across many moves when cash is short; only a few moves are material.
- Generating more leads than the sales team can work. Paul's tiny team couldn't call 3,700 leads, which Alex had worried about up front.
- Panicking in the middle of a launch when cost per lead rises.
