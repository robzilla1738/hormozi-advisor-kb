## Core thesis
Alex breaks down Mint Mobile's paid ads, landing page, sales page and emails. Every ad has three parts: hook/callout, meat (value), and CTA/payoff. Value = more good stuff (dream outcome, fast, easy, risk-free) plus less bad stuff, and you can "sell the don't" (what keeps happening if they don't buy). Mint wins with a consistent us-vs-them theme, a famous spokesman aimed at a most-aware audience, a tested offer re-wrapped endlessly, and heavy UGC. Non-celebrities can copy the structure; they just need a real callout because they're targeting colder traffic.

## Frameworks & models
- **Ad = hook → meat → CTA** (Leads book p.134 steps: where to advertise, targeting, what the ad says).
- **Value in copy**: more good (dream outcome, speed, ease, likelihood) / less bad (nightmare: slow, hard, risky, costly). **Sell the don't**: the mirror case, "without us you keep the bad and miss the good." That gives four angles × risk/speed/ease, which is endless email and ad fodder.
- **Us vs them**: use it when you're NOT the market leader. The leader should do "us vs the alternative" (e.g. us vs your couch). Keep a winning theme across all ads.
- **Branding by association**: brand placement next to a liked face. You can't brand in the negative ("don't think of pink elephants"). Mint's "chunky milk" ad associated the brand with something disgusting and dropped Ryan Reynolds, and it underperformed.
- **Eugene Schwartz levels of awareness**: unaware → problem aware → solution aware → product aware → most aware. Pick the hook by level. Unaware = curiosity ("Texas homeowners getting checks…"). Most aware = just make the offer. Reynolds's "Hey, it's Ryan Reynolds" works only because his warm audience is everyone. An influencer with 50K followers can do the same to their warm audience; a local "Hey [suburb] locals" works by likeness.
- **Buy X get Y**: an asymmetric structure (buy 1 get 2, buy 3 get 6, buy 2 get 4) usually beats a mirror one (buy 3 get 3) because the gap between paying and getting is bigger. Prepaying 3 months pulls cash forward to fund acquisition.
- **Same bundle, different wrapper** ($100M Offers last chapter, magic headline formula): once an offer wins, re-wrap it with any reason (holiday, Super Bowl, back to school, "my dog's birthday"). "Fraternity party planner." He calls this one of the most underused angles for small businesses.
- **Van Westendorp pricing questions**: too expensive to consider; too cheap to believe; expensive-but-would-buy; great deal. Budget buyers' biggest fear is quality, so address it.
- **Price as everyday analogy**: "$15/mo = two coffees a month," "less than 50¢ a day," "less than a pack of gum a week." Rotate props per audience. Restate the offer in multiple forms ("$45 until the end of winter").
- **Three-tier pricing / soda sizes**: never space tiers evenly. Where you put the middle nudges people. Middle close to large → pushes large. Middle close to small → middle looks like the deal. The aim is to maximize revenue per buyer, based on where most enter. Mint's 6-month plan looks like a decoy between 3 and 12, and the 12-month plan keeps the $15 rate.
- **UGC machine**: build advertising into the business so customers generate ~90% of ads and you make ~10–20%. Mimic trending organic formats (PSA, fake conversation with a big-carrier rep, "why did no one tell me about…"). "The best advertisements are things that don't look like advertisements."
- **Retargeting**: keep the same creative (the audience bucket rotates); top of funnel needs far more creative. Use Facebook Ads Library and sort by longest-running ads: old ads still running are likely the winners (often retargeting).
- **Landing vs sales page**: a short landing page as an intermediate step adds friction that sorts traffic ("when you have too much traffic and not enough conversion, you add a step"). Above the fold: attention/avatar, headline, subhead, three bullets, button (layout 3, Leads ~p.147). Objection tools like the coverage/ZIP checker act as "buying questions." Then a family upsell, FAQ (each question = a few % of buyers), and a nurture opt-in.
- **Scarcity vs urgency**: scarcity limits quantity, urgency limits time. "Ends soon" is subjective urgency (he flags compliance as a question for lawyers).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Adding a 2nd and 3rd CTA button usually raises conversion, until "too many referees on the field."
- Emails: pictures work for physical/low-ticket; plain-text, one link, real-looking email for complex/high-ticket services (better deliverability).
- Follow-up sequence: one objection per email, ordered by prevalence. He rates Mint's emails 5/10 (just restating the offer) and would add personalization, micro-steps (pre-onboarding), local testimonials after the ZIP check, and cohort testimonials ("someone who considered buying last week").
- Birthday plus anniversary offers ≈ 1/6 of your list gets a personal reason for urgency every month.
- Page speed: he cites roughly ~2.5% conversion gain per second saved (recalled from Neil Patel, approximate). Cut 9–13s loads to ~3s.
- Read ad comments for prospects' objections and address them in the next batch.
- Underdog/budget brands: use side-by-side competitor comparisons (Progressive). The top dog shouldn't. State "no hidden fees" explicitly.
- Showing how to cancel before purchase can raise conversion (risk reversal). Same for showing activation speed and the signup steps.
- Test ideas: a harder cold hook with Reynolds; buy 1 get 2 / buy 2 get 4 vs 3+3.

## Playbook steps
1. Callout/hook matched to the awareness level. 2. Meat: more good / less bad / sell the don't across speed, ease and risk. 3. Clear offer restated plus urgency CTA. 4. Generate lots of UGC in native organic formats. 5. Congruent landing page (urgency, avatar, headline/subhead/3 bullets, multiple CTAs), then a sales page with deliberate tier pricing, an objection checker, FAQ and nurture capture. 6. One-objection-per-email follow-up plus birthday/anniversary offers. 7. Re-wrap the winning offer with new reasons.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Opening an ad with the offer instead of a hook (for non-most-aware audiences).
- Branding yourself with a negative image; removing your best asset (the star).
- Incongruence between ad promise and landing page.
- Evenly spaced pricing tiers; using us-vs-them as the market leader.
- Confusing scarcity with urgency.
- Slow pages; poorly visible buttons.

## Examples / case details
- Mint Mobile: $15/mo for 3 months, prepaid $45, unlimited data throttled after 35GB, video at 480p, no phones sold (BYO, keeps prices low). Ads: "deflate our prices due to not hating you," angry goat (actually a sheep), "$500 stock footage," the clone holiday ad.
- Liquid Death ran its 1-star reviews as ads. Spirit Airlines leans into being cheap. Erika Kullberg's conversation format.
- He wears his Acquisition.com hat for the same association effect.
