# How I Put Out So Much Content Despite Being Part-Time

## Core thesis
Q&A clip from a paid seven-figure entrepreneur event. A fitness coach asks how to make content and get leads before he has results. **Speakers:** Leila Hormozi answers first, describing what she has seen Alex do. Alex then adds his own rules. Their shared answer: know exactly who you're talking to, film when an idea is fresh and you feel strongly about it, only talk about things you have actually done, and assume nobody will watch. "Step one, do stuff and then step two, talk about the stuff you did."

## Frameworks & models
- **Two content triggers (Leila, from watching Alex):** (1) Be sure of who you're talking to every time. (2) Catch creativity when it shows up. Leaving a call with an epiphany means filming right away, sometimes several videos. Booking a fixed filming block creates pressure, and "creativity doesn't like constraints."
- **Alex's two reasons to make a video:** to document his own thought process, or because of a fresh conversation with a client, employee or portfolio owner. He makes it for them and keeps it anonymous.
- **Messenger is part of the message:** content takes a long time to work for two reasons. You have to get good through repetition, and people don't care until you've done something (the Elon Musk poop emoji example). If you can't answer "why should I listen to you?", the audience can't either.
- **Domain expertise only:** talk about what you have personally done. Even "7 clients" is expertise nobody can question. Repeating other people's science is weak, because the audience will go to the original expert.
- **Client-story framing:** to handle a belief your audience is stuck on, tell it through a real client ("my client Karen…", "Sarah and Kate"). That gives you domain authority.
- **Non-declarative framing:** say "this is my two cents, how I see the world and what I have experienced," not "this is the way it is." The intent stays pure and it's hard to contradict.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If the source of your content is other people's content, don't make it.
- If you have nothing worth saying, don't say it. Alex would rather someone skip his content than waste their time once and never come back.
- Don't make content on topics you don't want to talk about. Make the videos you believe matter even if they might not hit (Leila's "how to run a great meeting" example).
- Assume zero views, a rule Alex credits to Grant Cardone, and make everything from that perspective.
- Make content for your specific avatar, not generic industry topics (e.g., no carb timing or training splits).

## Playbook steps
1. Define the specific person you're talking to. 2. Do real work and get client results. 3. When a call or idea sparks emotion, film immediately. 4. Frame it through real anonymized client stories. 5. Present it as your experience, not universal fact. 6. Repeat for years. It's a long play, and "the work works on you more than you work on it."

## Mistakes he warns against
- Talking like everyone else in your niche.
- Teaching what you haven't done, like "passive income" gurus who never made $10K a month. All you produce is "a worse diluted version with less authority."
- Making content because it's easy or to serve a hidden agenda.
- Forcing output during scheduled blocks and concluding you suck.
- Declarative statements that are easy to contradict.
