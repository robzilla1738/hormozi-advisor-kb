## Core thesis
He says he has personally closed ~4,000 sales over 13 years. His sales blueprint has six parts: sales multipliers, sales training, before the sale, the sale (CLOSER), closing/objections, and mindset. His claim is that most sales gains come from things *around* the call: speed to lead, 7-day coverage, calendar design, show rates, lead routing, and handoffs. Those beat anything said on the call. On the call, sales is "passing conviction from one person to another... an education process over a bridge of trust." You run it with questions, a script your reps actually follow, and rapport kept through Acknowledge-Associate-Ask. Never deceive, and never discount.

## Frameworks & models
- **Sales multipliers (outside the call)**: sell 7 days a week; respond in under 60 seconds; offer a "call now" option; let calendar slots start every 15 minutes; give reps an off-the-call SOP that pulls appointments forward; "feed the killers"; reward show rate; layer manual reminders on top of automated ones; book same-day/next-day only.
- **Data sources he cites**: a Harvard Business Review study, plus Allan, a software company they own handling 4,000+ appointments/day. At Allan, opening up calendar availability was the strongest correlate of throughput.
- **Training cadence**: daily huddles (team role play on a shared weakness); one-on-ones (one skill per person, picked from recordings); a weekly sales + customer-success game-tape review; record every call across the customer lifecycle.
- **Feedback speed**: how trainable someone is depends on how fast feedback comes. Give one piece of feedback at a time, in real time ("great, keep going", stop and correct), then drill the corrected line 2–6 times in a row. Stay on one skill for 1–2 weeks.
- **Tonality**: emphasis (where you stress or pause; Jason Fladlien's "I didn't say he hit his wife"), tone (rising = question, falling = statement), and pacing (slow = important; speed up toward the close, slow down and lower at the ask). Put a tone guide in the script: underline, italics, bold and caps each cue something.
- **CLOSER** (for transactional 1–2 call closes; he uses a separate "diagnostic script" for complex sales):
  - **Clarify** why they're here, by reminding them of the action they took.
  - **Label** the gap between present and desired, plus the obstacle.
  - **Overview past pain**, the "pain cycle": deprivation creates motivation, and "the pain is the pitch." Quantify time, money, status and the daily cost of inaction.
  - **Sell the vacation** (Maui, not the plane flight).
  - **Explain away concerns.**
  - **Reinforce the decision** after payment.
- **Three-pillar pitch**: chunk the offer up to three pillars, then state each one with a metaphor.
  - Weight loss = fitness + nutrition + accountability (the three-legged stool).
  - Leads for mortgage brokers/realtors = timely, qualified, exclusive.
  - Whatever a prospect tried before is missing one pillar.
- **Metaphors he uses**:
  - Toothbrush, for accountability (credited to Alison Prince): as a kid you needed external accountability, and it became a habit.
  - Amazing Race, for coaching (credited to Tom Miller): the couple who hired a local to sit in the back seat.
  - SEO vs ads: SEO is an investment account; ads are a paycheck.
- **BANT** (attributed to IBM): Budget, Authority, Need, Timing. "Kill zombies early": load the BANT objections before you ask for money.
- **AAA**: Acknowledge (repeat back; buys time and builds rapport) → Associate (neutral praise, a customer like them, or an internal authority such as "our senior engineer," which works as a foil for delivering harsh truths) → Ask the next question. He says this is what separated his top reps from his mediocre ones.
- **Stall question ladder**:
  - Early: "What's your main concern?"
  - Later: "What are you afraid of having happen?"
  - Then: "What variables are you using to make the decision?" → "What makes this a yes?" → "What makes this a no?"
- **All-purpose closes**:
  - "Do you think [objection] is the reason you haven't succeeded / you're on the phone today?"
  - 1–10 close: "What would make it a 10?" If they say 6: "Why not a 1?" so they sell themselves. Then "We feeling tennish?"
  - Zoom out to best case / worst case, which is strongest when you have a guarantee.
- **The core belief behind each objection**:
  - Time = a priority problem.
  - Decision maker = you want support, not permission.
  - Details = a preference ("you change the variables, you change the outcome").
  - Money = value ("nothing is too expensive, it's just not worth it"; resourcefulness, not resources; the Ferrari-for-$5K example).
  - Stall = avoiding the decision, either from fear of choosing wrong or not knowing how to decide.
  - Keep 2–3 closes per category that you're comfortable saying.
- **Details are death traps**: never give a "blind answer." Ask a question about their question, or chunk up to the reason behind the detail. The secretary-at-the-heart-surgeon and mechanic closes ("can't ethically answer until we look under the hood"). Decision tree: find out the right answer from them → if you have it, "that's what we have"; if not, ask why → chunk up → "would you care how we got there?"
- **Four-step decision-maker sequence**:
  1. "What would happen if they said no?" He says about 1 in 3 answer "I'd do it anyway."
  2. "What would their main concern be?" Their partner works as a foil in reverse.
  3. Past agreements: do they already disapprove of your current situation?
  4. Support, not permission.
- **Handshake, not handoff / BAMFAM** (book a meeting from a meeting): the closer personally introduces customer success and builds them up. CS then runs a personalized proof-promise-plan using the setter and closer notes.
- **Sales and marketing are one continuum**: put them under one revenue leader (a CRO). Think of sales as personalized marketing that fills whichever pieces of buying information marketing didn't cover.
- **Trust and belief are continuums.** "Bigger plane, longer runway": higher prices need more education before the purchase.
- **Hire for the smallest skill deficiency.** That is what "hire for attitude" really means at junior levels. It flips toward hiring for skill in rare-expertise roles.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Selling on weekends adds 104 days a year, a minimum +29% over 5 days. Weekend availability also raises show rates.
- Contacting a lead within 60 seconds gives a 391% higher likelihood of closing. After more than 5 minutes, the odds of closing drop by 80%. 50% of buyers go with whoever responds first.
- Add a sales rep whenever the revenue lift is much bigger than the cost of onboarding one.
- Slots should start every 15 minutes. Faking a busy calendar "doesn't do anything."
- Manual reminders at 24 hours before (showing personal prep), the morning of, and 1 hour before. Prefer iMessage, voice memos or video. Automated messages should look automated.
- Same-day/next-day availability only. If that doesn't fit, hire more reps.
- Best leads go to the best closers and worst leads to the worst. Reward the highest show rate weekly (e.g. a flat $500).
- Script adherence is drilled first: "I would rather have 100% of my team saying the script word for word and us close no one." Script the first ~2/3 of the call up to the ask, roughly 12 questions.
- The three-pillar pitch should run no more than 2 minutes. Spend about 90% of the call on the prospect's pains.
- Train closers back to front: collecting payment first, then asking for the sale, then everything earlier in the call.
- Once they say yes, stop talking and take payment.
- Never change price to close a deal ("we don't negotiate with terrorists"). Change terms (payment plan) or features (downsell) instead. Test prices only in planned tests.
- The prospect should have about 2/3 of the talk time.
- Getting 3–4-call closes means too little education before the first call.
- Check in on "no for now" leads every 3–6 months.
- Stop selling if the prospect would genuinely be better off not buying.
- Compensation attracts talent but rarely changes behavior. The exception: paying on cash collected tends to get you more cash collected.
- Enterprise and government sales on 6–12-month cycles mostly don't fit this playbook. There, build many contacts between both teams.

## Playbook steps
1. Open weekends and evenings, respond in under 1 minute, and add a "call now" option after qualification.
2. Set up 15-minute slot starts with same-day/next-day booking, and an off-the-call SOP: call booked leads, qualify them, and offer them an earlier slot that day.
3. Route leads by score, rewarding show rate.
4. Setter: frame the business ("did you hear about us on the best-of list?"), build up the closer, qualify with BANT, and log notes in the CRM.
5. Closer: spend 5 minutes prepping and read the notes (never make the customer repeat themselves). Open with proof-promise-plan in 15–20 seconds and set the agenda.
6. Run CLOSER: clarify → label → pain cycle → three-pillar pitch with metaphors → ask ("Ready to [goal]?") → AAA plus the stall ladder or category closes, stacking closes when they teeter.
7. In person, ask for their ID first, then "I'll trade you" for a card. On Zoom, ask them to hold their ID up to the camera.
8. Hand them off with a handshake to CS, and book the next meeting from this one.
9. Weekly: joint sales + CS game-tape review, daily role-play huddles, and one-skill one-on-ones.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Selling only 9–5 on weekdays; slow follow-up; blaming "lead quality" when the real problem is how leads are worked.
- Round-robin lead distribution.
- Giving several pieces of feedback at once, or delayed feedback; managers too proud to role-play in front of the team.
- Promoting your best closer to sales manager. He says he's made this mistake several times: Phil Jackson wasn't better than Jordan.
- Cutthroat internal competition. Pick an outside enemy ("us vs them") instead.
- Superstitious rituals bloating the sales process.
- Training reps on the product instead of the prospect.
- Selling past a yes; blind answers to detail questions; disagreeing with prospects ("statements are bombs").
- Mistaking a remark like "wow, that's expensive" for a real objection ("wasting bullets on zombies that aren't there").
- Discounting to close.
- Screening sales hires by résumé. Use group interviews of 1–2 minutes per person instead.
- Re-tweaking compensation to change behavior.
- Splitting sales and marketing into rival departments.
- "Commission breath" (putting the close before the person); being nice instead of kind.
- Quitting during the "suck" phase. The returns come late in mastery.

## Examples / case details
- His gym: he closed from 4–8pm. On his heaviest days he ran 48–50 one-on-one consults, using a mini trampoline between sessions to keep his mood up. On weigh-out day his team's close rates were close to 100%.
- His wife Leila booked with the first salon that could take her right away.
- The top rep at a timeshare company (best rep 5 of the last 6 years, out of 3,000) convinced the CEO to route leads by score. His income went from $250K to $3M, and the company 5x'd once it rolled out nationally.
- Allan: the joint sales + CS game-tape reviews, which he learned from the software world, cut close-then-back-out losses of 5–10%.
- Reading testimonials to the team during a slump, crediting the closer who "arm-wrestled" that customer (Sandy).
- Leads for mortgage brokers: Zillow leads shared with 20 other agents → pitch exclusivity.
- Re-engaging ghosted leads with the Kevin Hart "where you at" meme, which he says gets the highest response rate of any message they've sent: send it to 200 leads and 5–10 re-engage.
- Outbound voicemail/email: "Hey [business], I'm calling about [competitor]. Give me a call back."
- Calling a local lead: "John?" (tone up) … "It's Alex" (pause) … "you opted in on Facebook for our promotion… make sure you weren't a crazy person… I've got 2 and 4 today, which works better?"
- At 23 he pretended there were multiple employees at his gym. He now calls that deceptive and wrong.
- A friend who sells high-ticket weight loss brings the spouse onto the call and often closes both.
- Cheap-competitor close: "If ours was the same as theirs, which would you do?" → "Why?" → "Right, that's why we're more."
- "Can you do it for less?" → "We could do it for more." He says it re-anchors higher and has never failed him.
- Anti-guarantee: "If you need a guarantee, this isn't for you… I can't eat the food for you."
- Lead magnet / "I'll do it myself": "You should totally do that… either you succeed and hit the next problem I can solve, or you don't and I can help."
- Price-objection math: a 30% conversion lift at 90% gross / 30% net margins roughly doubles profit.
- A private equity CEO's advice, "Don't be cute": "two fat guys in the middle, run to the right."
- He has twice found sales-team growth stalled by résumé screening.
- Post a Slack kudos only when a sale is actually processed in the CRM (via Zapier), not when a rep claims it.
