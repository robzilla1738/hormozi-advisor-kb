## Core thesis
There are four business "shapes": e-commerce, service, education/info, and software. Each has a growth curve and one "big hairy problem" that comes with the model. That problem is a feature, not a bug, so stop thinking something is wrong with your business. Name the core problem, work out how much enterprise value solving it unlocks, and put lots of resources into solving it properly instead of duct-taping it. That's where you beat competitors who don't understand their own model.

## Frameworks & models
- **E-commerce shape**: you front cash for inventory, then grow fast in steps. It plateaus at cash, traffic/ads, distribution (e.g. 7-Eleven → CVS) or supply-chain limits (manufacturer makes 1K units/day, you need 10K). Growth resumes when supply chain or distribution unlocks. Distribution can be retail, ads, or affiliates/influencers ("synthetic distribution").
  - Pros: scales fast, low founder key-man risk, very sellable, and tangible goods are easier to sell.
  - Cons: capital intensive (profit goes back into inventory; founders at hundreds of millions in revenue often live on salary), depends on partners, and is easy to copy.
  - Big problems: cash-flow forecasting against lead times (e.g. 12 weeks), distribution, supply chain, and brand.
- **Smash-and-grab vs brand**: media arbitrage (buy eyeballs at $10, make $11 gross profit) gets copied. Moats are patents, own manufacturing or secret sauce, and above all brand (Nike). Brand gives higher CTR, more repurchases and premium prices, which means lower CAC, higher LTV and higher gross profit. Brand also decides which affiliates and discounts you refuse.
- **Perfect e-commerce company**: own manufacturing (decentralized per market at scale), trade secrets or unique ingredients, a strong 3PL, a lean performance-marketing team working with a brand team, a **70/30 spend split** (70% top-of-funnel/brand, 30% direct-to-purchase; most startups do 100% direct), then retail partnerships or flagship stores (Gymshark's online-first approach is the reverse of Lululemon's). At saturation a store's CAC = rent ÷ monthly sales. You also need "savage" email marketing plus attribution, and an exceptional product.
- **Service shape** (he says 78% of US businesses): slow, steady, close to a straight line. People sit in attraction, conversion and delivery. It's easier to find more product than exceptional people.
  - Pros: least risky (you can always cut headcount down to just you), stable, high cash flow (sell $100K of work at $50K cost and keep $50K every month), good retention because clients don't want to do the work themselves.
  - Cons: talent is the bottleneck (maids → trades → white-collar with multi-year ramps), the founder is usually the best technician, and it's the slowest to scale.
  - Coaching (fitness) is easy to hire for but hard to sell and retain. Done-for-you work is easy to sell but always supply-constrained.
- **"A service business is a teaching business"**: profit = value of outputs − value of inputs. Bring in under-skilled people, add skills, and sell those skills at a premium.
- **Price as a service north-star metric**: good work → demand exceeds supply → raise prices → higher margins → better ICPs → repeat.
- **Education/info shape**: the fastest straight-line rise, then a hard stop. Good education means people graduate, so retention is low, and you create your own competitors. It's fast, easy and cheap to start (so others start too), and hard to scale past $1–3M because most founders lack promotional skill. Delivery bleeds into service.
- **Milk analogy**: with one glass of great milk and 10 customers, give each a shot of pure milk rather than a watered-down glass. Don't sell weekend-trained coaches at your own price.
- **"Big head, long tail"**: charge a high one-time price for the inherently valuable one-time part and a lower recurring price for consumable parts.
- **Stickiness levers in education**: consumable recurring items (ad creative, community, a weekly list of trending 3D-print items), group-buying discounts worth more than the subscription, and continuing education (e.g. $250K to become a doctor vs $3K/yr in continuing credits).
- **Overkill bias**: people want the jacket that stays warm on Everest, and they want Tony Hawk to teach them an ollie. Real-world proof is the best education brand (Warren Buffett's course would be worthless without Berkshire).
- **Software shape**: slowest start (capital, engineers, years to product-market fit; he hasn't yet seen a fully vibe-coded $100M business), then the most scalable of the four once PMF hits (YC's boulder image).
  - Pros: effectively infinite scale, high gross margins, sticky revenue when done well, huge enterprise value (low risk at sale).
  - Valuation drivers: gross margin, growth rate, and revenue retention at scale. Without retention it "looks identical to" an info business.
  - Cons: slow start, founders quit early, high burn, low willingness to pay (Netflix going $10 → $13 causes churn), VC dilution (ending up owning 4%).
- **Facebook growth line**: real growth means a retained base that never churns while you keep filling the front.
- **Logo vs revenue retention**: 10 customers × $10/mo = $100. If the original cohort still brings in at least $100 a year later (thanks to upsells), revenue retention is ≥100%.
- **Viral coefficient**: each customer brings in about 1.1 more, which divides CAC down (ChatGPT reached 1M users in a week). Build return, referral and upgrade loops into the product.
- **Good product = remove friction** between people and an outcome everyone wants (iPhone).
- **Founder-personality fit**: software suits a creative engineer who disregards others' opinions and can suffer for a long time. E-commerce and education suit strong promoters who dislike details. Service suits a skilled good teacher who loves people and culture. "Culture is the internal brand."
- **Enterprise-value lens on the hairy problem**: e.g. if getting 2 new hires up to speed per month adds $10–50M of enterprise value, spending $100–500K to solve it is obvious.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- E-commerce: you're constrained by free cash flow (10 units of profit can't meet 300 units of demand), so line up lending partners and redundancy at every layer (raw materials, manufacturing, 3PL, capital).
- E-commerce product strength: 60–70% of a new cohort buying again after the first purchase suggests a strong product.
- Brand-to-direct spend of about 70/30 for e-commerce brands that last.
- Service: design for higher gross margins than the other three models, as a cushion for ramp-up dips (a new sales rep may halve conversion during a ~30-day ramp).
- Narrow the avatar and pick 1–3 deliverables at around $1–3M revenue. That's when you decide what business you're really in.
- Reinvest service cash in talent (recruiting better raw material) and brand (premium prices plus premium talent).
- Only be the low-cost service provider if you decide on day one (offshore, automate everything, "we're the cheapest"), not because people balked at the price. He recommends premium: "I'd rather be making a million dollars per person."
- Education payment plans: don't spread a $25K course into $2K/mo forever. After the value is delivered they're paying community prices for something worth less.
- Consumer software: keep >60% of logos by year-end and ideally >100% revenue retention. Enterprise retains more easily because switching costs are higher.
- Software: hire 10–100× engineers and pay 10–100×. If you can't judge talent, get educated or get a strong technical co-founder. Build scalable code and documentation from day one.
- Don't build every feature customers ask for ("customers will hang themselves with their own requests"). Weigh each feature against the complexity it adds.

## Playbook steps
1. Identify which of the four shapes your business is (or blend, by revenue line).
2. Name its big hairy problem: e-commerce = cash/supply chain/distribution/brand; service = recruit/onboard/train talent; education = stickiness; software = survive to PMF, then retention.
3. Estimate the enterprise value unlocked by solving it, then fund it heavily and ignore the rest.
4. Service sequence: narrow the avatar → 1–3 standardized deliverables → systematize delivery → build training for each step → hiring/onboarding/talent selection → career paths → replace yourself in ops → reach the point where demand is the constraint → raise prices.
5. Education sequence: split one-time vs recurring pricing → add consumables to the recurring part → use one-time cash to fund acquisition → build brand through real-world proof (get very good first).
6. Software sequence: obsess over customer feedback and quality (watch users, like the Dropbox Craigslist tests) → fix each user-flow loop → retain → build viral, referral and upgrade loops → scale.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Treating the model's core problem as a sign your business is broken (he says he sees it even at $30–100M).
- E-commerce that's 100% direct-response with no moat, then complaining about dupes.
- Training someone in a weekend for something that took you 10 years, then being upset they're not good.
- Doing any work for anyone with a pulse past the early stage.
- Selling diluted coaches at your own price, which hurts reputation and word of mouth.
- "Offer hoppers" who launch a new education product every year.
- Marketers going into software and rushing to sell before the product sticks; cobbling together an outsourced back end.
- Feature-bloat "Frankenstein" products.
- Diluting yourself to a sliver of equity.
