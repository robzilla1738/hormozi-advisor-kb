# How to Speak So Well People Give You Money

## Core thesis
You're not making as much money as you want because you're saying the wrong words (scripting) or saying them the wrong way (tone). Tone comes down to five things: three constants (volume, speed, articulation) and two variables (pauses, pitch going up or down). Mark them in the script with three punctuation marks. Then reps get consistent results and managers have concrete feedback to give.

## Frameworks & models
- **CLOSER script** (brief recap): Clarify why they're there, Label the problem, Overview past experience, Sell the vacation (3-step pitch), Explain away concerns, Reinforce the decision.
- **Sales tone = 3 constants**: (1) loud enough to hear (matters more in person), (2) slow enough to understand: ~135–185 words per minute, (3) articulate every word, no mumbling. All three are about comprehension.
- **2 variables**: (1) **pauses**: a short pause draws attention, a long pause asks for a response; (2) **pitch**: rising = question, asks for a response; falling = statement.
- **Script notation**: "..." = short pause, "." = full stop, "?" = rising voice / ask for a response. That's all you need.
- **The ask** = long pause + rising voice: "you ask them to buy and you shut up."
- **Word blackout memorization**: read the whole script aloud, cross out one word, read it all again, repeat until the page is blacked out and you're "breathing the script." Natural tone comes from knowing the script, not reading it.
- **Overcomes are excuses to ask again**: the reps who close the most ask the most times. The skill is asking as often as possible without annoying the prospect.
- **Universal close example (one of "seven universal closes")**: "The reason you gave is the very reason you should do it" (no time/money → that's why you need this. In B2B: the problem stopping you from buying is the problem we solve).
- **Discovery "pulling teeth"**: with one-word answers, alternate "Can you give me an example?" / "Can you be more specific?" until you get something concrete. Then **chunk up** to a problem you sell ("sounds like a marketing issue, fair?"). Get 2–3 chunked problems.
- **Transition / pre-close**: recap the problems → "If we solved these, would it help grow the business?" → yes = they've accepted the sale → match each problem to your solution/expert with a yes question → invite → shut up.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Speak between 135 and 185 wpm. Too fast lowers trust; too slow sounds dumb. He knows his own pace (185 wpm) and used it to time when he dropped the link in the book launch pitch.
- The script is gold: "no one is above the script." If reps say different things you can't coach the basics; he says this is 95% of sales teams.
- Wait up to ~8 seconds after the ask. He cites meta-analyses ("Columbia is one," he doesn't recall exactly) showing ~30% more closes.
- Only ~20–30% of sales are picked up after the first "no." Most selling happens before the ask, and more still before the conversation starts.
- Most sales are lost in discovery, not the pitch or close.
- Handle true logistical objections (e.g., needs more seats) on their own terms; for decision maker / time / money, reframe and ask again.

## Playbook steps
1. Get a script (CLOSER). 2. Mark it up with "...", ".", "?" 3. Memorize it with word blackout. 4. Keep volume, speed, and articulation constant. 5. In discovery, pull teeth (example/specific) and chunk up to 2–3 problems you solve. 6. Recap → get a "would solving this help?" yes → match the solution to each problem. 7. Ask with a long pause and rising voice; stay silent up to ~8s. 8. On a non-logistical no: reframe (e.g., "that's why you need it") and ask again. 9. Managers coach with concrete terms ("speak louder," "slow down") instead of "more confidence."

## Mistakes he warns against
- Vague tone advice ("curiosity tone," "ambitious tone," "have more confidence," "stop sounding nervous") that no one defines.
- Reading the script, which sounds robotic and has less natural variation.
- Talking the most: "the person who speaks the most in the sale loses"; sales is "in your ears, not from your mouth"; people believe what they say, not what you say.
- Filling silence after the ask because you're nervous.
- Trying to win arguments in objection handling: "you're never going to sell someone by being right."
- Talking too fast/loud in the close, which sounds aggressive. (Enunciating fully slows nervous talkers down automatically.)
- Over-obsessing over objection handling compared with discovery.

## Examples / case details
- Book launch: $106M in 72 hours; he says it set a Guinness record for fastest-selling nonfiction. His longest pause in the 2+ hour pitch came at the ask.
- "I didn't say he hit his wife": stress on different words gives four different meanings.
- Discovery role-play: "business isn't growing" → specifics → "same leads as last year" → marketing issue; profit per sale → pricing/packaging; matched to a marketing director and a pricing expert (home services, 6 yrs, 2,000+ businesses; illustrative).
