# Make More Profit than 99% of People

## Core thesis
He calls pricing the strongest profit lever. In the intro he says it is 6× stronger than getting more customers and 2× stronger than cutting churn or getting people to buy more often; later he says roughly 3× the other levers. Pricing questions are math problems, not "Alex, what should I charge?" questions. Price to match ongoing value, separate one-time value from consumable value, bill on longer cadences to stretch the "look-back window," and pick products people already don't cancel. Recurring customers that stack year over year are what make a business compound. A live Q&A from the Skool call / Instagram live follows the lesson.

## Frameworks & models
- **Price-to-value discrepancy**: three lines, value > price > cost. Price minus cost is your profit. Value minus price is customer surplus (goodwill). People cancel when perceived value drops to or below the price.
- **One-time vs consumable value**: a course has one-time value; once the skill is learned, its ongoing value is about zero ("arithmetic the day after you learn it"). Community, accountability/support, hosting, software and ads help are consumed every month. Price them separately.
- **Big head, long tail**: a high one-time start-up fee for the one-time value (e.g. $5K, which offsets CAC) plus a low recurring fee for the consumable value (e.g. $200–300 a month). Keeping the price-to-value gap wide means longer LTV. He links this to the churn problem in info and education.
- **Billing cadence vs churn** (a ProfitWell study, as he cites it): monthly ~10.7% churn per month, quarterly ~5%, annual ~2% (converted to a monthly rate). **Napkin LTV = price ÷ churn**: at $100/mo that is about $935 (he says ~$950), $2,000 and $5,000.
- **Look-back window**: at each bill, customers judge the value they got since the last bill ("what have you done for me lately"). Longer cycles average out volatile months (10×, 1×, 3×, 15×), so more people renew. Annual buyers can also afford to pay up front.
- **Revenue per conversation**: price × close rate. 10% × $10K = $1,000 per call; 30% × $5K = $1,500 per call. Maximize absolute gross profit, not the price.
- **Compounding stack**: if about 80 of 100 annual customers renew, last year's sales stack on top of this year's, and so on.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Keep raising prices until price × conversion makes less money, then step back. Refund the difference or give a bonus to those who paid more.
- Test prices from low to high. Raising is easy to justify ("more demand, we're better now"); cutting means downgrading existing customers unless you have a real efficiency reason and pass the savings on.
- He'd take $5K a year paid up front over a $12K-a-year ($1K/mo) deal at the same close rate. People on the $1K/mo plan cancel around months 4–5, so they're worth about $4–5K. At $5K a year with 2% monthly churn, LTV is about $416/mo × 50 ≈ $20.8K.
- Price for what they'll keep paying on their worst month, not for the most they'll say yes to today. He no longer preaches charging as much as possible.
- "Get in the right boat": sell things people structurally don't cancel (alarm systems, insurance, payment processing, banking, cable, cell service), not things they do cancel (gym memberships).
- Add a CPI/inflation clause to continuity contracts and use it every 2–3 years rather than often.
- Typical setup: monthly plus annual at about 16% off ("buy 10, get 2 free"). That is still worth it, since annual LTV can be about 5× (he says the net is about 4.5×; [inference] 5 × 0.84 ≈ 4.2×). Quarterly-only billing can be a middle ground.
- Sweeten annual plans with VIP status perks (no waiting in line, the session times they want, VIP-only sessions), even at the same price.
- Annual billing front-loads cash. Even if half as many choose it, you collect about 5× the up-front cash, which lets you spend more aggressively on acquisition.
- Contracts are only as good as the signature. They matter for enterprise customers. For US consumers they mostly cut sales without cutting cancellations (card companies side with consumers on chargebacks), so most big consumer brands skip them.
- Report value cumulatively (Instacart-style "hours saved") to stretch the perceived look-back window. Be realistic: it lifts renewals by maybe a few points.

## Playbook steps
1. Split your offer into one-time and consumable value. 2. Price as big head, long tail: a start-up fee plus a small recurring fee. 3. Move to quarterly or annual billing and attach VIP perks to annual. 4. Test prices from low to high on revenue per call. 5. Show cumulative value in customer success reporting. 6. Long term, move into categories people don't churn from.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Charging monthly for a course once its one-time value is used up ("$1K/mo for 12 months" → people cancel at month 4).
- Growing only by adding new customers each month: if an ad account or channel dies, the business dies.
- Guessing at prices instead of doing the math.
- Leaning on consumer contracts.
