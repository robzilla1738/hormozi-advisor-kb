## Core thesis
The stores that win Black Friday give a lot before they ask: brand building, free value, community, Easter eggs. Then, on the day buyers have their wallets out, they run a site built only for that day: a timely banner with urgency, a narrowed set of best sellers, an easy first-time offer and minimal friction. Smaller companies win by using what incumbents can't: speed, things that don't scale, gamified data collection, partnering with bigger players, owning a niche, and growing the pie instead of fighting for share.

## Frameworks & models
- **Give before ask**: "How much give has happened prior to this ask is almost directly proportional to the amount of sales" (Alex). Gymshark gives through athletes and causes, then asks hard on Black Friday. ButcherBox acquired Truffle Shuffle (live cooking classes) and ran free, high-production classes with no call to action.
- **Day-specific conversion site**: (1) a top banner with a code and same-day urgency ("Bark Friday", ends at midnight); the top banner is the most valuable spot, especially on mobile, where more than half of traffic is. (2) Navigation cut to 3–4 sale categories. (3) Top sellers shown first ("lowest prices on top sellers," not the leftovers). (4) A first-time-buyer keystone offer or "start here." (5) A push toward subscription.
- **Focus beats choice**: the jam study (24 jars vs 6). More people tried from the 24, but the 6-jar display got about 2–3× the buyers.
- **Code revealed at checkout**: Alex's untested hunch is that it protects perceived retail value, and the savings feel bigger close to purchase. He says he has no split test.
- **Gamified data collection** (BarkBox quizzes, challenges for discounts): engagement plus customer data (pet name, age, breed) for personalized remarketing. "Give information in return for a discount." Alex's parallel: the Acquisition.com scaling roadmap gated behind business info. Mischief (the PT Cruiser giveaway) is the model for challenge-based commerce.
- **Hyper-buying cycles** (Alex): when someone decides to start something (a Turkey Trot), they want shoes, shorts and an armband all at once. Sell everything inside that window and don't break the moment. QVC's shoppable video on demand lets viewers buy without leaving the video.
- **David partners with Goliath** (Harley): BK Beauty, a top TikTok Shop seller, partnered with Maybelline and L'Oréal to run their TikTok ads instead of competing with them. It's channel agnostic (TikTok and The View) and judged on ROAS and CAC vs LTV.
- **Ride the platform's macro mission**: native integrations outperform. Alex's native X buy box for a $0 product feeding his email list beats his other platforms per view. Early TikTok Shop and early Spotify podcasts benefited the same way.
- **Do things that don't scale**: the Xbloom CEO sent Harley a handwritten, researched note. It took about 5–8 minutes, and Harley has since told the story about 10 times. Small companies make up for less capital with creativity, while incumbents are stuck on the old playbook (the Nike board fearing Gymshark).
- **Advent / promo calendar** (Burger King: $19.54 for its 1954 founding, 12 items, ~$100 of value, sold out; also Fly By Jing, BrewDog): it's a "multi-dip." It gets PR and earned media, sales, rewards hardcore fans and collects data. Fly By Jing used it to test candidate sauces for next year, selling out through email, social and SMS only.
- **Grow the pie / your competition is the couch**: at Shopify's 2015 IPO, investors kept saying its TAM was too small (93 roadshow meetings). Instead of a gym taking share from other gyms, target people who've never been to a gym. Sell in a vacuum and be their "first love." Netflix's competition is sleep; can you make sleepwear cooler than a big t-shirt?
- **Mayor of a tiny dot**: own a niche you're obsessed with (James Perse black t-shirts, Lola Blankets, Little Sleepies, Firebelly Tea's high-end niche). The niche expands outward. Default global: cross-border orders are nearing 20%, and Canada is 40M people with similar household income.
- **Marketing and sales are one continuum = education** (Alex): education means a new behavior under the same conditions. Example: an accountant posting 60-second tax-code-change videos beats the big firms, which are slowed by compliance and committees.
- **Golden BBs / lottery tickets** (Alex): failure has never been cheaper (about a software subscription), so you get near-unlimited shots on goal. The barbarians, not a rival empire, toppled Rome. The real competition is distraction.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Treat a 3-day peak like an extra month of the year and put the same resources into it.
- There's a time for branding and a time for asking. "When the going is good, go hard."
- When you know your LTV, make a "crazy" first-time offer during high-traffic windows (BarkBox's first box for $7).
- Copy the category choices of big players who have done the research ("use someone else's team").
- Brand spend needs patience. Harley says New Balance moved its budget from 70% bottom-of-funnel / 30% storytelling to the reverse. It paid nothing for 18 months, then took off in month 19 (Alex's account of the CMO's story).
- Alex's example of delayed ROAS: at $1M/month in ad spend, a $50K car giveaway probably beats spending 5% more on ads [his illustration].
- In the current climate, no "growth at all costs" at any scale; show profitability (Harley).
- Seasonal "wrapping paper" (Alex, from the offers book): any reason works for a promotion (a holiday, an anniversary, a birthday). "Become a fraternity party planner."

## Playbook steps (Alex's recap)
1. Build presence beyond your native channel (Gymshark's 12-week popup started before competitors and ended after them; Harley's hypothesis is it pretested winners from Oct 1). 2. Hide Easter eggs so fans study and make content about your marketing. 3. Narrow to the products that drive the most revenue. 4. Run quizzes and challenges for data and personalized discounts. 5. Give first-time buyers an obvious keystone offer and "start here." 6. Put urgency in the banner and theme the site for the day. 7. Remove friction and meet buyers where they are. 8. Collaborate with big players instead of fighting them. 9. Run an advent/test calendar for PR, sales and data. 10. Give value with no CTA until it's time to ask.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Making buyers leave the video or content to check out ("too much friction").
- Giving buyers too many choices on peak days.
- "This is the way it's done" thinking in traditional businesses. If QVC can change after decades, anyone can.
- Sizing your TAM as a share of the existing pie.
- Thinking small companies should fear incumbents; it's actually the reverse.
- Showing only the highlight reel. Harley mentions his failed slipper and poker-chip companies, and Shopify buying a shipping business for over $1B, then exiting it as a "side quest."
- Confusing failing with being a failure. Not taking shots is worse.

## Examples / case details
- Gymshark: founder Ben Francis was a pizza delivery guy who started it in 2012. NYC holiday popup; "there will be signs" campaign.
- Alex's content system: ~450 short pieces a week, with 1–2 long-form pieces built from the top performers ("shots on goal").
- Burger King: a Whopper deal if you were near a McDonald's (~500 ft per captions) drove app adoption. "Forget the clown" tagline.
- Levi's "how to style these jeans" video: one pair shown on 4–5 different personas cross-sells and widens the audience.
- David's Tea (~$800M market cap at IPO, ~350 stores) → Harley and David Segal co-founded Firebelly Tea for a high-end niche.
- Top trending products during the stream: a Vuori beanie, Little Sleepies pajamas, a Lola Blanket, a Victoria Beckham lip liner, a Rhode peptide lip treatment, Olaplex hair serum. Shopify has 13K apps in its store, which left room for Klaviyo and others.
- Shopify started as a snowboard shop in Ottawa and went from ~$1B at IPO to ~$150B in 10 years (per captions).
