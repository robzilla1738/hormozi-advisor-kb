## Core thesis
Great salespeople are selected, not made. "You win the championship in the draft" (S. Truett Cathy, Chick-fil-A). Spend training time on high-base talent, recruit proven closers from similar businesses, and judge fast: if they don't close in their first full sales cycle, they're not a killer.

## Frameworks & models
- **Selection > training** (Harvard selects the smartest. McKinsey/Bain/BCG skim a huge pool). Training "reminds them how good they are", it doesn't make them good.
- **Training ROI:** 10 hours takes someone from 2→4.5 or from 6→9. Start with the higher base.
- **Recruiting by pay level:** can't pay well → interview many more people, run ads, use your network (and maybe fix your price points). Can pay well → the best are already employed, so poach from similar companies (same sale type, cycle length, product category) for shorter ramp and faster judgment.
- **Cut the bottom, the top moves up.** It also sets the bar for new hires, and a tight team rejects misfits or absorbs fits.
- **Hire on the "tingle"** ("I really have to have this person"), not "I guess they could do the job."

## Decision rules
- No close within one full sales cycle (transactional, 2-week, or 1-month, depending on the business) → not exceptional. Every killer he's had closed in the first cycle.
- The longer he's in business, the shorter his tolerance for non-exceptional performance.
- You can spend a year training *yourself* in sales, not an employee.
- Counter-model: 24 Hour Fitness hires anyone with a pulse and ~10% survive the first 90 days. Brutal filtering in place of selection.

- First non-Alex sale: driving home, a text said they closed 2 sales out of 4 walk-ins. He pulled over, teary. The business could work without him.
- Mauro Negretti (name as captioned): told Alex to finish his session, closed the first walk-in paid-in-full without knowing the pitch, pure rapport.
