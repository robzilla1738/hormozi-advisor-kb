# You Made Your Customers ANGRY... Now What?
id: p39nLmVtOjc / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p39nLmVtOjc / duration: 1502s (~25 min) / tags: 06-retention-churn, 07-hiring-management, 08-ops-scaling
Signal: high

## Core thesis
Mistakes are inevitable. How you respond decides whether you create an enemy or a lifelong champion. "A refund is an apology," and an apology isn't enough. Get them back to neutral (refund plus the costs they incurred) and then above neutral with magic moments. Own it immediately, be angrier about it than the customer is (the "angry boat"), and give frontline staff a pre-set box of make-good powers.

## Frameworks & models
- **Disney: 37 magic moments to make up for one tragic moment** (as Alex cites it). Avoid tragic moments. When one happens, you have to do a lot.
- **Never become someone's project**: a wronged customer who stews will try to destroy you.
- **Refund = apology** (his manslaughter analogy: society doesn't accept "sorry" alone). Customers also lost time and expectations, so expect to go net-negative to make it right.
- **One-star review = the customer acting as the judicial system** when they feel invalidated.
- **Angry boat**: (1) confront it head-on ("this was our mess up and I'm super pissed"); (2) get in fast, quicker to anger than they are; (3) validate, validate, validate. Only they can minimize their pain. If you're more upset than they are, they're the one who says "it's not that big a deal."
- **Always your fault**: if it's your fault, it's under your control and you can fix it. The buck stops with the owner. Having the power to fix it makes it your responsibility.
- **Employee version**: the boss is "the customer" in an internal mistake. Don't minimize it. Nobody credits you for beating yourself up in silence, only for (1) making it right and (2) making sure it never happens again. Skip the guilt and shame.
- **Box of decision-making power** (from hotels): give high-volume frontline staff 2–3 make-goods tiered by severity (drink credits → free meal → upgrade/refund). Bake them into margins.
- **Malpractice insight**: people sue doctors they dislike, not the ones who make the most mistakes.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Never say "you should have checked the email," and never invalidate the customer.
- Make-good = refund + incurred costs + something above and beyond. "ROI is out the window."
- If staff use more than ~5 make-goods a month, fix the process.
- If a large share of customers ask for refunds, fix the product or the selling (over-set expectations or poor delivery), or stop selling it. Don't block refunds.

## Playbook steps
1. Own the mistake immediately and be more upset than they are. 2. Refund, then cover their incurred costs. 3. Add above-and-beyond magic moments (comps, upgrades, personal attention). 4. Fix the root cause so it never recurs. 5. Pre-authorize frontline make-good options and track how often they're used. 6. Follow up personally; customers often update 1-star reviews to 5.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Hiding behind policy ("you accepted the shipment, it's your liability") and charging extra to fix your own error.
- Treating a refund as the ceiling.
- Owners blaming the team: "you messed up."
- Frontline staff with no power to fix anything ("a losing card").

## Examples / case details
- Gym equipment seller: a bent frame arrived. The seller cited liability and wanted extra for a replacement part. Alex threw the piece out and tells everyone the company sucks.
- Ritz-Carlton Orlando (during a gym conference trip): a 3.5-hour Michelin-star dinner with no drinks for 45 minutes. Manager Javier comped the dinner, upgraded rooms for 8 team members, sent fruit and drinks, booked restaurants and met them there to seat them. It wasn't even his division. Alex now always stays there and refers people.
- Acquisition.com scheduling mishap: 7 business owners (5 international, some needing visas) flew in to find nothing scheduled. They refunded the money plus travel, and Alex planned a 3-hour dinner and ~3 hours of personal consulting per business.
- Hot sauce acquaintance: a kit shipped without caps. He sent a handwritten note and extra caps, the customer changed 1 star to 5, and he now ships every kit with a note and an extra cap.
- Lady suing a doctor: when told her beloved primary care physician was the one at fault, she dropped it.
