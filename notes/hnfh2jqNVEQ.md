## Core thesis
Lesson from Marcus Rivera (Vista's former head of pricing, packaging & product): the best operators score leads, prospects and customers, find the "vein" of highest-LTV customers, and focus on expanding it. Gym Launch applied this by qualifying the front end: sales halved but escalations went from 22 to 0 and new clients paid more. Second lesson: customers decide whether to stay between the sale and the end of onboarding, so set conservative expectations and choreograph onboarding. Despite the title, the content is mostly about customer qualification and retention, not price-setting. (Older Gym Launch-era video.)

## Frameworks & models
- **Vista "3 and 3"**: triple money in 3 years; VSOPs (closely guarded SOPs) laid over acquired companies; keep simple systems and fundamentals.
- **Score each pipeline segment**: prospects, opportunities, customers. Diligence aims to know the customers better than the founder does.
- **Exit/escalation analysis**: take the last 90 days of exits, escalations and hardest-to-serve/least-result clients vs best (most money, highest returns, easiest). Profile age, job, goals, what they bought, money down, continuity terms. Best data comes from people who left.
- **Two retention decision points**: (1) between purchase and start of service; (2) onboarding/implementation itself.
- **Selling hot**: expectations set so high they can't be met for most customers. Fix: sell toward behavior and long-term change; show averages, not outlier testimonials.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Gym Launch qualification: at least 25 clients, signed lease/own building, full-time gym owner; ideally ≥1 employee (optional).
- Accept a smaller inflow if it removes disproportionate operational load; "it's okay to have a stern line."
- Ideally sell on the bottom ~25% of results so most customers overachieve the baseline.
- If onboarding is rushed, lengthen it; it multiplies LTV.
- Defined-end program beat a year-upfront version in their trial.

## Playbook steps
1. Pull the last 90 days of churned/escalated vs best customers. 2. List traits for each. 3. Turn the best-customer traits into front-end qualification. 4. Double down on that vein across platforms; route poor-fit segments elsewhere (e.g. a self-paced info product for entrepreneurs). 5. Post-sale: personalized video, swag/letter, texts before onboarding. 6. Onboarding: do it with them, have them repeat the process to prove skill transfer. 7. Sales and testimonials: show averages and explain half get less.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Not scoring leads/customers.
- Lowering the bar: unqualified customers take team time, earn less, and then say the product is bad.
- Treating orientation as an afterthought ("you're screwed").
- Outlier testimonials creating disappointment (e.g. someone makes $20K first month and is disappointed; a client loses 5 lb in 14 days and is upset).

## Examples / case details
- Gym Launch: qualification applied in October after a Jul–Sep analysis; sales fell from 100+/month to ~40–50; escalations in the 4-week windows went 22 → 0; new cohort paid more, earned more, needed less work.
- Non-gym-owners, side-hustlers, no lease, and no employees did worse; entrepreneurs were a poor demographic.
- Average gym ~$16K in first six weeks, shared instead of "$100K in six weeks" stories.
- Gym Launch's 72-hour "ready, fire, go" pace with leads arriving.
