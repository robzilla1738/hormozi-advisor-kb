## Core thesis
Most growth comes from finding and solving the one A+ problem, the constraint. Knocking out lots of easy B+/C problems doesn't move revenue. Commitment is "the elimination of alternatives": you prioritize one thing, create urgency, eliminate everything else (let the small fires burn), then reassess and repeat. ## Frameworks & models
- **Pruned tree**: the tree's height is revenue. Side offshoots (B+ "growth hacks" like email sequence tweaks) pull resources away from the trunk. Cut them so everything goes into the trunk.
- **A+ vs B+ problems**: you know how to start, run, and finish problems #2–5. For #1 you don't know where to start, what to do, or how it ends, but solving it makes the others irrelevant. People pick the easy ones for the "fast dopamine."
- **Theory of constraints**: one thing limits your growth. Strategy = "prioritize limited resources against unlimited opportunities." The best strategists also see unknown moves (a 10x option vs the 2x on your list).
- **Big wheel vs small wheel**: one turn of a big wheel beats ten of a small one. Judge effort by distance covered per unit of effort, not by how fast you work.
- **Four-step focus process**: (1) Prioritize one thing → (2) Create urgency (deadline, more frequent meetings) → (3) Eliminate everything else from people's plates → (4) Reassess and re-prioritize, then repeat.
- **Graveyard of half-built bridges**: money is lost on unfinished initiatives that never get a dollar "to the other side."
- **One objective per company**: cites Frank Slootman (three-time IPO CEO), whose companies get only one objective.
- **Information transparency**: share financials, conversion rates, and margins so more people can help solve problems. Cites Sam Walton's fear of showing financials, and points out that public companies show everything and still function.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Work on #1 only. Don't bother ranking #7 vs #8: "you're not even going to work on it."
- Always reassess after solving #1. Fixing it often makes #2–5 irrelevant (e.g. fixing the pixel may fix conversion-rate problems) or raises a different priority.
- Make the meeting cadence match the priority: move from weekly to end of day or twice a day. Check in about an hour after a meeting.
- Ask for estimates in minutes and hours, not days.
- Don't add an urgent priority on top of normal work. Remove the normal work, or it won't work, especially over time.
- Test for a top priority: "Is there anything else we could do with the resources we have that would give that kind of a return…in the next 30 days?" If not, it becomes the only priority.
- Accept that the A+ problem may take 1–5 days with no visible progress. Understanding the problem counts as progress.

## Playbook steps
1. List everything that's wrong, then explicitly say you'll ignore all of it except the one thing.
2. Run a prioritization meeting with "amnesia": nobody is accountable for anything before today, and "who did this" questions are for understanding, not blame.
3. Give named permission to let fires burn ("John, if CS complains your turnarounds are slow, tell them I said it's fine"). The boss signs the paychecks, so the boss takes the heat.
4. Break the fix into line items, assign each one to a person, get time estimates in hours, and set a same-day check-in (e.g. 4:30pm) for people to show their work.
5. Have each person say back what they'll do next. Repeat until it's correct, and have them write it down.
6. Make winning visible: a dashboard or thermometer, plus a "pot of gold" reward (team dinner, rest of the day off).
7. Regularly pull up each person's agenda ("show me everything you're working on"). Kill or table old asks that still use up their attention, then give praise ("atta boys") right away.
8. When it's solved, go back to the list, pick the next one, and repeat.
9. On an unknown problem, "start pulling the thread." The next step becomes obvious only after the first one.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Doing #5, #3, and #2 because they're quick: happy at the end of the day, sad at the end of the month.
- Teams fighting small fires because they fear blame while the big fire grows.
- Many simultaneous "priorities" that overwhelm people. Last week's priority is still unfinished.
- Following common growth advice: "if they were right people would do them and then grow." Growth is counterintuitive.
- Hoarding information out of an "irrational fear."

## Examples / case details
- **Paid-ads portfolio team**: a pixel-tracking issue went unsolved for 3 weeks while people optimized funnels, pages, and follow-ups. If the pixel is fed bad data, no conversion optimization matters. He ignored ugly pages, cancelled people's other meetings, assigned line items across 7 funnels (about 6 hours of work), and met again that day. He recorded the video between those calls.
- **Show-up-rate portfolio company**: the team blamed lead quality. Reaching the industry-benchmark show-up rate would add +35% revenue, which at about a 10% margin would "more than triple the profit." The whole company focused on that one number, and the business grew more than 35%. Small CS and sales issues went unaddressed in the meantime.
