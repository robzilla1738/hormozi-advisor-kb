## Core thesis
There are only four paths to ultra wealth, defined by whose money goes into whose business: (1) other people's money in your business (raise capital/VC), (2) your money in your business (bootstrap, compound via ROIC), (3) your money in other people's businesses (investor), (4) other people's money in other people's businesses (fund manager). Every business takes on some form of "debt" from day one; the choice is which kind. The richest outcomes come from concentrated bets (paths 1–2) or stacked leverage (path 4); investing (path 3) is the surest way to "just get rich."

## Frameworks & models
- **Four paths matrix**: OPM→your biz (venture-backed founder); your money→your biz (bootstrapped founder); your money→OPB (investor); OPM→OPB (fund manager/GP).
- **Every business incurs debt**: bootstrapping = talent debt, technology debt, etc.; raising = financial debt/dilution. Choose financial debt only when market dynamics demand it.
- **When to raise (path 1)**: winner-take-all/network-effect markets where speed captures the market (Facebook, LinkedIn), or businesses needing huge capital for a long time before profit (pharma, cinemas). Otherwise "rush is imaginary" — 99% of businesses don't need aggressive growth.
- **Dilution math**: $10M pre-money + $2M = $12M post; investor owns ~20% [inference: 2/12 ≈ 16.7%; his round figure], founders ~80%. "Raised at $500M valuation" may mean selling only 2% for $10M.
- **Primary vs secondary**: secondary = money paid to the founder personally to de-risk them so they make calmer decisions.
- **Liquidity exits**: IPO, or large PE/sovereign funds buying billion-dollar companies privately (Kajabi/Tiger example).
- **Value Equation tied to Bezos**: "what won't change in 10 years" — faster, easier, risk-free (reviews), desirable outcome.
- **Compounding vehicle**: Company A churns everyone and must resell; Company B keeps customers — both hit 300 customers in year 3, B is far better. Two ways to compound: (a) customers who never stop buying (recurring like Netflix or reoccurring like Coca-Cola/Starbucks), (b) a distribution network that never stops selling (Prestige Labs: ~1,000 gym locations selling monthly).
- **LTV:CAC → ROIC**: LTV:CAC is the base unit of arbitrage; ROIC is the bigger frame (includes build-out, licenses, recruiting, tech). Reinvest profit in your own business when ROIC is high.
- **Investor thesis (Acquisition.com PE arm)**: 1) high cash flow/profitable (monthly distributions = faster feedback); 2) clear growth path (more customers or higher lifetime gross profit per customer); 3) focused founder on one avatar/ICP (no "niche slap" needed); 4) jockey over everything (Buffett's competence + work ethic + integrity), as long as the market isn't dying.
- **Theory of constraints**: find the constraint and throw everything at it; it moves down the pipeline (ads → sales staff → fulfillment crews in lawn care).
- **Fund economics (2 and 20)**: GP puts ~5% ($5M) of a $100M fund; leverage $100M + $200M debt = $300M of companies; triple to $900M over 10 years. Minus $200M debt, $100M LP capital, ~$100M interest = $500M profit → 20% carry = $100M (~$80M after capital-gains-style tax) + $20M management fees (2%×10 yrs). ~$100M+ on a $5M slug; scale ×20 for a $2B fund.
- **Two strongest forces in business**: leverage and supply & demand.
- **Fund justification**: proprietary deal flow (better price/terms, no auction) + edge to accelerate value. Investing as LTV:CAC: cheap deal acquisition + higher "lifetime deal value" = alpha.
- **PE vs VC**: PE = bigger AUM, 5–10 deals/fund, consistent 3–5×, inorganic growth/financial arbitrage (roll-ups); VC = 50–100 deals/fund, hunting 100–1000× and accepting losers, organic growth. Acquisition.com sits in the middle: value-add organic growth, little M&A.
- **Roll-up arbitrage example**: buy shops at 5× with half debt, combine, sell at 10× — triple money without growing any company.
- **Deal flow = advertising**: same customer-acquisition laws (content, paid ads, outbound, brokers/affiliates); "customers are now deals and products are now companies."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- First business: bootstrap with your own money — pay down "ignorance debt" on your money, not friends and family's.
- Only raise if speed/network effects or long capital needs require it.
- Don't act venture-backed if you're bootstrapped; don't chase growth for its own sake.
- Franchising = "most expensive form of equity" (give away the business for a % of top line, still service locations); usually chosen because founders are in a rush.
- Reinvest in own business when ROIC beats alternatives (e.g., $50K store returning $250K in 12 months).
- When taking a VC check: you are their 1% bet, they're your 100%; expect pressure toward aggressive growth.
- Invest where you have edge; the better you understand it, the bigger the bet.

## Playbook steps
1. Pick the path that matches your goal (just rich → invest soundly; top-100 rich → concentrated founder bet or fund). 2. Start bootstrapped. 3. Build a compounding vehicle (retention or distribution). 4. Track LTV:CAC and ROIC; reinvest profits while ROIC is high. 5. Attack the current constraint. 6. With excess cash, invest using the 4-criteria thesis. 7. Eventually, with proprietary deal flow + edge, consider raising a fund.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Raising money for businesses that don't need it; ego-driven growth at all costs (Boston Market vs Chick-fil-A).
- VC-forced overspending on ads/over-hiring that masks bad fundamentals; diluted culture.
- Losing board control through many rounds (Steve Jobs; a friend fired after a billion-dollar-valuation exit, asked back 3 years later).
- Franchising out of impatience.
- Investing in dying markets (gas cars, print newspapers).
