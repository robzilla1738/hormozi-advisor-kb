## Core thesis
How Acquisition.com operates from the inside: culture is shaped by who you hire and fire. Hiring is a two-way sell that starts onboarding. Job descriptions are copy written by the manager from the pain. A "write a freaking memo" culture replaces meetings-by-talking. Unreasonable timelines plus "what do you have to show for it?" beat reasonable ones.

## Frameworks & models
- **Competence-to-humility ratio**: rare and prized. Leila cuts high-performing leaders who step on others: "I know what we will become if I multiply them through the organization." The test is "Would I want 10 more of them?" (from the book *Multipliers*).
- **Environmental boost**: a culture that already practices sincere candor gives a hire about 50% of the skill. A little skill gets them to about 70%, versus starting from 0. Environment acts as a thermometer: a 50° environment drags down someone who wants to burn at 80°.
- **80% + gap**: nobody comes in at 100% on the values. Ask what it will take to get someone from 8/10 to 10/10.
- **Interview = beginning of onboarding**: interview and sell at the same time. The senior interviewer is "what above you looks like" and validates (or not) the brand. Congruence between interview and onboarding reduces churn.
- **Separate culture and technical interviews**: culture interviews are run by people unrelated to the role, who have no skin in the game. Keep technical rounds light: "you don't need 17 people doing 17 technical interviews."
- **Hire for skills implied by experience** (avatar hypothesis): e.g. a Wynn resort concierge → executive assistant. Define how the person talks and acts (their "swagger") and hand that to the recruiter.
- **Job description = ad copy, written by the hiring manager**: list all the pain (or the future capacity needed), then turn each pain into what this person will do, then let AI polish it. "ChatGPT doesn't know the pain that you have." Hire for one of two reasons: solve current pain or create future capacity.
- **Memo culture (#WFM, "write a freaking memo")**: 1–2 pages covering the big idea, why it matters, how to solve it, risks, and a recommendation. Memo structure: **Why → What → How → Now**. No memo, no meeting. Two models: pre-read (theirs) or Amazon-style silent reading for the first 15 minutes. The writer gains depth, and readers start at about 70% context ("organizational IQ"). Memos separate the person from the issue (an artifact replaces group-chat complaining). They're also used for 1:1s and personal development: "what's your constraint?"
- **Accordion (future ↔ present)**: the partner says Leila jumps 3 years out and reasons back ("we need 3 more hires now = capacity for the future"). With Alex, "stay in the future"; he takes Alex's present-day ideas and implements them.
- **"Do and who"**: Leila admits she over-indexes on people and leadership and under-presses on output speed. She now focuses on both.
- **"No-hinged vs yes-hinged" / too-long vs shortcut people**: push anxious perfectionists to go faster, and give shortcut-takers more time.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Ask "can we do 6 months of work in 60 days?" Reject reasonable quarterly framing ("86 days left" on a 4-hour project).
- End each day by asking "What do I have to show for it?" If the answer is nothing, re-prioritize tomorrow.
- Start any assignment the day it's assigned. Inspiration and momentum are perishable. Ask for a super-fast "shitty first draft."
- Clear standing meetings to get priorities done; you don't need permission.
- Expect bad hires: "You will hire the wrong person." Leila hired 3 bad executive assistants before a good one. Even experienced teams "get it wrong all the time."
- Bring in a senior leader or partner only if you'll use your freed capacity for something more valuable. Don't cruise after hiring them.
- Treat everyone to the same standard regardless of title. Don't call people "VAs"; give functional titles like "operations manager." Put contractors in Slack and give them gifts.
- No silos: leaders are "a head in the organization," not head of one function. Recruiting partners with sales and marketing ("recruiting is sales"). Frame cross-team work as "I'm partnering with you; it's your thing, you get the credit."

## Playbook steps
1. Write the JD yourself, pain first, in about 20 minutes, then let AI polish. 2. Define the avatar and hand it to the recruiter. 3. Run separate culture and technical interviews. 4. Interview as the first step of onboarding: sell and showcase. 5. A new leader meets everyone and asks "What one piece of advice do you have for me?", then looks for patterns. 6. Require memos (Why/What/How/Now) before meetings. 7. Set unreasonable timelines and a daily "what do I have to show for it?" check.

## Mistakes he/they warn against
- Copy-pasting generic role JDs ("a CFO is a CFO is a CFO"). Teams that protect the manager from writing the JD push the pain into the interviews.
- Over-technical hiring loops that exhaust candidates.
- Group-chat complaining about people instead of writing an artifact.
- Skipping process. Alex and Leila got "ripped" for dropping a persuasive video instead of a memo, and Leila recorded an apology video.
- New hires "bringing their California to Texas" (importing silos and corporate jargon).
- Demeaning offshore contractors.

## Examples / case details
- About 1,000 applications for the General Counsel role. The JD was a page-long memo the manager wrote, and the interview was mostly discussing the company's problems. A non-fit candidate with "magic" was routed to Leila to keep the talent.
- Portfolio operator JDs written by the partner drew strong applicants: "the job description was just exactly what I want."
- Leila's first EA (Yasmi) was hired from a Facebook post headed "Busy business owner, tired of working 17-hour days with no clear inbox." She still works for Leila 8 years later.
- Gym Launch's Nigerian CS contractors: a $300–350 swag bag sent during COVID unrest. That contractor's family joined and "outperformed every person in the United States."
- Finance project cut from 6 months to a 60-day target.
- Leila's book: a first draft written chapter by chapter with no editing until the end (editor's advice: "all first drafts look like shit").
- Creativity takes conditions: Alex and Leila block the first hours of the day or weekends to write, not after 17 meetings.
