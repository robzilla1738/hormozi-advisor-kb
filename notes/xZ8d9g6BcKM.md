## Core thesis
At 21, Alex nearly opened a frozen yogurt store (alternatives: test prep or fitness). This is a teardown of fro-yo economics and the psychology tricks worth borrowing: pay-by-weight pricing, default (bigger) cup sizes, highest-margin items first, more selection, buying a failed competitor's equipment for pennies, and word of mouth or affiliates as the only profitable acquisition at low ticket prices. Commoditized, poorly run industries can be won by beating the bottom 25%.

## Frameworks & models
- **Unit economics**: average store ~$750–800K/yr (~$2,100/day, ~500 eight-ounce cups/day); net margin 10–15%. He cites ~$328/day owner pay, and says the average Menchie's owner takes home ~$93K/yr.
- **Franchise math**: ~6% of top line in royalties with a ~10% margin takes ~60% of your take-home. Franchisors set fees so you survive but don't get rich (targeting ~20–25% return on capital vs the stock market's ~10%). Bulk purchasing should save you money, but franchisors often mark up supplies.
- **Ingredient margins**: yogurt costs ~8¢/oz and sells for ~50–60¢/oz. Toppings cost 10–40¢/oz against a 25–60¢ price; fruit roughly breaks even (1 lb of strawberries ≈ $10 ≈ 60¢/oz). Cups average ~75% yogurt, 25% toppings by weight.
- **Psychology levers**: (1) pay by weight, so customers blame themselves ("I'm such a fat ass") instead of the store; (2) default option: removing small cups probably lifted sales 20–30% [his guess]; same logic as "medium or large?" and assumed closes; (3) sequence from highest margin to lowest (yogurt → dry toppings → fruit), like buffets putting salad first and seafood last; (4) more selection means people consume more.
- **Acquisition at low ticket**: paid ads are mostly unprofitable now (PPC used to work), so rely on word of mouth and affiliates. Top stores win through better service, cleanliness, selection and word of mouth.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Before buying new equipment, check foreclosure or liquidation sites. He bought his first gym's equipment for $13K by refreshing weekly. He found three failed fro-yo stores whose full contents were all ≤$20K, i.e. 5–10¢ on the dollar from motivated sellers.
- "To beat everybody you've got to be better than the bottom 25%."
- Pay a small incentive to capture a text-list lead (he cites a ~$2 lead cost), then use LTV to bring them back or bring friends.
- Ask for reviews and shares explicitly. Doing that on his podcast 20× the reviews.

## Playbook steps
1. Pick an industry with commoditized, sloppy incumbents. 2. Buy liquidated equipment. 3. Use usage-based pricing, big default sizes and margin-ordered layouts. 4. Promote through groups: fraternity and sorority competitions, companies, affiliates. 5. Capture SMS leads with an incentive. 6. Systematically ask for referrals and reviews.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Assuming revenue = owner income ($800K/yr ≠ getting rich).
- Paying franchise royalties on thin margins without understanding them.
- Sticky floors, broken nozzles, staff on phones.

## Examples / case details
- No "Chick-fil-A of yogurt" exists, so there's an opening for a real brand.
- His planned store: floor-to-ceiling see-through candy, deliberately "clunky" nozzles that over-dispense, near a college.
