# NEVER lower your prices...
id: BZQtuK-ucDM / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BZQtuK-ucDM / duration: 530s (~9 min) / tags: 03-pricing, 06-retention-churn, 02-offer-design, 10-money-cashflow
Signal: high

## Core thesis
Lowering prices almost always makes you less money ("99 times out of 100"). Two stories: tripling gym prices with more value doubled revenue and more than doubled profit; a 25% price cut to reduce churn left churn unchanged and cost ~$5M/yr in profit. Charge more and deliver more to fewer people, especially in services.

## Frameworks & models
- **6 ways to raise LTV**: (1) raise price, (2) lower costs, (3) resells (buy more times: burgers twice a week), (4) upsells (more of the same: small → big burger), (5) cross-sells (different thing: fries), (6) downsells (buy something instead of nothing: junior burger).
- **Price-cut break-even**: $100 price, $40 profit → cut to $80 = $20 profit, so you must sell 2x as many to break even. You usually won't.
- **Price-raise math**: $100 → $150 = $90 profit; even at 3/4 the customers you make more. He says "two times the money"; [inference] 0.75 × $90 = $67.50 vs $40, so about 1.7x, still a big gain.
- **Churn-cut bet that failed**: he reasoned a 25% price cut was worth it if churn halved (10% → 5% = 2x LTV). Churn stayed the same.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Default: don't lower prices; most times you make less money.
- Before a price cut, work out the volume needed to replace lost margin per unit (e.g. 2x).
- More value at a higher price → serve fewer people at higher prices = more profit. More stuff at a lower price to more people usually = less, especially in service businesses.
- Sell what you believe in; conviction makes you more money.
- Expect some to leave on a price raise (he lost 1/3) — fine if the remainder pays enough more.

## Playbook steps
1. Build the higher-value offer you believe in.
2. Announce to existing customers: free trial period of the new service (he fronted 2 weeks free labor).
3. Auto-enroll at the new price after the trial; invite people to tell you if it materially hurts them.
4. Accept attrition; check revenue and cost to serve afterward.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Cutting price to fix churn without proof churn will drop.
- Adding overhead (more services) while cutting topline.
- Listening to a team chanting "we have to lower our price."
- Expecting gratitude for a discount: one client complained he hadn't discounted sooner.

## Examples / case details
- Gyms: bootcamp $99/mo → semi-private $299/mo (3x). 1/3 of customers left, 2/3 stayed → revenue 2x, fewer people to serve, profit more than 2x.
- Later business: $42K/yr (~$800/wk) cut to $600/wk (~$31K/yr), ~25% off, plus added services. Overhead +~$100K/mo; topline -~$400K/mo. Churn unchanged; lost ~$5M/yr profit. At ~70% margin a 25% topline cut ≈ a third of profit. [inference: $400K×12 + $100K×12 ≈ $6M/yr gross impact; he rounds to ~$5M.]
