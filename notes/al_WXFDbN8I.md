## Core thesis
There are seven ways people state business "value," and most online gurus use the inflated ones. Learn the terms, ask the right follow-up questions, and judge sources (and your own business) by bottom-line reality: net free cash flow and net worth.

## Frameworks & models
- **The 7 measures, with his ratings**:
  1. **Extrapolated contract value**: NO. A one-time event's annual contracts get quoted as monthly revenue. Only legit when contracts are secured (asset-backed, reliable companies) or the business has net negative churn (e.g., Salesforce, HubSpot).
  2. **Lifetime revenue**: NO. A McDonald's employee earns about $1M over a career.
  3. **Business valuation**: YES, with caveats. Ask what multiple, whether it's on top line or bottom line, and why it's defensible.
  4. **Yearly revenue**: PARTIAL. Only meaningful alongside profit.
  5. **Yearly profit / EBITDA**: (rated together with #4) "what are we doing bottom line per year."
  6. **Net free cash flow (owner earnings)**: YES. What you can take out after reinvesting to maintain/grow your competitive advantage (Buffett/Munger's metric). NFCF × multiple ≈ what an investor pays.
  7. **Net worth**: YES. "What's left after all the chips have fallen." It has two parts: after-tax extracted investable assets, plus your ownership share × a fair valuation. (Ultra high net worth is defined by investable assets, >$30M.)
- **Revenue vs cash**: he uses "revenue" to mean cash collected. Newer entrepreneurs conflate contract value with cash.
- **Typical EBITDA multiples**: <$5M revenue → 2–3× (sometimes 4× with an advantage); mid market $10–100M → 6–8×; >$100M → 12–15×. Top-line multiples are rare: only for fast-growth, capital-reinvesting companies expected to have high future gross profit (often software). Businesses sell at 1–3× every day; the outliers get the press.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- When someone says "$X million business," ask: lifetime or yearly? contract or collected? what multiple, on top or bottom line? what's NFCF? what do you take out as dividends?
- A high EBITDA multiple usually means near-zero profit, not a great business. E.g., $10K profit selling for $5M = 500×.
- A low profit margin can be fine if they reinvest for growth. Look at revenue and profit together.
- If the business requires the founder's face, it won't get the multiple.
- Extract profit monthly (dividends) to de-risk, even while growing. "99 times out of 100 your business never actually sells."
- Prefer businesses that don't need much extra capital (Buffett/Munger).

## Playbook steps
1. Hear the claim. 2. Classify which of the 7 measures it is. 3. Ask the follow-up questions above. 4. Judge the source's authority by NFCF/net worth, not headline numbers. 5. Apply the same to your own business: take out cash each month and build investable assets.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Quoting one event's contracts as monthly run-rate.
- Quoting lifetime revenue as company size. (He admits to doing it himself for headlines: "$122M in revenue" vs "mid-30s for the last three or four years.")
- "I could get 15× top line": "the market will pay what the market will pay."
- Taking advice from people with big revenue claims and little net worth (he says many message him with ~$500K to their name while claiming $10M revenue).
- Betting all your eggs on the business instead of extracting dividends (the Silicon Valley mindset "doesn't live in reality" for everyday owners).

## Examples / case details
- 100 people × $30K/yr contracts at an event = "$3M," but at $2,500/mo it's really $250K collected.
- "$1M lifetime revenue" over 10 years = $100K/yr; at 50% margin = a $50K/yr owner income, about US median income.
- ClickFunnels award for passing $100M total revenue in ~3.5 years; he expected to come close to, but not cross, $100M that year.
- Company with $20M revenue / $500K profit sold for $168M (software, reinvested everything) vs a $1M revenue / $500K profit business that's worth far less.
- $10M revenue / $2M EBITDA at 5× when it doesn't require the founder's face.
- He's had 13 businesses and owns 8 more (21 total). He says only GE and Ford remain from the Fortune 500 of 50 years ago, hence: build personal wealth along the way.
