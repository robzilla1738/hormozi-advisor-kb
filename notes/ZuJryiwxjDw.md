# Why you aren't making as much money as you want
id: ZuJryiwxjDw / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZuJryiwxjDw / duration: 2257s (~38 min) / tags: 03-pricing, 02-offer-design, 05-sales-process, 01-what-business-to-start
Signal: high

## Core thesis
You aren't making enough because you sell to people who don't have the money. Wealth and profit both follow a power law, so price for the people at the top: each new tier at 5–10× the price, expect ~20% (or fewer) to take it, and start as high on the ladder as you can. Stop selling from your own wallet. Use close rates to tell whether you're underpriced.

## Frameworks & models
- **$100 wealth model** (US household net worth ~$163T scaled to $100 across 100 people): bottom 50% ≈ $2; next 40% ≈ $28; next 9% ≈ $38; top 1% ≈ $32. "This one guy has more than the bottom 90% combined"; the top 10% hold ~69%. Top 10% earn ~40% of income. One in 10 Americans has a $1M+ net worth (his claims).
- **Recursive Pareto**: 20% of customers → 80% of profit; 4% → 64%; 1% → ~51%. One whale costs little more to serve than the others, so it's disproportionately profitable.
- **Tier rule of thumb**: each new tier = 5–10× price, ~20% take it, and each tier should add roughly another full amount of revenue (double), or the extra operational constraint isn't worth it. Example: 8 × $10 = $80 + 2 × $50 = $100, so revenue more than doubles. If base covers overhead, profit goes from $10 → $90 (he says "5x"; the math shown is 9× [inference]).
- **Four-tier ladder** (1,000 customers): $10/mo × 800; $100/mo × ~160; $1K/mo × ~32–40; $5–10K/mo × ~8.
- **Acquisition.com ladder**: Skool hobby / Skool pro $100/mo → L1 $5K → L2 $35K → L3 $135K → portfolio company. Built over years, one tier at a time.
- **Top-down entry** (Tesla Roadster $250K → S → 3/Y): anchoring high strengthens the brand ("Rolls-Royce light" beats "Honda making a better car") and has lower operational volume. Rich buyers are less demanding because the price is a smaller % of their net worth.
- **Serving the masses** requires capital plus automation and razor-thin margins (Tesla near bankruptcy; Netflix/Spotify earning $13). ~78–80% of US businesses are services, so go the other way: best customer, highest price.
- **"Before I tell you the price, it's super expensive"**: an emotional anchor that adjusts to each prospect's buying power. In person, write the price down and slide it over.
- **Close rate → hidden price headroom**: 60–80% → 2–3× (he first says 3–4× at 80%); 50–60% → 1.5–2×; 40–50% → 1.25–1.5×; 30–40% = priced about right; <30% = get better at selling (better offer or better-qualified leads).
- **Rich buyers think value ratio, not cost** ("$20K… for what?" Berkshire A share or Lamborghini at $20K = deal). They want **fast, easy, guaranteed**; pre-do the work ("pre-chew the food").
- **Virtuous price cycle (services)**: higher price → higher gross margin → better talent → better service → reputation → demand → price. Raise price until supply meets demand. Pricing is two-way communication; buyers self-select.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- The next tier is 5–10×, not +50% ($1K core → $5–10K upsell, not $1.5K).
- Make an upsell something you'd be happy to deliver at 5–10×. If it's too much work: "either do less or charge more." The added cost is often ~5% of the new price.
- Expect 1 in 5–10 (even 1 in 100) yes's; optimize for the most money, not the most yes's.
- Consumer price points (services): impulse ~$500–600; high ticket ~$3–10K. Small business: cheap ~$400–800/mo (~$500); mid-tier ~$2–3K/mo.
- Qualify leads (e.g., only $1M+ business owners) to lift close rates on expensive offers.
- If competitors undercut you, you're a commodity. Price so high, in such a different category, that buyers must evaluate you separately.

## Playbook steps
1. Stop selling from your own wallet; "imagine everyone is rich."
2. Build an upsell at 5–10× the core, designed to "blow people away."
3. Pitch it to qualified, monied prospects; expect most to say no; the anchor still lifts sales of the rest.
4. Check your close rate against the headroom table; raise price accordingly.
5. Add tiers one at a time; start as high as you can.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Competing with other small businesses for the bottom 50%'s "$2."
- Taking pricing advice from friends/family who don't have money.
- Being so cheap buyers don't believe it works.
- "The only thing worse than offering a $1,000 thing to somebody who's got a $100 budget is offering a $100 thing to somebody who's got a $1,000 budget" (lose $100 vs lose $900, mostly profit).
- Copying Netflix/Spotify-style mass models while bootstrapped.
- Pitching the 10× price to the wrong audience and concluding it's a bad idea.

- Absolute profit: he says one $10K sale costing $2K equals 400 × $50 sales costing $25. [inference: as stated, those are $8K vs $10K of profit; the point stands that one large sale rivals hundreds of small ones.]
