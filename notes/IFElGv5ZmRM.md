## Core thesis
"The single greatest skill you can develop is the ability to stay in a great mood in the absence of things to be in a great mood about." A four-part resilience framework from a hard year (including a surprise multi-8-figure bill due in cash in 72 hours): (1) learn the greatest skill: reframing; (2) redefine pain as the price of what you want; (3) endure what others won't, which becomes a compounding competitive advantage; (4) zoom out and keep going. Act on behavior, not feelings. Mostly personal and mindset content. It is useful for founders in a hard season, not for tactical business decisions [inference].

## Frameworks & models
- **Math of hard days**: across 100 days, about 10 are top, 80 neutral and 10 bottom. Bottom-10% days are statistically normal and are what make top days recognizable.
- **Utilitarian loss test**: "What does this change about what I do?" (food, home, work). If nothing, only an expectation in your head changed. Marcus Aurelius: "What are you so afraid of losing when nothing in this world belongs to you?" Money is chips you're "renting."
- **Good day in a bad season → good moment in a bad day**: string good days together to turn the season. Bill Ackman (caption: "Aman"): make a little progress every day. Big goals come with equally large obstacles that may take years.
- **Good-day formula** (his): eat with people I like, lift with people I like, write something. Upgrade: no rush and few hard stops. He trades some money or upside for consistently better days.
- **Pain = price**: "accept boredom or pain; the bigger the goal, the more of both." Reframe pain as the price of the thing you want. He cites a shock study where pain threshold roughly quadruples when it spares family (cited from memory). Purpose turns aversive stimuli into positive reinforcers.
- **Work works on you**: "in all labor there is profit." You only start from scratch once; after that you start with experience.
- **Hate comment decoder**: almost every attack = "he lives his life in a way I would not prefer." Just because someone hurls it doesn't mean you catch it.
- **Hardship reveals, doesn't define**: traits are opinions until tested. Survived hardship pays "memory dividends."
- **Define in behavior** (team training example): vague terms like "clean vs dark energy" are useless until defined as observable behaviors under conditions. Then it's just "in these conditions do this."
- **Sadness vs anxiety**: sadness = perceived lack of options → solve with knowledge. Anxiety = many options, no priorities → solve with a decision (cut paths). "If you don't know what to do, learn. If you know, do it. If you're doing it, never stop."
- **Resilience vectors**: resilience = speed of return to baseline behavior; toughness = how much it takes to cause a drop; depth = how low you go.
- **Zoom-out frames**: Veteran (how would I feel the 1,000th time?), Cosmic relevance, Grandfather (wake up at 85, then back in today's body). Gratitude = imagine a terrible thing, then notice it hasn't happened.
- **Solomon frame** (Solomon paradox): write to your wise 85-year-old self like a DM. You get full context and confidentiality, and you can't spin your data for an advisor. He has also reversed roles with a business owner so she gave the obvious advice to herself.
- **Use what you have**: the motive (shame vs aspiration) doesn't matter; actions that raise the likelihood of the outcome do.
- **Second arrow** (Buddhist parable): the first arrow is uncontrollable; your reaction is optional. "Bad things come in threes" = failing to isolate one bad thing so it snowballs.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If an event changes nothing about what you actually do, don't let it consume you; decide how long you'll beat yourself up.
- "Are you settling or are you moving mountains?" (a mentor's line to Leila). Before accepting two bad options, ask what you'd do if lives depended on it.
- Don't skip doing something because you might lose it later; you lose everything at death anyway.
- Don't worry about what hasn't occurred: it won't happen as imagined, you'll have different resources then, and the path will be obvious. Plan, but don't ruminate.
- Isolate a bad event fast. Recover before it touches your spouse, kids or other projects.
- In some seasons, maintaining is winning.
- Judge a year zoomed in (e.g. 85% good moments / 15% bad), not by an abstract label.
- Score yourself on effort: winning without trying is a loss; exhausting every worthwhile path and losing is still "good job."

## Playbook steps
1. Reframe the event (why, origin-story test: "in 40 years, is this my pivot point?"). 2. Run the utilitarian test. 3. Aim for a good day / good moment; run your good-day formula with no rush. 4. Name the price the pain is paying for. 5. Define the next action in behavior, not feelings (learn / decide / keep doing). 6. Return to baseline behavior fast; isolate the damage. 7. Use the Veteran, Grandfather or Solomon frame when emotional.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Letting one bad thing ruin many good things; using a bad day as an excuse to stop exercising self-control.
- Equating actions with feelings ("your mood, follow the plan" — Leila).
- Amorphous training concepts with no observable behaviors.
- Spinning context when asking others for advice; telling friends or family about spouse conflicts they won't forget.
- Expecting something for nothing; not really trying. He says ~9% of Americans are millionaires and calls a six-pack after 30 rarer (he says he hasn't verified this).
