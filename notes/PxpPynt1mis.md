# Here's Why I Always Give Away a % of My Business

## Core thesis
To grow your slice, grow the pie and let key people get wealthy using your business as their vehicle. His go-to tool for this is **phantom equity**: equity that hasn't vested and only vests when a trigger event happens, usually a sale or liquidity event. He has used it with high-level employees in just about every company he owns. The goal is "owner-like thinking, owner-like engagement, owner-like behavior" from people who aren't majority owners. He explains it "to the extent that I understand it as an entrepreneur." It is not legal or tax advice.

## Frameworks & models
- **Phantom equity variables (all negotiable):**
  - **Trigger:** a sale or change in majority ownership lets holders sell alongside the owner. They might sell up to a cap or a proportional amount. An owner may leave out a sell-alongside clause because a buyer may not want key employees cashing out and leaving.
  - **Tax:** no equity is actually transferred upfront, so the employee isn't taxed on a grant and doesn't have to buy in. The downside is that it is usually taxed as regular income at the sale. He also calls it "entirely tax free" at one point; [inference] read that as "no tax at grant," because he says payout is taxed as income.
  - **Vesting schedule:** for example 5% over 5 years with a 1-year cliff. 1% vests at month 12, then 0.25% each quarter after. At month 9, nothing has vested.
  - **Leaving:** the agreement can say an employee who is terminated or leaves forfeits unvested phantom equity. This protects the owner and rewards staying.
  - **Liability:** holders don't carry the downside or liability of real ownership. The caption says "are personally liable," which is likely a caption error given the context.
  - **Profit vs equity are separate levers:** you can own equity without profit distributions, and the reverse. He personally keeps profit sharing out of it ("just my preference").
- **Sizing (from his mentors):** senior or executive-track leaders get about 0.5–2%, depending on business value, seniority and experience. A true executive leading about half the company might get 3–5%.
- **Alternatives:** bonus programs and ESOPs.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If a role's results are easy to measure (e.g. marketing), use a performance-based deal, not equity. This came from a newer owner who wanted to give equity to a marketer.
- Equity-type incentives make sense for roles that are harder to measure, over time.
- Give phantom equity in proportion to effort and contribution, to key players only. Don't hand out disproportionate chunks.
- "The best people want a stake," because winners know they'll win.

## Playbook steps
1. Decide whether the role can just be paid on performance. If it can, do that.
2. For key leaders, grant phantom equity sized to their role (0.5–2%, or 3–5% for a true executive).
3. Define the trigger (sale or change of control) and the sell-alongside terms.
4. Set vesting (multi-year, with a cliff) and forfeiture if they leave.
5. Decide separately on profit distributions.
6. Get professional help writing the agreement. [inference] He says he won't go into the complexities.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Giving equity when a performance deal would do.
- Swinging between extremes. He was very loose with equity early on and "ended up paying for it," then became too stingy to attract top people.
- Treating equity and profit share as the same thing.

## Examples / case details
- He was an affiliate for a big software company. The CEO offered him phantom equity instead of real equity to make promoting it worth his focus, and that is how he learned the structure.
- Sam Walton, in *Made in America*, credited Walmart's employee stock option program with huge buy-in and growth and wished he'd done it sooner.
- People who held phantom equity have left on good terms, protected from a tax standpoint.
