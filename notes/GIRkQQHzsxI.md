# You're Wasting 80% of Your Time (here's how to fix it)
id: GIRkQQHzsxI / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GIRkQQHzsxI / duration: 2217s (~37 min) / tags: 08-ops-scaling, 07-hiring-management, 12-mindset-leverage

## Core thesis
"Productivity is how much money you get out for the time you put in." Building on Paul Graham's maker vs manager schedule essay: managers profit from full calendars of small slots, while makers profit from empty calendars with a few large blocks. When managers schedule makers like managers, they destroy the deep work that actually moves the business. He calls being "ruthless with my time" his single highest predictor of success. The fix has to come from all three sides: managers, makers and the organization.

## Frameworks & models
- **Manager schedule**: 20+ chunks/day (5–90 min). An empty slot is a lost opportunity; a fully booked day = maximally invested. The day ends at the last meeting. The output is decisions, direction and coordination; they work *on* meetings.
- **Maker schedule**: 2 (sometimes 3) 4–6-hour blocks/day, roughly 10/week. An empty calendar = a maximally productive day. Set start time, open-ended finish ("work open to goal"). The output is the thing built; they work *off* meetings.
- **Zeigarnik effect** (caption: "zernick"): a pending meeting is an open loop that kills flow even before it starts.
- **Cost asymmetry**: one meeting costs a manager about 1 of 100+ slots but costs a maker 1 of ~10 weekly blocks (~10% of weekly output). It is "10× more" expensive, and coordinating it disrupts the maker twice. A stressful meeting (e.g. a 2pm performance review) can wipe the whole day.
- **Vicious cycle**: a maker falls behind → the manager checks in more → less making.
- **Maker's no**: a declined meeting is the maker keeping the bigger commitment; don't penalize it.
- **Three stages of maker time** (his career): V1 = nights, weekends and early mornings around the client's day (gym owner: ad copy before 9am, then sales and training; 5 weekday + 4 weekend = 9 blocks/week; "work the job you're in and the job to get out of it"). V2 = 50/50: mornings maker, afternoons manager, booked **back to front** (first meeting ends at 5, next ends at 4…) to keep the morning open and avoid dead 45-minute gaps. V3 = whole maker days (2–4/week) plus one stacked manager day.
- **Inverted schedules**: Alex has ~4 maker days and 1 manager day; Leila (operator) has ~1 maker day and 4 manager days. Each needs some of both. The maker time buys leverage on the manager time.
- **Control vs freedom**: you can't have absolute control and absolute freedom. Each level of entrepreneurship means giving up more control: extend trust with guardrails, measure outputs, tighten the reins if missed.
- **Meeting cost math**: $50K/yr ≈ $25/hr, $100K ≈ $50/hr. A 10-person, 1-hour call ≈ $400–500. That's like taking the team to Chili's, "10 times a day."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Managers: before an ad hoc meeting, ask if it can wait for the set cadence or is just your convenience. If it truly needs the maker, say so and give them prep time.
- Managers: ask makers what their ideal day/week looks like and follow it as closely as possible.
- Makers: if a meeting lands in a block, convert the whole block to manager mode and batch every other meeting into it.
- Makers: set standing meeting windows (e.g. Mon/Thu afternoons) and leave spare slots for ad hoc meetings. Truly urgent meetings are rare.
- Tell people when you'll be slow to respond and be responsive when you said you would. Upset comes from unmet expectations.
- Actually work in maker time, or you prove the skeptical manager right.
- On a "dead brain" day, take meetings. On a "touch of God" day, ask to move meetings; most people say it wasn't urgent.
- Remote makers: extend longer trust periods tied to deadlines and measure output. Fast Slack replies likely mean they're *not* working. If deadlines are missed, ask why; it's often personal life.
- After every meeting: does it need to happen again? Can a process or person replace me? Weekly: delete meetings first. Quarterly: audit all recurring meetings across the company (Leila's practice).
- Normalize leaving meetings where you're not adding value; that returns time to the company.

## Playbook steps
Organization: 1. Mandated quiet time for makers only (engineers, editors, copywriters, writers…). Acquisition.com: Wednesday quiet day for all makers, so no maker waits more than 2 days for a guaranteed quiet day. 2. Say which hat you're wearing ("maker hat today"). 3. Prune meetings constantly. 4. Spread the maker/manager language to everyone. Payoff: happier, longer-tenured staff with higher quantity and quality of output.

## Mistakes he warns against
- "Got a minute?" / "meet John for coffee at 10" — no agenda, steals a morning and returns nothing.
- Judging makers by empty calendars.
- Makers saying yes to everything to be polite, then losing nights, weekends and sleep to catch up.
- Entrepreneurs with neither maker nor manager structure. "This is how businesses stay small": reliving the same 6–12 months.
- Without early maker time, he says he'd never have gotten past one gym location (training, nutrition and ad systems were all built in maker time).
