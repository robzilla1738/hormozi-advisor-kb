## Core thesis
Document your business mistakes and successes so you learn the *right* lesson and can skip known "levels" next time. Entrepreneurship is like a video game: replaying levels you've already beaten is fast, and new territory is slow. He says his wealth only began compounding after he started documenting, 90 days after losing everything for the second time. Documenting publicly adds accountability and content.

## Frameworks & models
- **Entrepreneurship as a video game**: known levels (hiring a sales director, running FB ads) go fast once learned. New ones ("virgin territory") mean die, respawn, repeat. Experts don't move faster; they make fewer wasted moves.
- **"Lessons and Failures <year>" email thread to yourself**: hundreds of one-line notes per year. Open-loop stories compare what you thought would happen with what did.
- **Wrong-lesson risk**: "the only thing worse than having the mistake is learning the wrong lesson from the mistake." Paraphrasing Dr. Bergman: it's worse to succeed and not know why than to fail and know why. People blame outside circumstances for failure but not for success.
- **Learning continuum**: a fool never learns. A dumb person learns eventually. A smart person learns one lesson per mistake, a very smart person several. A wise person learns from others' mistakes. The wisest also learn from successes.
- **Arrow vs sweet spot**: outcomes usually need 2–3 conditions at once. Attributing the result to one of them leads to failure on replay.
- **Three benefits of documenting publicly**: (1) crystallizes the lesson, because stories give context (Musk: teach the wrench by taking apart the engine); (2) accountability to lessons you've stated; (3) money/brand as gravy.
- **Content source = "my news, not the news"**: talk about your current problems, "in arrears" if they're sensitive (e.g. three months later).
- **Multi-game penalty**: playing 3 games (businesses) alternately leaves you not 3× but maybe 6–9× behind a focused competitor, because of the cost of switching and catching back up. [He frames the numbers loosely.]
- **Time-travel frames**: coach yourself as if mentoring someone else; keep a chat thread with your imagined 80-year-old self.
- **Cost of change is guaranteed; the upside from change isn't.** Don't tinker with what isn't the constraint.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- After any failure, ask "what did you learn from that?" and test whether it's the right lesson. "No one can sell like me" after a failed sales hire is the wrong lesson.
- Don't start a new company/major project if your operator isn't on board.
- Learn how long it takes you to do something well. That tells you how few things you can do, so say no to almost everything, including ideas you desperately want (Steve Jobs: "what have you said no to lately?").
- Study successes as hard as failures and repeat their checklist.
- Make yourself the main audience of your documentation, so reviews don't matter.

## Playbook steps
1. Start a yearly lessons/failures log (email thread, tweets, voice memos, podcast). 2. Log predictions vs outcomes. 3. After each win or loss, write why, including all the contributing conditions. 4. Before a new venture, review the log and estimate the real resource cost. 5. Publish lessons (in arrears if sensitive) for accountability and content. 6. Reread winning-period material during losing streaks.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Attributing success to your own skill when it was arbitrage. Gym Launch's early profit came partly from cheap Facebook ads. When that went away, the business had to move from arbitrage to execution.
- The "woman in the red dress" (shiny new opportunities). His repeated lesson was Allan, a software company he started while CEO of others.
- Steamrolling your operator. Leila said they lacked bandwidth, and one business suffered, as she predicted.
- Feature wars in software. Software is hard, and competitors copy features, so build other moats.
- Tinkering with things that work.

## Examples / case details
- Lost everything twice, around the halfway point of 13 years in business. Started his podcast within 3 months, then built a $100M+ net worth over the next ~7 years.
- Gym Launch: at age 27, "took home $17M" profit in the first 12 months. Grew to $26M the next year because he had already run 6 locations, 30+ employees, 8 salespeople, 30–100 sales/day and $1K–3K/day ad spend.
- An Amazon e-commerce founder after his exit: "you could never have a business as successful ever again." He attributed it to luck and never beat it. Every later Hormozi business beat the first.
- Prestige Labs launch: operator on board, team trained, alpha and beta phases, team set the date, staffed, inventory ready. Did $1.7M recurring revenue in month 1. Leila: "nothing fails like success"; the win took a year of resources. (Caption says "nothing feels like success"; likely "nothing fails like success" [inference].)

- Hiring sales directors: it can take 3 hires at ~6 months each to learn what a good one looks like.
