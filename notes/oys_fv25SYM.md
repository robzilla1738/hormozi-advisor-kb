# Advice I Wish Someone Gave Me When I Was Broke

## Core thesis
Getting out of being broke is a matter of foundations, not secret tactics. In order: (1) "it's my fault," (2) use what you have, even if that's anger, shame or pain, (3) spend less than you make, (4) put the surplus into increasing income (skills, experiments), and (5) protect yourself from people who pull you down. It's brute force through a painful "glass" period. Format: a podcast-style interview; Alex answers the interviewer's questions. The content is mostly mindset and personal story, with few business mechanics.

## Frameworks & models
- **Foundation layers**: the base layer is "who is source/in power." If it's anyone but you, everything built on top is "a foundation of sand." He claims ~90% of people never get this layer.
- **Blame = power transfer**: "whatever you cast blame to is where you also cast power to." Blame becomes self-fulfilling through confirmation.
- **Delay pain vs delay gratification**: poor people "take pleasure now and pain later"; "today is also yesterday's tomorrow," so the bill comes due.
- **Pull the timeline toward you / "eat the glass"**: live today's life plus tomorrow's. Life is glass early, then neutral, then sweeter. People "wait too long and they die too early."
- **Income as potential energy vs savings as stored energy**: his goal was monthly income, not bank balance. He'd spend $2K of savings to lift income from $5K to $6K/mo because that returns $12K/yr.
- **Money sequence**: it's your fault → spend less than you make → take all excess and spend it on making more. "You're not going to save your way there" on minimum wage.
- **Failed experiments = experience points**: you're never back at zero, just at "plus N experiences." Enough XP levels you up, and higher levels cost more XP.
- **Skills → buy other people's skills**: the wealthy think only about skills. First you buy skills yourself; later you buy other people's time and years of skill. "Get good at stuff, then eventually you generalize to being good at people."
- **How money is made**: voluntary exchange. You can sell stuff at a margin (part of the margin is convenience), sell your own made stuff, or sell skills/outcomes. Start by selling someone else's stuff (affiliate/referral/ambassador programs) so you learn one skill: selling.
- **Expertise ladder**: learn something → get good → have evidence → people ask for help → monetize. Fitness → gyms → gym-owner licensing → national-scale businesses → big businesses. "The path has always been revealed as I took more steps."
- **"99% right but 100% wrong"** (attributed to Dr. Kashi): naysayers are right on each failure but wrong on a long enough horizon.
- **Pain of change < pain of staying the same** (attributed to Tony Robbins): future-pace and catastrophize the do-nothing path until it hurts more than your comfort.
- **Friend filter**: does this person increase or decrease the probability you hit your goal? It's binary; if they decrease it, drop them.
- **"No social obligations, only social consequences"**: the "punishment" for skipping events is fewer invitations, which he calls a win-win.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Reinvest surplus into raising income rather than hoarding savings while income is low.
- Master basics (e.g., showing up on time) before complex money skills: "they want to go straight to calculus but they don't know simple addition." Understanding something conceptually isn't mastering it.
- First sale: sell something small or someone else's product; don't expect a $10M-building commission first ("factually true, spiritually false").
- Don't take advice from people whose life you don't want.
- Don't wait for the right feelings or a passion. Act from whatever emotion you have.
- Don't try to map the whole path before taking the first step.

## Playbook steps
1. Say "it's my fault" about everything up to now. 2. Use what you have (time, anger, shame). 3. Spend less than you make. 4. Put the excess into skills and income experiments (courses, ads, a store); count failures as XP. 5. Start with affiliate/referral selling to learn sales. 6. Get good at one thing and build evidence so people ask you for help; then sell that. 7. Audit friends (increase/decrease odds) and accept a lonely middle period; use the time to learn. 8. Raise self-approval, ignore the opinions of people whose lives you don't want, and keep taking steps.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Blaming parents, the ZIP code, politics or the economy, which gives them power over you.
- "Starting tomorrow" (the diet Monday, the New Year's resolution).
- Thinking you can save your way out on minimum wage.
- Confusing understanding with skill; skipping basics for complex tactics.
- Requiring positivity or passion before starting.
- Keeping friends who lower your odds of success out of obligation.
- Treating failed experiments as a reset to zero.

## Examples / case details
- 13 years earlier his goal was $10K/month income. His first $100K took 5 years.
- He opened his first brick-and-mortar gym at 23 and did everything himself (cleaning, billing, sales, sessions, equipment); he "didn't know you could hire employees."
- He left a consulting job he hated ("if I didn't wake up tomorrow I would be okay with it"). His joke Plan B was Uber and stripping.
- He moved from Baltimore to Huntington Beach knowing no one and spent weekends learning to sell and market. He stopped watching the Ravens.
- He closed a sale while a client's kid drew on the wall in permanent marker: "be right or be rich" (credited to Zig Ziglar).
- First money from a stranger: a $200 check for an hour-long nutrition consult at a pizza place. At the time he was a state-record powerlifter.
- He had six gyms at 26, all funded from cash flow. The licensing business did $4M/month.
- A woman at a conference listed six reasons offers wouldn't work for her. When he said "you're right, you'll never make it work," she argued she would.
- He was reframed by writing a college psych essay from his immigrant mother's perspective, which ended their recurring fights.
