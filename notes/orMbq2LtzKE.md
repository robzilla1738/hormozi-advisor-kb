Speaker note: interview. Most of the story is from guest Mike Salguero (ButcherBox founder/CEO). Alex asks questions and adds commentary; lines are attributed below.

## Core thesis
ButcherBox reached about $550M/yr in revenue (the "$600M" in the title is rounded up; Mike says "it's really 550"). It was bootstrapped, profitable from day one, and Mike owns about 70%. It got there by pre-selling instead of raising money, chasing channel arbitrage (Kickstarter → influencers → Facebook), using "for life" offers that lower CAC and raise retention, and tracking dollars per box. Mike's earlier VC-funded company, CustomMade, is the counterexample: dilution, investor pressure and lost culture. Alex's point: most businesses don't need outside capital. "Have you considered just making money?"

## Frameworks & models
- **Switch sides of the table** (Alex, from a mentor): pretend you just bought your business. List what you'd fix, then fix those first.
- **Solving for zero** (Alex): build for "no time and profit." Businesses built to be low-time and profitable "scale a lot easier." Mike set out to build a hobby business: 1,000 subs × $20 profit.
- **Kill shot** (Mike): for each idea on the whiteboard, name the one thing that kills it if you can't solve it this week or month. For ButcherBox that was how to ship meat.
- **Who, not how** (Alex): find the person who already built that business, such as an ex-Omaha Steaks ops head whose non-compete had ended. You skip 15–20 years of learning.
- **Pre-sales as financing and a PMF test**: a Kickstarter, or Alex's gyms financed by multi-month pre-sold memberships and challenges. "If no one wants to buy your pre-sale… thank god you didn't raise money."
- **Arbitrage sequence**: find a platform that gives free reach and reverse-engineer how it hands that reach out. The Kickstarter "verified" badge came from early momentum.
- **For-life offers**: free bacon (about $4) in every box for as long as you stay subscribed. CAC fell by more than the bacon cost, and customers stayed longer because cancelling loses the perk. Offers stack, for example selling existing customers ground beef for life for $50. Later versions became time-limited ("for a year").
- **Trojan horse influencer outreach**: ask to feature the influencer's recipe in the box. That gets a reply, then you pitch. Pay in the currency they value, often fame over money.
- **Commissions for life**: $10–15 per subscriber per month for as long as the customer stays, with no upfront fees. The box is still profitable from the first shipment.
- **Dollars per box** (from the Omaha Steaks guy): everyone knows the fully loaded gross profit per box (meat, pick/pack, box, shipping, dry ice, card fees, tape). Target was $20 per box. Negotiate every line item.
- **DTC/CPG margin shape** (Alex): ~30% gross margin, of which ~10 points go to operations and ~20 points to marketing or profit. The playbook is to market for years, then cut marketing and live on word of mouth. ButcherBox runs about 30% gross and 6–6.5% net.
- **Own only the single point of failure**: everything is third-party, held to tight specs and quarterly business reviews ("the Toyota approach"). The only thing owned is dry ice (two plants), because without it no box ships.
- **Barbell hiring**: hire very young people (hungry to learn) and people out of retirement (who want to be useful). Avoid mid-career "careerists" focused on titles.
- **The $500M trap** (Mike): something breaks, you hire lots of managers, everyone starts saying no, and process crushes entrepreneurship. At the peak there were 230 people and 85 managers.
- **Square-root rule** (Alex, crediting Tom Bilyeu): about √N people create 50% of the value. That's an argument for one agency rather than four.
- **Free content retains paid subscribers** (Alex, labelled his opinion): few people log in, and people judge value as "has this brand given me $X this month?" without tracking what was paywalled. Public content gets shared, finds its audience through the algorithm and builds a warm retargeting pool. "Show don't sell."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Don't raise money for a normal business. VCs are paid to deploy capital through management fees, so their incentive is that you spend fast. Whoever holds the leverage decides.
- Value early equity sensibly. Mike valued ButcherBox at $750K and gave 10% each for one-time work (about $75K worth). He later bought those stakes back at a $48M valuation.
- Hire an expert when the math clears: across 60,000 lb of meat, a buyer only had to save $0.25/lb. The retired grocery-chain buyer found about $1.50.
- Money-back guarantee: refunds are "so small I don't even know the number." If you fear refunds, fix the product.
- Gross margin target is ≥30%. With 3% monthly churn on 430K customers, they need 12–15K new customers a month just to tread water.
- Referrals: a "nearly free" $20 trial box for the friend beat cash rewards for the referrer. Referred customers are better customers, and the program compounds.
- Outsource ad creative and buying when meetings slow you down. Speed of creative is the constraint.

## Playbook steps
1. Build the hobby or low-time version and identify the kill shot.
2. Recruit the person who has already built this business.
3. Pre-sell (Kickstarter) and use a stretch-goal bonus (free bacon at $100K) to turn buyers into promoters.
4. Ship fast, then phone every buyer. A $10/hr caller plus a $5 spiff converted about 40% to subscriptions.
5. Reverse-engineer a competitor's affiliate or influencer list with a VA. Mike used Thrive Market's partner URLs to get 400 leads. Alex did the same with CrossFit affiliates: 1,600 names became a lookalike audience that built Gym Launch.
6. Use dedicated email blasts with a deadline and an offer. Tweets and Facebook posts didn't work.
7. Add for-life offers and stack them.
8. Track dollars per box and negotiate every input.
9. Bring in experts at the right time and hold partners to specs with quarterly reviews.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Raising venture money on a "marketplace" story that wasn't a good business. At CustomMade, Mike ended with about 8%, a $500K/mo burn, and a friendly foreclosure.
- Firing the early team on investors' advice and never writing down core values.
- Giving away big equity at a throwaway valuation.
- Hiring a layer of middle managers during a crisis. ButcherBox went from about 85 people to 240.
- Overspending on share buybacks ($20–30M) just before inflation cut gross margin from 30% to 25%, even after two price increases.
- Pricing misaligned with input costs: $169 for six items the customer picks means ribeye-heavy boxes fall well under 30% GM.
- Trying to own everything in the supply chain.

## Examples / case details
- CustomMade was bought for $140K with a $2.5K deposit, using 90 days of due diligence to raise $500K. Revenue went from $35K/yr to $60K/mo. Rounds: $1.9M at $7M pre, $4M at $16M, and $18M at $30M ($10M equity, $4M debt, $4M secondary).
- Kickstarter: the goal was $25K. They raised $40K on day one and $210K in total, for about 1,100 boxes.
- The "bacon for everyone" tech bug became the core offer.
- Revenue by year: about $200K (Kickstarter), then $500K → $5M → $33M → $105M → $220M → $450M (COVID) → about $550–570M. Revenue fell in 2023 and grew about 6% the following year.
- Acquisition mix: about 30% referral, 30% Meta, 20% organic, and roughly 10% affiliates/influencers. They have shipped to 1.75M households, and NPS is 63.
- They bought Truffle Shuffle (live cooking classes) to fight churn caused by full freezers ("freezer as savings account").
- Employee liquidity comes from yearly buybacks at a 409A valuation plus a reverse Dutch auction.
