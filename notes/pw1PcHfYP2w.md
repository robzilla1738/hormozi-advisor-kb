## Core thesis
Info, service and course businesses that bolt on software hoping for (1) stickiness or (2) a tech exit multiple are making a "terrible idea." Software is sticky only when it's really good. Valuation comes from the risk-adjusted likelihood of future sales, not from being "tech." Buyers are smart and will check real usage. Stick to your game: make the service stickier, grow customers and TAM, and fix the reason people leave.

## Frameworks & models
- **Valuation = discounted future sales × likelihood they occur** (risk-adjusted return). Insurance gets good multiples because customers stay. A service business that never loses clients and expands spend also earns a high multiple. "Software is just a vehicle."
- **VC portfolio theory**: 100 bets, about 2 carry the portfolio. As one of the 100, your odds are tiny (he says roughly 1-in-50).
- **Product-market fit = Buy, Use, Tell**: customers buy it on its own, use it regularly on their own, and tell others specifically about the software.
- **"You're either service or you're tech. Period."** Being "tech-enabled" by automating one thing isn't tech.
- **Software exit reality**: most software fails; most that sell go "for parts"; the rare 10–50× topline multiples go to companies with tremendous growth, low churn and word of mouth.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Software churn is measured **annually**. Above ~10% annual churn, "you're just a service business." (Caption is garbled: "less than or above 10%." [inference] He means more than ~10%.)
- If users cancel the software when they cancel your service, it's just service.
- Instead of building, become an affiliate. Serious traffic can negotiate 20–50% affiliate commissions.
- The danger zone is around $1–3M in revenue, when founders "get cute."

## Playbook steps
1. Admit people leave because you're not fulfilling your promises. 2. Make the service stickier and raise retention and expansion spend. 3. Get more customers through affiliates, referrals, paid ads and outbound. 4. Expand TAM by serving more customer types. 5. Optionally acquire similar companies and roll them in. 6. Partner with best-in-class software as an affiliate rather than building it.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Splitting focus between the info business and a software side project, which means doing both "kind of shitty."
- Offshoring a $50K build and expecting a 20× topline multiple on a $2M-profit business.
- Disguising a mastermind as a "software subscription." Buyers check time on screen and daily users.
- Sunk cost and ego after announcing it publicly.
- Pitching an inferior product to your customers, which feels like losing integrity. Real software competitors can copy you instantly.
