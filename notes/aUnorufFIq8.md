# How to Get Anyone to Do Anything You Want
id: aUnorufFIq8 / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aUnorufFIq8 / duration: 1663s (~28 min) / tags: 07-hiring-management, 08-ops-scaling, 12-mindset-leverage, 05-sales-process, 03-pricing

## Core thesis
When someone doesn't do what you asked, diagnose it in a fixed order with the STAR-style five questions. Did they know you wanted it (write it down)? Did they know exactly what (define behaviors or outcomes)? Did they know how (train with document, demonstrate, duplicate)? Did they know by when (deadlines, with end of day as the default)? Did circumstances block them (judge their agency)? Only after those four come motivation and fit. The same questions apply to yourself as an owner, and the self-imposed "rules" owners hold as facts are usually what cap the business.

## Frameworks & models
- **The five questions (he calls it the "star system")**:
  1. **That**: did they know I wanted it? Fix: write it down. "If it isn't written, it never happened" (Acquisition.com saying). Memorialize with an email for a timestamp.
  2. **What**: define the request in behaviors or outcomes (format, printed vs email, etc.). Vague labels ("be less creepy," "harassing Courtney") mean different things to each party. "Clarity is high leverage work": 2–3 minutes of clarity saves 2–3 hours. He compares this to prompting AI.
  3. **How**: training. "Everything is trainable… not everything's worth training." Attitude ≈ 500 behaviors vs aptitude ≈ 20, which is why it's harder to train, not untrainable. Find the exact sub-step they can't do (e.g., 8 of 9 steps known; formatting missing).
  4. **When**: deadlines. Ask how long the actual work takes, then what they're doing between now and then. That reveals real-time workload and lets you reprioritize.
  5. **Circumstances/obstacles**: "no eggs for the omelette." Asked last because everyone blames it first. This is where you judge agency: "How hard would it have been to go get eggs? It's been 3 days."
  - Then **motivation**, which no one admits to lacking when asked directly. Motivation = temporarily increasing the perceived value of an outcome, the same thing as persuasion and sales.
- **Instruction specificity scales inversely with skill**: an expert can take "build a business around this"; a junior needs "write some emails about this." Chunk down until they can act.
- **Document → Demonstrate → Duplicate** training (Leads book, pp. 207–208): write the checklist; do it in front of them; they do it in front of you.
- **Default deadline = end of day**. He says an end-of-day org moves ~7× an end-of-week org and ~30× an end-of-month org. Doing in 2 hours what takes others 90 days is ~1,000× faster (math checks: 90 days ≈ 2,160 hrs ÷ 2 ≈ 1,080×).
- **"Driver" not "leader"**: leaders drive work ("Where is that? How can we do it faster? What can I pull off your plate?"). Culture = being potent enough to color the whole ocean; organizations regress to the mean otherwise (Bezos/Dawkins one-pager).
- **Reframe "no one can X like me" as a "how" question** ("I don't know how to find someone who can market like me"), which is solvable.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Run the questions in order: that → what → how → when → circumstances → motivation.
- If the blocker was real and more important, do it first, then this. If not reasonable, address agency.
- "Didn't think that was my job" → they probably shouldn't do anything else in your company either.
- Some skills should come "batteries included" (prerequisites). Don't train what isn't worth the time.
- If fundamental motivation isn't there, they don't need to work for you. Don't keep unemployable family employed.
- "If everyone's doing something, it's a great reason not to do it." Don't cap prices or pay at "industry standard" when the industry makes no money.
- Paying above market can capture the top talent (Henry Ford example).

## Playbook steps
1. Put every request in writing (email for a timestamp).
2. Specify the deliverable as behaviors/outcomes plus format.
3. If they lack the skill, find the missing sub-step; document, demonstrate, duplicate.
4. Ask "when," then "how long will the actual work take," then "what's between now and then"; default to end of day.
5. On a miss, ask the five questions neutrally. Probe whether the obstacle truly justified the delay.
6. For underperformers, use his framing: "compared to what I believe you're capable of… is it not knowing that, what, how, something blocking you, or motivation? Help me help you."
7. Motivate by changing the relative value of the outcome. Reframe boredom as a skill deficiency on the path to their stated goal.
8. Apply the same questions to your own stalled priorities.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Verbal-only requests and no follow-up (people infer it wasn't important).
- Vague labels instead of behaviors.
- Saying "this isn't trainable."
- Accepting "Monday" without checking the work estimate and workload.
- Opening with "you're subpar," which makes people defensive and feels like a character attack.
- Copying industry peers ("a little bit better for a little bit less") until you're a commoditized "nonprofit."
- Treating self-made rules as facts.
