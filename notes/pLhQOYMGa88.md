## Core thesis
Email is cheap follow-up on leads you've already paid to acquire. He cites a 35–45× average ROI, and one writer can reach 10M people. Treat every email as content: its job is to make a purchase more likely now or later. Reward each step (open → read → click), keep it plain-text and valuable so you stay out of the promo tab, and segment. Not emailing was "one of the biggest mistakes I made in my career." Acquisition.com sent just under 10M emails in 90 days and says email generates tens of millions across the portfolio.

## Frameworks & models
- **Reward every step (reinforcement loops)**: behavior repeats because it was rewarded last time. The subject earns the open. The top of the email (a quote or tweet) rewards the open within a glance. The meat (one tactic, under ~200 words / ~1 minute) rewards the read. The CTA and PS reward the click. A good email raises the odds of the next open. One bad email out of five can kill the habit.
- **Mozy Money Minute template**: branded recurring subject ("podcast episode" style, building brand over split-testing) → a proven tweet/quote → one problem-or-condition + solution tactic (<200 words) → contextual CTA (scaling → workshops, starting → Skool) → PS, which is a meme or the CTA. Same format every time ("McDonald's: no surprises").
- **Unsubscribes are fine / "shaking the tree"**: make unsubscribing easy. Dormant subscribers hurt deliverability more than unsubscribes do. The first email after a long silence pulls churn forward (his first was just over 1%; now about 0.42% per email). Workshop attendees are often people who unsubscribed.
- **Segmentation**: HubSpot cited a 760%+ ROI lift (transcript says "791"). Use opt-in qualifiers (revenue bands, business/no business) to send only relevant content. "Way less about how good the email is... so much more about who's getting it."
- **Deliverability = align with the platform's objective, not hacks** ("100 golden BBs"): look like a normal email (plain text, no images, 1–2 links), remove money language, ask for replies, and earn opens and clicks. He cites ~17% of B2B marketing emails never reaching the inbox. Google-native links (YouTube, Drive-hosted sales letter or VSL) improve deliverability and CTR.
- **Opt-in welcome email**: build anticipation (a big announcement coming), deliver the promise, sell them on staying subscribed (surprise and delight before the promised item), and require "reply YES" to get the bonus chapter. The reply trains the inbox. Platform-specific bonuses (peeled-off book chapters) give a reason to subscribe.
- **Annual renewal fee** (from one email's tactic): a one-time yearly fee of about 1–3 months' price on top of monthly. It's charged a year later, so front-end conversion isn't hurt, and it adds 8–24% revenue at no cost. Example: $39/mo + $99 annual → $468 → $567 (+21%), an effective $47/mo without advertising $47. At a 20% margin, +20% revenue ≈ doubles profit.
- **Speed to contact** (from another email's tactic): call leads within 60 seconds of opt-in. He cites HBR's 391% increase in sales. If nothing else could quadruple sales, make it the first priority. The extra sales pay for the staff.
- **Testimonial hooks** (from an email): across ~2,500 Gym Launch testimonials, the top 4 all had a pain-based hook ("two months away from shutting our doors," "finished the month with $0 of profit"). Ask "What was your worst moment?" rather than "how was life before?" "Moments make hooks, details make them powerful."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Cadence: the best email marketers he talked to suggest about 3×/week. More email → more conversion if quality holds. If you haven't started, start once a month. He prefers a quality threshold over a committed cadence ("two good ones in me, two go out").
- Batch it: he wrote 24 emails (2/month for a year) in about half a day.
- No separate promotional emails needed: make every email valuable with a "link in bio" CTA, so there's no give/ask ratio to manage.
- CTAs: 1–2 is fine if each has a clear audience or objective. Five is aggressive. Make the CTA contextual to the content, not copy-pasted.
- Track CTR over open rate (Apple privacy distorts opens). Optimize CTR across all emails, not per email ("deposit before you withdraw").
- Their benchmarks over 3 months: 35.7% open, 8.5% CTR (8,500 clicks per 100K sent).
- Blogs: put a nugget in the email and link to the full post to train clicking.
- Preview text: write it yourself (~24% of people check it before opening). Otherwise it pulls the first ~150 characters.
- A/B test subject lines on a small slice, then send the winner to the rest.
- Timing: B2C does better Mon/Tue (~18% open lift), B2B on Wed (via Neil Patel). Time of day 10–12 and 1–3; early morning is competitive. Matters less across time zones.
- Optimize for mobile (~58–60% of traffic), especially image-heavy e-com emails.

## Playbook steps
1. Start now, even after a long gap. Expect a one-time unsubscribe spike.
2. Add qualifiers to the opt-in and segment the list.
3. Build a repeatable template: branded subject → instant reward → one tactic <200 words → contextual CTA → PS.
4. Plain text, 1–2 links, minimal money language, custom preview text, mobile check.
5. Welcome email: anticipation, deliver the promise, sell staying subscribed, reply-YES for the bonus.
6. Batch-write a year's minimum (12–24), then add more when inspired.
7. Track CTR over time and A/B test subjects. Send B2C Mon/Tue, B2B Wed, late morning or early afternoon.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Not emailing at all (Gym Launch didn't email until 2–3 years in: "saying no to money"). Skool had no email follow-up for a while; adding it gave a 40% conversion lift.
- Making unsubscribing hard. Sending to dormant segments for vanity metrics.
- "Vegas Strip" emails full of images, links and money talk → promo tab ("email death").
- A lead magnet without selling continued communication, or a lead magnet that isn't worth the effort ("keeps me up at night").
- Copy-paste, non-contextual CTAs (he critiques one of his own).
- Fear of unsubscribes. The logical extreme is never emailing, which defeats the point of having the list.
