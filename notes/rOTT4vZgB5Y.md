## Core thesis
Rotating, constrained variety (as at Crumbl, Froyo/Yogurtland, Oreo limited flavors, a protein bar brand) extends LTV for high-frequency consumables. People love novelty, and more options mean more total consumption. Keep a few core staples, and rotate a small set of new items on the customer's consumption cycle.

## Frameworks & models
- **Staples + rotating variety box**: keep consistency seekers with the staples and capture novelty seekers with the rotation.
- **When variety works (checklist)**: 1) short consumption cycle (high-velocity, many-times-a-year, impulse purchases: food, drinks, apparel drops); 2) operational efficiency to deliver it (a few shared base ingredients, e.g. butter, sugar, flour; or vertically integrated manufacturing where only the filling changes); 3) low margin of error (hard to ruin a cookie, versus a hoodie you'll never wear).
- **Constrain variety ("jam effect")**: offer few enough that a customer can try them all and still keep their staple; too many becomes overwhelming, and missed drops stop feeling cool.
- **Mystery/surprise boxes**: skeptical, because value then depends on the surprise rather than the product.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Match the rotation cadence to the purchase cycle (weekly buyers → weekly rotation).
- About 4–8 rotating items alongside the staples (e.g. Crumbl: 5 of 6 cookies new weekly, sold in one box; Froyo: 6 of 8 flavors new weekly, as stated).
- Not for foundational, consistency-driven purchases (breakfast, underwear) or for services like agencies.
- Customers don't care about your operational complexity; they care about what they buy.

## Playbook steps
1. Check that you have a short consumption cycle and operational capacity. 2. Define the core staples. 3. Rotate a constrained set on the customer's cycle. 4. Bundle variety into one purchase (a box).

## Mistakes he warns against
- Endless variety (choice overload).
- Avoiding rotation just because it adds complexity.

## Examples / case details
- Opportunity ideas: an ice cream chain rotating all flavors except staples; a brewery with 2 house beers plus ~6 rotating weekly (people try all 6 but only ~3 of 20); pizza with staples plus 4 wild flavors.
- One speaker tries every flavor of a brand, then moves to another brand's new flavors rather than returning to a favorite.
