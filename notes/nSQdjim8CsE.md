## Core thesis
A **money model** is "a series of offers deliberately structured to generate the most upfront cash, get the most leads together, and get the highest lifetime value," and it speeds up when the cash comes in. Most businesses play on "hard mode": they spend to get a customer and hope to earn it back later. The cheat code is to make more from a customer in the first 30 days than it cost to get them. Then acquisition cash stops being the constraint, you can outspend competitors, and you're protected against ad costs, which "will only go one way, which is up." This is the launch promo for $100M Money Models (launch event Aug 16), with a lot of event and pre-order pitching.

## Frameworks & models
- **Four offer types**:
  1. **Attraction**: get the most leads to buy, often with up-front cash.
  2. **Upsell**: increase gross profit per customer.
  3. **Downsell**: turn no's into yes's. It's risky: done wrong, buyers of the expensive option switch to the cheap one. Present it so you keep every top-tier buyer and only pick up people who would have said no.
  4. **Continuity**: repeat purchases. "We don't get customers to make sales. We make sales to get customers."
  The book has 5 attraction offers; mix and match the "Lego blocks."
- **The 30-day test**: if gross profit from a customer covers the cost to acquire them, the cost to serve them, and the cost to acquire and serve the next customer, you can spend as much as you want.
- **Different wallets**: a customer who spent their membership money still has separate supplement, apparel and fitness-food budgets.
- **Point of peak motivation**: when they walk in the door, they're most deprived of the outcome. Sell the bigger ticket then.
- **Reason-why "lubricant"** between offers: e.g. "credit your $500 toward the year" instead of a bare "want to buy the next thing?"
- **Auction of attention**: everyone selling to the same avatar (gym owners: equipment, supplements, agencies, construction, apparel) bids for the same eyeballs. Whoever makes the most per customer can outspend the rest and push up CPMs, an "ethical and legal monopoly" of attention.
- **The book system**: Offers (something people don't price-compare) → Leads (content, outreach, paid ads, referrals, affiliates) → Money Models (connects them: ads go in one end and money comes out the other). They are additive and multiplicative like pipe throughput, not dependent like a car's parts. They started as one 2021 textbook, "Lead Generation and Monetization Structures."
- **Business is endurance**: you only need cash to stay in business; money models pull cash forward so you outlast.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If CAC exceeds what the first purchase pays (e.g. a $99/mo membership with ~$500 CAC means about 5 months negative), your options are savings, debt, investors or going broke. Fix the money model instead.
- Customers are "fractal 80/20": about 20% will buy something significantly more expensive.
- Don't hesitate to sell more 2 days after a purchase or before a trial ends; "they're going to buy them either way."
- Free front-end offers usually get leads at about half the cost (he still uses the same cost in his worst-case example).

## Playbook steps
1. Attraction: a free (or "win your money back") 6-week challenge, sold at $500–600 at the door. 2. Classic upsell ("you can't buy X without Y"): supplements in the first 48 hours. 3. Rollover upsell + continuity: credit the $500 toward an annual membership. 4. Buy X get Y: a pay-in-full discount ("buy 10 months, get 2 free" / 10–20% off) at week 6. 5. Keep selling seasonal challenges (Lean by Halloween, Slim for Santa), semi-private training and more supplements.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Funding acquisition from savings or debt instead of fixing your unit economics.
- Moving from a trial to continuity with no mechanism ("no lubricant").
- Selling a commodity membership instead of a transformation offer (grocery tours, a daily accountability coach, a peer group, prizes, pre-approved meals, supplements: flip each objection into part of the offer).
- Downsells that cannibalize top-tier buyers.

## Examples / case details
- **Origin story**: CAC rose from about $100 to $500 after an algorithm change. A college acquaintance said his two supplement stores made $80K and $130K a month. Alex started selling a few hundred dollars of supplements in the first 48 hours, which let him run ads again and open new locations.
- **Competitor LBO math (100 leads at $20 = $2K)**: 25% buy the $21 trial = $525; ~1/3 (8) convert at $99 = ~$800; ~5 months more ≈ $4K. Total about $5,325, so at most ~$53 per lead.
- **His model (same $2K, worse 15% conversion)**: 15 × $500 = $7,500 up front; 12 buy ~$200 of supplements = $2,400 at ~80% gross margin ≈ $2K; ~70% (10) roll over at ~$200/mo (after crediting the $500) ≈ $10K over 5 months; ~20% prepay ≈ $5K. Total about $24,500, so up to ~$245 per lead, "5×" the competitor ([inference] ≈ 4.6×). He says everything except the $5K came within 30 days and that it returned "$8–9 for every dollar"; [inference] the $10K membership figure is spread over 5 months, so that timing claim is loose.
- **Gym Launch** won with a better money model at two levels: it gave gyms one, and its own business beat the other vendors selling to gym owners.
