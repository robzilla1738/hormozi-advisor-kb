# How to get what you want every time

## Core thesis
"You get what you negotiate, not what you deserve." Five negotiation tactics he learned from mentors and real deals: (1) BATNA, built before you sit down; (2) anchoring, including the size of your concessions; (3) multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESOs); (4) reciprocity, traded across many unbundled variables; (5) framing (investment vs cost). They apply in three contexts: employees, vendors/customers, and partners (M&A, investment). The video title promises "every time"; it's really a negotiation playbook.

## Frameworks & models
- **BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement)**, learned from Chiron via HBS: the greatest source of psychological power, built before the table. Only accept deals better than your alternative. People who know their alternatives aim higher and open more aggressively (he cites a London Business School study). "Sell when you don't want to sell; buy when you don't want to buy." With a strong BATNA you can either show it ("beat it or no worries") or keep it private and swing higher. Showing it tends to invite only a marginal beat.
- **Anchoring**: the first number sets the frame (Kahneman). Anchor high to get "the gasp"; it also resets the increments people think in ($100K quote → $20K increments). Counter-anchoring is the weaker fallback. **Anchor your concessions too**: small moves signal little room to move.
- **Make them bid against themselves**: when a vendor messes up, ask what it would cost them to fix it, then use that number as the discount (the front-desk nick: "$1,500… sounds like a pretty good place to start for a discount").
- **MESOs**: present 2–3 different but equivalent offers (e.g., lower monthly fee with a longer commitment / higher fee plus premium support / pay-as-you-go with flexibility). Their choice reveals their priorities, works like a reverse assumed close, and builds reciprocity. On the receiving side, combine the best parts into an "option D," or ask them for 2–3 versions. Negotiation is positive-sum when each side gives what matters less to it.
- **Reciprocity across unbundled variables**: unbundle the deal into as many variables as possible (cash vs financing, 30- vs 90-day close, furniture, speed, risk, ease, i.e., the value-equation levers). Trade non-price concessions while holding price. He keeps a deal sheet of ~80 deal variables. Reciprocity only works in cultures that value it, and concessions must be valued fairly (lunch ≠ an airport ride).
- **Framing**: sellers (employee or vendor) frame themselves as an investment with a return; buyers reframe it as cost/overhead. "For a $5,000 investment you can see $15,000 in maintenance savings." Back it with data, e.g., pool or awning resale comps: "If a $100K pool adds $100K of value, the pool's free"; if it adds $50K, frame it as half off.
- **Post-deal reassurance**: after accepting a first offer, say "I wouldn't have done it for a penny more/less" so the other side doesn't feel they left money on the table.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Never take the first offer. Get 4–6 vendor bids, which also teach you the terms others include.
- Employees: negotiate with another offer in hand, but sparingly, because it burns goodwill.
- Vendors: your BATNA is your other customers (demand > supply).
- Needing the deal with no alternatives is "winning poker only on bluffing." You want pocket aces.
- Anchor high on the initial offer and low on the counters.

## Playbook steps
1. Before negotiating, create real alternatives (other offers, bids, buyers).
2. Open with an ambitious anchor.
3. Counter in small increments while adding terms.
4. Offer 2–3 equivalent structures and read which one they prefer.
5. List every variable (price, speed, risk, ease, payment, timing, extras) and concede on low-cost ones one at a time.
6. Frame your ask as an ROI, backed by comparable data.
7. Close graciously ("wouldn't have done it for less").

## Mistakes he warns against
- Assuming negotiation is zero-sum.
- Fixating on price instead of terms.
- Saying "I'm not trying to anchor" (it's still an anchor).
- Playing every card at once.
- Accepting the other side's frame without noticing.
