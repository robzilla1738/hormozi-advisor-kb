## Core thesis
Most businesses are linear: founder/investor effort and money → customers → results, and then the value leaks out, so you have to keep putting more in. Build a self-reinforcing loop instead: customers → results → reviews/word of mouth → more customers. Four tactics make that loop run: a surprise gift plus a third-party ask, merch for reviews, a point-of-sale discount, and unlockables. "You are lighter fluid, not the log."

## Frameworks & models
- **Linear vs self-reinforcing growth loop**: effort/money → customers → results → reviews & WOM → customers.
- **Tactic 1: Surprise gift + third-party ask**: after delivery, give a gift sized to spend (~$20 → mints; $100 → $5 dessert; $1,000 → ~$50 gift). Then someone *other* than the giver (e.g., a manager) asks: "if you leave a review mentioning them, our staff member gets $X." This incentivizes the employee, not the customer, and the customer reciprocates to the person who helped them. The further the asker is from the owner, the more compelling the ask, he believes. (Must be legal where you are.)
- **Tactic 2: Merch for reviews**: $5 shirt in exchange for Yelp + Google + Meta reviews + a check-in, covering every surface where prospects find you (search, SEO/PPC, ads) plus organic WOM from people wearing it. If the brand is weak, put an inspirational saying on the front and the logo on the sleeve/back. Best for B2C and recurring membership businesses.
- **Tactic 3: Discount at point of sale** ("removing bad stuff"): present the price → get acceptance → offer the discount → they agree → verify the review → recalculate → hand over the final check. Size it to gross profit and average ticket.
- **Tactic 4: Unlockables** (best for B2B, digital, national): a review unlocks media/training, event entry, or a **VIP trial of a higher tier** (e.g., 30 days). The marginal cost is low, and the trial can upsell, so "you literally get paid to get reviews."
- **Meta-model**: gift = add good via reciprocity; merch = direct quid pro quo; discount = remove bad.
- **Make it easy**: QR code on a table tent next to the check; rotate the destination over time (Yelp → Google → Meta → Instagram follow → SMS list).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Pay for reviews indirectly, in proportion to what a review is worth. Early reviews are worth far more (1st vs 10,000th), but keep the system running regardless.
- Volume of reviews beats a slightly higher rating: 4.6★ with ~1,000 reviews beat 5★ with ~20. He says the business with the most reviews may get ~90% of search volume.
- Immediacy beats size: "latency is one of the strongest multipliers"; risk and delay work alike in people's minds. "$2 off today" beats "$100 in a year"; don't offer "20% off your next visit."
- Higher-stakes purchases depend more on referrals; lower-ticket ones still convert on proof.
- Track the review rate per employee as a revenue-generating customer satisfaction metric; find the outlier and teach their method to everyone else.

## Playbook steps
1. Deliver the core service. 2. Give a surprise gift proportional to spend. 3. A manager/third party makes the ask, with the staff bonus tied to the review (naming the employee). 4. Put a QR code by the check/invoice. 5. Optionally trade merch for multi-platform reviews, or offer an instant discount verified at checkout. 6. For online/B2B, unlock training/events/higher-tier trials. 7. Display reviews everywhere (e.g., a lobby covered floor to ceiling). 8. Measure the review % per staff member and copy the best performer.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Running a linear business that stalls when founder input stops.
- The server asking for the review themself works, but it's "so much cleaner" for the manager to ask.
- Delayed incentives (next-visit discounts).
- Not displaying the reviews you earn.
- Assuming the manager ask takes too much time: about 20 seconds per table.
