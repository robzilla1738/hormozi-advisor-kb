## Core thesis
Growth comes from focus and from executing the basics better, not from new ideas. After product-market fit, "the new needs to end for a while." Fix the holes in the bucket, then add more of what works, and only then add new things. Spreading yourself across several businesses lets each one decay while focused competitors compound. Business isn't complicated. It's hard because people can't stay focused on one thing for long.

## Frameworks & models
- **Woman in the red dress** (The Matrix): distractions and shiny opportunities. She comes back at every level, "hotter and hotter." You say no to 1,000, then 10,000, then 100,000 opportunities.
- **Better → More → New**: better is the lowest-risk move; more is riskier; new is the riskiest. Don't take on more or new until better is maxed out.
- **Business as a bucket**: water in the top is traffic. First fix the holes, then increase the flow, then add new flows. Never add buckets while the first one leaks.
- **Magic wand question**: "If you could eliminate all businesses but one, how easy would it be to grow that one?"
- **Need-to-believes**: what must stay true over the long horizon (people always want things fast, a good product, good service)? Bet on what won't change.
- **Learned-wrong-lesson trap**: starting something new got rewarded early, so founders keep starting new things. After PMF, starting new things gets punished. Unlearn it.
- **"Two fat guys in the middle, run right, consistent yardage"**: advice from a mercenary PE-placed CEO (third time as CEO) whom bankers introduced him to during two company sales. "Don't be cute."
- **Paying down ignorance**: if someone in your industry makes 100× what you do, they know it better. Split focus divides how fast you pay down ignorance by four.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you're not doing the basics (on every platform, regular email, SEO built out, sales training), don't start a new business.
- Make a list of the top ~25 obvious things that would make you more money (more email, outreach, content, sales training, call reviews, onboarding, support cadence). Finish it before doing anything new.
- After PMF, scale "more": 1 → 5 pieces of content a day, 1 → 5 salespeople, 1 → 5 editors.
- Strategy is easy and execution is hard. Execution risk is the bigger risk.
- Boring markets (dry cleaning, lawn care, buildings) aren't contested by the smartest people, who are in Silicon Valley.
- "Being advanced means you never don't do the basics." Big businesses do the same things as small ones, better.

## Playbook steps
1. Pick one business; use the magic wand test and shut down the rest. 2. List the obvious undone basics and do them all (better). 3. Scale the inputs that work (more). 4. Only then add new flows. 5. Check need-to-believes. 6. Commit over a long horizon and "start the compounding clock."

## Mistakes he warns against
- "I'll do them all and see which takes off." None will, because any one of them could if you focused.
- Calling distraction "vertical integration."
- Diverting resources from the core, so it atrophies while media and talent costs rise.
- Doing it only for income, so you keep chasing the next income source instead of a mission.
- The arrogance of thinking you can outcompete focused rivals while split five ways.

## Examples / case details
- A friend whose wife is popular had ~60 businesses tracked in Excel, making about 8 figures total. On the magic wand question he said he could grow one to $30–50M. It "messed with him for a year," and he shut down business after business.
- A high school acquaintance with roofing, flipping, GC and windows at ~$4M/yr: "You're not vertically integrated, you're just distracted." The biggest roofing companies are multi-billion-dollar.
- Ben Francis started Gymshark at ~17, has run it 10–11 years, and it's now a billion-dollar business. Zuckerberg had no side hustles. Alex had 9 businesses before Gym Launch took off.
- Example of good execution: 10 reps × 200 calls = 2,000 calls/day. The work is recruiting, training, incentives, call reviews and leaderboards.
- The focused lawn care owner (a story about his dad) vs the guy doing drop shipping, lawn care, consulting and NFTs.
