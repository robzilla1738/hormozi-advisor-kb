# Why I Don't Go Home for the Holidays
id: OvEfWrDOfNk / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OvEfWrDOfNk / duration: 934s (~16 min) / tags: 12-mindset-leverage
Signal: low

## Core thesis
This is a personal and mindset video, not a business one. He left home to become a different person. Going back often pulls you into your old identity, because people "meet you in your past" and treat you the way you used to accept. If your family and old friends don't support the behaviors your goals need, you have permission to skip the holidays, or to set conditions. He says he is even less tolerant now than in his original video 4–5 years ago. If you love going home, he says go.

## Frameworks & models
- **Conducive test**: What behaviors do I need for my goals? Do these people and places support those behaviors or take away from them? If you're less likely to keep up your committed behaviors there, the environment is a detractor.
- **Reject the frame, don't negotiate it**: saying "please don't say that, I'm working on it" still accepts their label. Answer as the person you're becoming: "I might have in the past. I don't now."
- **You teach people how to treat you**: you can't control how they speak to you, only how you respond. Don't respond to commands like "get over here." Respond to respectful asks.
- **Reward vs punishment memory asymmetry**: the memory of punishment fades faster than the memory of reward. Time away makes you remember the good and forget the bad, which is why you go back and get reminded why you left. Examples: the hangover ("never drinking again") and nostalgia for an old relationship. Remind yourself on purpose why you left.
- **Play it out**: if you don't go, either they forget you're gone (fine) or they talk trash about you the whole time (so you aren't missing anything). Not going has no downside either way.
- **Tell the truth** as the "litmus test" for handling hard conversations: made-up excuses get overcome because they're BS.
- **Rest is productive if it nets a gain**: if you have to recover from your holidays, you lost their main benefit.
- **"Be the rock"** (attributed to Epictetus): "I understand. Not going to change my behavior."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you're on a streak of new habits (punctuality, consistency, drinking less, a work grind), "don't break it. It's not worth the trade-off."
- If some people there are worth seeing, go but avoid the others. Don't answer their texts, and tell them plainly you don't want to.
- Conditional attendance: "I'll come on the condition I'm not going to talk to so-and-so," cleared with the host first and told to that person up front.
- Still feel obligated? Send money for presents.
- Being home doesn't give everyone free access to your time. Saying no to going out is fine.
- Language to use: "I'm not saying this is forever. I'm just saying this is for now."

## Playbook steps
1. Run the conducive test on the people and places back home. 2. Decide whether to go, go with conditions, or skip ("for now"). 3. Give the true reason, not an excuse. 4. If you go, reject old labels by speaking as your new identity, and only respond to respectful requests. 5. Protect your leisure and rest. 6. Before deciding, remind yourself why you left.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Going because you fear what people will say.
- Accepting labels like "you know how you are" or "you have such a short temper," even passively.
- Making up excuses instead of telling the truth.
- Coming back from the holidays needing 1–2 weeks to shed bad habits. He counts 3–4 weeks lost as about 8% of the year, and his math checks out (4/52 ≈ 7.7%).

## Examples / case details
- "Get over here" from the couch: "Don't talk to me that way. I'm not your dog."
- The temper exchange: "Would you prefer that I be angry?… Are you here to help me or to get me to behave the way you want me to?"
- "Uncle John, there's 38 people here… talk to any of the other 37 but me."
- Wealthy people keep "contained and pristine" environments. He cites the teams around Musk, Bezos and Jobs as intolerant of low standards.
