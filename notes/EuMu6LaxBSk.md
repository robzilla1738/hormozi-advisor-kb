# Everything We Learned From Making $106 Million in 72 Hours

**Speaker note:** Alex is NOT a speaker. This is a conversation between Leila Hormozi and an Acquisition.com executive (captioned "Chiron"/"Tron", probably Sharran) after the book launch. Alex is only talked about.

## Core thesis
The $106M in 72 hours (a Guinness record for a non-fiction book launch) was "a 10 plus year thing" that came together at the end. They frame it as "a $106 million exit for work done over 10 years." What made it work: one owner per piece, everything written down, a fully built company team pointed at one goal, and obsessive risk removal through redundancies. After the win: dissect success the way you dissect failure, step on the gas, and redeploy the cash so it makes the next $100M without another launch.

## Frameworks & models
- **Top 3 operational requirements:** (1) someone who "wakes up every morning and own[s] it"; (2) "nothing great ever happened without it being written down" (2–3 dozen offer-design memos and a very large sales support manual); (3) existing teams (sales, CS, media, events, AV, design, legal, tech, finance) that could all be pointed at the launch.
- **Repeated topic = under-resourced:** when the founders keep talking about the same pain, it means no one owns it.
- **Redundancies on redundancies:** a one-time event can't recover from a 60/100 score. They planned for everything that could kill it: a second generator, a second Wi-Fi line plus Starlink, every traffic channel instead of only paid ads, and load tests for 100k+ people hitting the landing page.
- **3 reasons for objections:** value prop not good enough, sales process not clear enough, brand not big enough. Fix all three and you can sell an $18,000 back-end offer as a one-click upsell with no sales call. They never said "buy" in 33 hours of livestream.
- **Fresh eyes:** new hires get about 2 weeks of fresh eyes. Have them keep a doc and book a review 2 weeks out. You can also manufacture fresh eyes after a break (a 3-day internal audit) or right after a big event (they surveyed the team the day after the launch). Capture both what to fix and what worked, so you can do it again.
- **Goal → Plan → Behavior** (for team buy-in): a goal with no plan sounds crazy. Show the plan ("WFM, write a freaking memo," using the why/what/how/now structure), then live it. Example: Alex was in the building at 3:45–4am for 90+ days.
- **Three buy-in camps:** believers, doubters, and people waiting for evidence. Don't try to persuade the fixed camps. Ask everyone to "disagree and commit" and do their job at 10/10: "you don't need to think it's going to happen... you just need to act like it could."
- **Hilo to Humvey** (a Navy SEAL story): they rehearsed only the helicopter-to-Humvey transition for 3 days. For any big goal, ask what the one behavior is that you need to practice until it's precise. Related ideas: isolating the sticking point in a powerlifting lift, and practicing only chord switches on guitar.
- **Constraint focus:** put every resource on the current constraint. Solving it creates a new one ("Welcome to business").
- **Goal sizing (Leila):** first figure out whether you undershoot (you always hit your goals) or overshoot. Take your first goal and pressure-test it at 3× and 10× bigger or faster. Her threshold: take the shot if the odds are at least ~35%, and if they're lower, work to raise them to 35%. Plan from math, not emotions.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Every important initiative needs a single owner who thinks about it daily.
- For one-shot events, spend heavily on backups for the backups.
- A high price doesn't automatically require a salesperson if value, process clarity and brand are strong.
- Take a hard conversation within 24 hours at most.
- Frame difficult or performance conversations around the employee's own goal ("I know you want to be CFO someday"), then show the gap and offer to coach them.
- Don't state a mission bigger than your plan (the example was "10 million people" for a local gym). Raise the goal as your ability to plan for it grows.
- Big goals motivate more than comp plans: "you can't fix a comp plan if you have no goals."
- Windfall: normalize it to your own scale. You wouldn't quit over a one-time $106K, so reinvest it, tax-efficiently, so it repeats without another launch.
- After a win, pull hiring forward (they pulled 6–12 months of planned hires forward).

## Playbook steps
1. Set the goal: 3×/10× pressure test, ≥35% odds, check the math.
2. Write the plan as memos and assign one owner per piece.
3. List everything that could kill it and add layered redundancies.
4. Find the "hilo to Humvey" behaviors and rehearse them (delivery, transitions, load tests).
5. Show the behavior yourself and ask for disagree-and-commit.
6. Afterward, survey the team while they have fresh eyes, dissect what went well and what went badly, then step on the gas and redeploy the capital.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Believing it was a one-weekend thing.
- Thinking one backup is enough.
- Relying only on paid ads for traffic.
- Treating success as an end point: not dissecting it and easing off the gas ("nothing fails like success"; "when it feels easy is when you step on the gas").
- Announcing a big goal "cold" right after a seminar without a plan.
- Trying to win over every doubter.
- Fixing comp instead of setting goals.
- Assuming a windfall means you can retire or "buy a few houses."

## Examples / case details
- $106M in 3 days. The previous biggest they'd seen was about $40M. 33 hours live in 72 hours. Teams worked until 1–2am.
- An $18K back-end offer was sold as a one-click upsell.
- The traffic team's only job was getting the right people to the stream; delivery was the media team's and Alex's job.
- Gym Launch's early days: $350K/month with two people and one EA, then $550K the next month.
- Diligence on a company with a "help 50 million women" goal and no plan: "The goal is not the plan."
- Leila's meditation (40 min a day, no noticeable effect until about week 5) was used as an example of boring reps.
