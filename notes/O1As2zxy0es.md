## Core thesis
A service business (renting out labor/knowledge at a profit) can be packaged into exactly four vehicles: private chain, franchise, licensing, or software/tech-enabled service. Each trades off cash flow, upfront cost, risk, operational drag, and enterprise value. Pick the vehicle based on build-out cost vs. profit per location, how protectable the IP is, cash needs, and risk appetite. Prompted by a single-location salon owner asking how to scale. Alex says he owns businesses in three of the four.

## Frameworks & models
- **The 4 vehicles**:
  1. **Private chain**: open more locations yourself; keep all ownership, take all risk, front all capital, hire all labor, control the brand. Highest per-location cost (build-out, lease negotiation and lease risk, staffing). EV very good.
  2. **Franchise**: outside investors buy in to own units; you take royalties off the top line. Legally = **name + business systems + fee** (all three).
  3. **Licensing**: typically **two of the three** (e.g. CrossFit = name + fee, no systems; white-label = systems + fee, no name). Licensing with all three = an illegal franchise (fines, shutdown).
  4. **Software / tech-enabled service**: only if software can actually do the service (can't replace a haircut).
- **Pros/cons matrix** (from transcript):
  - Private chain: high cost per location; high EV; you keep all revenue.
  - Franchise: ~$750K to set up (legal/filings); 1–2 years to profit; very litigious; upfront fee (~$50K) usually just covers franchisee acquisition cost (brokers/leads), so money comes from royalties after opening; high EV but the multiple applies only to corporate franchise revenue (franchisees own most of the profit).
  - Licensing: almost no cost, super high margins, cash flow from day one, speed to grab market share; not defensible, low EV/low multiples unless licensing agreements are very sticky.
  - Software: big cost, lots of time, low cash flow; usually the highest EV at exit, but only with ≥80% yearly retention.
- **Productization lever**: lower variability and skill requirements of labor → scalable (Subway: anyone can make the same sandwich with the same recipe/process).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- **Private chain** if a new location costs less than ~6 months of a location's profit (example: <$40–50K build-out vs ~$400K/yr profit), the service needs little specialized skill, and you're confident you'll dominate each market.
- **Franchise** if build-out is very expensive (McDonald's ~ $1M), so capital risk gets pushed to franchisees. Need 100+ locations for it to make real money. 90–95% of franchises never pass 100.
- **Licensing** when you're newer, cash-poor, need cash flow now, and the IP/offer is easy to copy anyway, so speed wins. You accept a smaller exit.
- Licensing exit: need roughly 80%+ (the "plus" is garbled in captions; 80% implied by the parallel software line [inference]) yearly licensee retention to reach a ~6+ exit multiple.
- **Software**: must hit ≥80% yearly customer retention, or buyers value it as a normal service with some tech, and you wasted the money and time.
- Don't build software without an in-house software co-founder (code, UX, engineering teams). Never outsource it to a third-party dev team.
- The investor lens for choosing: IP/value, cash flow required, upfront investment, time to go, entrepreneur's risk appetite, how structurally sound the return is.

## Playbook steps
1. Confirm the single-unit model is profitable. 2. Measure build-out cost vs. annual profit per location. 3. Assess skill requirement/variability; productize to lower it. 4. Assess IP defensibility and your cash and risk appetite. 5. Pick: cheap to open + sure win → private chain; expensive to open + sure win → franchise; costs nothing + copyable IP → licensing for speed and cash; software-replaceable + tech co-founder + 80% retention possible → software.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Treating franchising as the end goal. "It is the beginning of the next journey."
- Outsourcing software development without a technical co-founder ("$2–3M of development later… let me give you a three million dollar gift: don't do it").
- Giving away revenue and trade secrets via licensing when locations are cheap to open and very profitable.
- Licensing all three franchise elements (illegal franchise).
- Highly specialized, inconsistent service in a chain.

## Examples / case details
- Insomnia Cookies: ~800 sq ft locations, oven + window + delivery. Cheap build, huge margin, so own them all. Chanel/LV/Dior owned their stores (product, not service, noted by him).
- Acquisition.com portfolio company moved from licensing to private chain because licensees made so much money and openings were cheap: $0.5M → $12M/yr in 14 months; 12 locations, opening one per month (niche undisclosed).
- Gym Launch (his first business) = licensing, chosen for cash flow with no capital. Offer-driven, easy to copy, so speed.
- Portfolio ~$85M/yr revenue at time of video.
