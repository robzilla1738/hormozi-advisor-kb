## Core thesis
A live workshop of "12 rules of thumb" (in practice about 11; numbering skips around), each one a ratio between two paired numbers. They let you check whether a part of the business is a problem to fix or just something to manage. Covers price vs close rate, LTV:CAC by number of humans in the loop, Rule of 100, lead response time, sales calendar utilization, 30-day payback, gross margin, 30-day cash collected, retention, prepay/financing/layaway rates, funnel conversion benchmarks, and ignoring industry averages. Credibility claims: 14 years in business, portfolio just over $250M/yr, book launch that did $106M in sales.

## Frameworks & models
- **Price ↔ close-rate ladder** (assisted sales, phone/Zoom/in person): ≥80% close → underpriced 3–4×; 60–80% → 2–3×; 50–60% → 1.5–2×; 40–50% → 1.25–1.5×; 30–40% → appropriately priced *if* prospects are educated before the call (the call is personalization and decision help). If you close 30–40% without that pre-education, there's probably still a 2–3× in price once you add a proper sales motion. <30% → avatar or sales-motion problem; fix those before lowering price.
- **Service businesses only go up in price**: get good → demand > capacity → raise price → better margins, reputation and talent (virtuous cycle) vs cheap → low margin → worse hires/customers (vicious cycle). Exception: unlimited-scale SaaS (~5% of the audience), where price trades gross margin against growth rate.
- **LTV:CAC by human-in-the-loop count** across attraction / conversion / delivery: 0 humans (e.g. SaaS via ads → checkout) → 3:1; 1 human → 6:1; 2 → 9:1; 3 → 12:1. The extra padding absorbs the "lumpiness" of new reps and technicians who are worse at first. 12:1 is "the minimum" if you want to scale big; 6:1 can run a business making $1–2M/yr.
- **Two long-term winning strategies**: CAC approaches 0 (massive brand or viral product, e.g. Facebook) or LTV approaches infinity (e.g. Salesforce enterprise contracts).
- **Rule of 100**: 100 actions/day for 100 days (~10,000 actions) on one channel; bigger companies apply it again for each new channel or platform. Feast-or-famine is a symptom of insufficient volume measured on too short a timeline.
- **Lead response time** as the "resting heart rate" metric under LTV:CAC.
- **Calendar utilization ~70%** for sales reps.
- **30-day payback period**, plus **30-day cash collected > COGS + CAC** (a credit card's ~30 days interest-free lets you grow with no money out of pocket).
- **Gross margin dictates everything**: net margin can't exceed gross margin.
- **Retention → LTV**: LTV ≈ annual revenue per customer ÷ annual churn. CAC within an industry is roughly a commodity; retention is where one business becomes 5–100× more valuable.
- **Prepay levers**: faster, less risky, easier (skip the line, dedicated concierge, add or double the guarantee).
- **Layaway**: start paying now, get it when paid off.
- **A business = a black box that transforms inputs into more valuable outputs.**

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Close-rate ladder above; personal sales rule of thumb is 35% with a proper process (above → raise price; below → fix process first).
- LTV:CAC 3/6/9/12:1 for 0/1/2/3 humans in the loop.
- Gym Launch year 1: ~100:1 LTV:CAC ($100K spend → $10M); later settled around ~30:1. He has had four arbitrage windows in his life, each above 30:1.
- Rule of 100: most people get their first customer by about week 3 but must commit to 100 days.
- 3 customers/month = 1 per 10 days → do 10 days' worth of advertising in 1 day to get ~1/day; companies doing 30× your sales usually do ~30× your advertising.
- Acquisition.com makes ~450–500 content pieces/week (~25–30K/yr) vs ~365/yr for a typical $1M business. "Diminishing returns are still returns."
- Call leads within 60 seconds; slow response can mean paying ~4× more per customer, plus lower close rates and margins.
- Sales calendar: target ~70–75%, never above 85%, try not below 60%. Overbooked → appointments further out and less convenient → lower show rates, no outbound or follow-up. Underbooked → low morale and "commission breath."
- "I have yet to make less money by hiring more sales[people]." Keep reps just below their income "nut" so they squeeze the pipeline.
- Payback: goal within 30 days. Every one of his businesses that got really big recovered CAC in the first 30 days. Investor-backed (~1 in 25 of the room) or capital-rich companies can go longer.
- Gross margin for services: minimum 80%; he won't get into a business below 80%. $100 cost → 70% GM = $333 price ($233 profit); 80% = $500 ($400); 90% = $1,000 ($900).
- Retention: B2B target >80% annual retention, measured on the *original* cohort (100 on Jan 1 → ≥80 of those same customers a year later, not total count). $100/yr at 50% retention ≈ $200 LTV; at 80% ≈ $500 (~5 turns), 2.5× more per customer.
- Discount for paying in full: at least 10% (time value of money / stock-market return).
- Buy 10 get 2 → ~15–20% take it; moderate discount plus a bonus (speed, risk reduction, ease) → ~30–40% prepay.
- Third-party financing (from a contact at Affirm, per the transcript's "a firm"): ~35% increase in overall sales. Financing won't save a bad business.
- Conversion benchmarks: Meta leads → in-person service ~10% of leads close; cold webinar opt-ins → sale 2–3% (broad) up to ~5% (niched); in-person/home salesperson ~35%; web pages 1–2%; Skool about pages ~4%; his Amazon book page ~25% of clicks buy (but ~1/3 the gross profit; still ~3× the money).
- Industry averages are "dumb." A friend's complex-machinery business runs net ~70%, selling machines for $400K that cost ~$17K.

## Playbook steps
1. Check close rate → place yourself on the price ladder → raise price (or fix avatar/sales motion if <30%).
2. Count humans in attraction / conversion / delivery → set the LTV:CAC target (3/6/9/12:1) before scaling ("nail it then scale it").
3. If volume is lumpy, apply the Rule of 100 to one channel for 100 days.
4. Get lead response under 60 seconds.
5. Hold rep calendars ~70%; add reps when consistently above.
6. Restructure the money model to pull cash forward: initiation/setup fees, onboarding/front-end program, bundled physical product, extended-term bundles, buy-one-get-two, last month up front.
7. Payment sequence: sell durations → offer prepay with guarantee + priority + concierge → if no, half now/half in 30 days → if no, three payments. Align payment dates to their paydays (or when deposits hit), not to delivery. Offer layaway.
8. Price services to ≥80% gross margin; decommoditize the offer if the market won't pay.
9. Measure cohort retention annually; fix churn before scaling.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Lowering price when the real problem is avatar or sales process (often pushed by a weak sales culture or a scared founder).
- Treating 3:1 LTV:CAC as law when it only applies to near-zero-human models (~5% of businesses).
- Scaling (reps, locations) before the model is nailed; ego tied to the number. "When you scale problems, they just get meaner and uglier."
- Stopping because of diminishing returns; optimizing for efficiency instead of maximum.
- Letting leads "marinate," which invites comparison shopping and price wars.
- Underpricing by "selling out of your own wallet" (feeling bad charging, or assuming your skill isn't valuable).
- Confusing gross with net margin; thinking 60% is "close to" 80%.
- Measuring retention on total customer count instead of the original cohort.
- Benchmarking against industry averages.

## Examples / case details
- Gym Launch year 1: ads half-automated, some tech-support reps, phone sales team → 100:1.
- Gym pricing was fixed first to at least 80% gross margin.
- Allen (white-label SaaS for agencies): $25K onboarding. Partners asked for unlimited installments (~$2K/mo); told onboarding would be in a year once paid off, they switched to half now, half in a month.
- Ad volume swings: high/low ad weeks produced similar sales, because reps squeezed their pipelines on slow weeks, which led him to add reps.
- Childhood Oakley sunglasses saved for over a summer: anticipation beat the purchase (layaway psychology).
- Pink Himalayan vs regular salt: branding decommoditizes.
