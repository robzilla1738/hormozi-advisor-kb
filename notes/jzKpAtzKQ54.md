## Core thesis
LTV:CAC, more precisely lifetime gross profit to customer acquisition cost, is the fundamental economic unit of a business. It predicts how far you can scale ads, how profitable you'll be, and how many customers you can get. A huge ratio lets you outbid every competitor in auction-based ad markets, an "ethical monopoly." Most strategic questions ("which offer do I advertise?", "which product line?") are math problems answered by this ratio. Improve it by raising LTV (8 levers) or lowering CAC (cheaper eyeballs, better funnel efficiency, offer).

## Frameworks & models
- **LTV = lifetime gross profit**: (price − cost of delivery/COGS) × number of purchases or months. Equivalently, monthly gross profit ÷ monthly churn. Fixed costs (rent, non-delivery staff) are excluded.
- **Service COGS**: divide delivery payroll by customers served, and model capacity (e.g., one more hire every 5 customers).
- **Early-stage churn estimate**: of the customers you started the month with, how many remain in 30 days? 9 of 10 means 10% churn, so LTV = price ÷ 10% (×10 months).
- **CAC** = (marketing payroll + media spend + sales commissions/payroll) ÷ new customers. Annualize it to smooth volatility. Example: $10K ads + $10K payroll = $20K ÷ 20 customers = $1,000.
- **Two monopolies**: undercut everyone (capital-intensive), or outspend everyone in attention auctions, which is legal and enabled by a high LTV:CAC.
- **Stack LTV**: advertise the offer with the best LTV:CAC as the front end, then ascend buyers into the other offers so their CAC becomes zero.
- **8 LTV levers**: raise price; lower cost of delivery; more purchases/frequency; cross-sell (fries with the burger); upsell better (wagyu); upsell more (two burgers); downsell smaller/cheaper (turn a no into a yes); downsell fewer units. Pick the easiest ones with the least operational drag.
- **Build LTV back to front, CAC front to back.** Top-of-funnel improvements affect more people (CTR 1%→3% is a 3× gain; show rate can't go 60%→180%). Exception: a clear outlier, e.g., 5% show rate vs a 60% industry average is a 12× opportunity.
- **CAC is similar across players selling to the same avatar.** The long-term advantage comes from how much LTV you stack.
- **Productized "shower door"**: within a business, find the simple, fast, widely wanted, low-COGS, repeatable offer. A lower ticket can still mean a higher LTV:CAC and a bigger TAM.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- At 100:1 LTV:CAC with a reliable acquisition channel, scale hard. You can be 10× less efficient and still get 10:1.
- When your ratio is absurd, "jam as much as you possibly can through that machine."
- Only a tiny TAM is an exception. 100 customers in a 1M-person market is nowhere near saturation.
- Biggest CAC gains usually come from ads, offer, headline, and sales team availability (or page CRO for e-commerce). The closest thing to a silver bullet is "nailing your offer."
- If an activity doesn't get customers cheaper or make them worth more, you're probably wasting time.
- Choose the business or product line with the highest likelihood of scaling over "passion." At scale, every business looks the same day to day (a team reporting to you).
- Only ~1 in 250 businesses reach $10M/yr, per his stat.

## Playbook steps
1. Compute CAC (trailing 30 days and trailing year).
2. Compute lifetime gross profit (price − delivery cost, ÷ churn).
3. Compare across offers and product lines; front-end the best ratio and stack the rest behind it.
4. Run through the 8 LTV levers and pick the easiest.
5. Optimize CAC from the top of the funnel down unless there's an outlier stage.
6. Spend more aggressively, entering colder audiences and new channels.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Calling revenue LTV ("wink wink Stripe screenshot").
- Service businesses ignoring delivery payroll as COGS.
- Not knowing your numbers past ~$1–3M/yr. A business coach at $3M didn't know his LTV:CAC.
- Treating math problems as opinion questions to poll mentors about.
- Pursuing custom, one-off, slow-cycle work over a productizable offer.

## Examples / case details
- **Facebook ads agency "5:1"**: $2,000/mo × 5-month average stay (20% churn) = $10K "LTV" vs $2K CAC. But $1,000/mo goes to ad spend, and reps paid $5,000/mo handle 5 accounts = $1,000/account, leaving $0. The caption says "$11,000 per account"; [inference: $5,000 ÷ 5 = $1,000]. "A way to take $2,000 and turn it into zero."
- **Starbucks**: ~38,000 locations, mostly corporate-owned and grown on unit economics. Per-customer lifetime value is garbled in the caption ("$4,99" / "$14,000"). His cookie-shop ads (free cookie with a beverage): leads under $1, 1 in 10 came in, about $10 to get someone in the door, "a 100 to one return" and more. Even if CAC rose $10→$50, still great.
- **Hair extensions educator**: $2,000 course at ~30:1 (the caption also garbles CAC as ~$30–67); a business coaching offer [inference: $15,000; caption says "$115,000"] at ~7:1 with ~$2,000 CAC. Fix: stop advertising coaching, make the course the front end, ascend buyers. 20% × $15K = +$3K, so $2K → $5K LTV and the ratio goes ~30:1 → 75:1.
- **Glass repair**: a partner spun off doing only shower doors. Easy to sell, low COGS, one tech does 5–10 doors/day vs months for custom work. Higher LTV:CAC despite the lower ticket.
- **Pool designer**: modular pools have 6–7 variables, good margin, fast cycle, and a bigger market than resort pools, so focus there.
- Gym Launch year 1: ~100:1 ($100K spent → $10M back).
- Book example: raise price; buy printing volume; 3 warehouses to cut shipping; cross-sell another book; a summary "book-lite" downsell; a 3-pack vs 1-pack.
- Applies to investing too: cost to buy a company vs what it returns over its lifetime.
