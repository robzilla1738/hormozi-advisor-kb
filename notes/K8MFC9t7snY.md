## Core thesis
Fortunes are built in the reorder business, not the order business. Make the product so valuable that customers never stop paying. Then time becomes an asset, LTV lets you outspend everyone, and you build compounding enterprise value. Most people do it backwards: they promote a weak product harder, or launch new products, and burn goodwill. Caption cuts off at the very end. (Stats at recording: crossed $100M net worth at 32; acquisition.com portfolio ~$200M/yr.)

## Frameworks & models
- **Order vs. reorder business**: one-time sales mean "always selling for tomorrow." You want credit today for a sale made a year (or 10 years) ago.
- **Recurring vs. reoccurring revenue**: subscription or repeat purchases both count; the point is that people come back. Facebook ads are reoccurring: you keep buying eyeballs with no subscription.
- **Wall of glue**: sell just one customer a week and keep them all → ~500 paying monthly after 10 years. 10 new a month with zero churn → 600 paying after 5 years. With churn, 100 sales a month just maintains you.
- **Can't-live-without test**: would they call you ahead of time when their card changes (like a phone carrier, Netflix, utilities, rent)?
- **No-marketing thought experiment**: if you could only get new customers via referrals from current ones, how would you treat them, and how good would the product be?
- **"You don't get customers to make sales. You make sales to get customers."** Each customer is a recurring revenue node.
- **Three steps to scale a fortune**: (1) promote just enough to get consumption and feedback; (2) improvement loop (survey, review client experience and results, make it faster and easier, add value until there's no comparison) until customers refer; (3) make it harder to leave (B2B: collateral or controlling the money flow, as Stripe does). Then "put gas on it" and outspend competitors.
- **Customer surplus**: price is relative to value ("price is what you pay, value is what you get"). Sell a $500K house for $50K, then learn to build it for $10K. Accept lower margin up front and drive down cost through iteration.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Due-diligence question: can we get someone to buy this and never stop paying?
- At a plateau, don't market harder (more people learn your product sucks) and don't launch another product. Fix consumption and onboarding.
- Low consumption or follow-through = your fault (onboarding too hard), not lazy customers.
- Higher LTV with equal CAC wins every ad auction (a portfolio target had 10× competitors' LTV at the same CAC).
- Scale marketing spend only after customers stick and refer.

## Playbook steps
1. Promote just enough to get first customers using the product.
2. Gather feedback; fix onboarding and consumption friction.
3. Iterate on speed, ease and value until referrals come in.
4. Add switching costs or money-flow control (B2B).
5. Then spend aggressively on acquisition.

## Mistakes he warns against
- One-time merch drops/launch cycles with no compounding vehicle.
- Blaming customers for non-use.
- Promoting harder on a product that "sucks."
- Thinking short-term like the poor ("time as a liability").

## Examples / case details
- Gym Launch: ~5,000 gyms bought the license. Had they all kept paying, he says it would be worth over $1B vs. ~$100M now; "missed out on about $900 million." Took him 7 years to internalize.
- Amazon fixes products rather than complaining about service tickets.
