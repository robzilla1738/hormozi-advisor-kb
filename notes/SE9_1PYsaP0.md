# Build a Mind So Strong It Scares People

## Core thesis
Mental toughness isn't a binary trait. It is the probability that a bad thing (anything against your preferences) changes your behavior against your goals, and it breaks into four measurable, trainable components: tolerance, fortitude, resilience and adaptability. Separate feelings from behavior. Strategy fails if you can't execute consistently because you let events dictate how you act. He wrote this while processing his mother's death.

## Frameworks & models
- **Six-point behavior curve**: (1) normal baseline → (2) bad thing forces a behavior change → (3) how far you drop → (4) time until recovery begins → (5) direction: better or worse → (6) how much better or worse the new baseline is.
- **Four components**:
  - **Tolerance**: how much hardship, or how long, before your behavior changes. The fuse length.
  - **Fortitude**: how intense the behavior change is once past the threshold (a 5-minute walk vs quitting your job, divorce, drugs).
  - **Resilience**: time to return to baseline (5 minutes vs 5 years). He says it's the most workable one and the one to work on most.
  - **Adaptability**: whether the new baseline is higher (high), the same (medium) or lower (low). "Let the hard times beat the strength into you, not out of you."
- **Trauma (his operational definition)**: a permanent change in behavior from an aversive stimulus. It can be positive or negative. If behavior didn't permanently change, you showed toughness, not trauma.
- **Guilt = broke your own rules; shame = broke others' rules.** Both are useful only if they change behavior.
- **Main-character frame** (credited to something heard on Joe Rogan): you're the protagonist of a movie with a happy ending; what would the character do next to move the plot forward?
- **Frankl gap**: between stimulus and response is a choice.
- **Biology moderates every component**: sleep and food raise all four "stats."
- **Reputation loop**: acting unshakable earns the label "he's a rock." People reinforce it and you live up to it.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Be more upset about letting something change your behavior than about the thing itself.
- If only you know you're in a bad mood, "great job." Ask what you'd normally say and say that.
- Once you hit bottom, reverse with a "full 180" immediately.
- How long you mourn has nothing to do with how much you loved.
- Telling people to "toughen up" doesn't help; name the specific component to improve. [inference: this also applies to coaching employees]

## Playbook steps
1. Recognize that you've diverged from your normal behavior.
2. Act as you would at baseline despite the feeling.
3. Shorten recovery time with each repetition.
4. Afterward, ask: "How can this bad thing serve me?"
5. Look after sleep and food to raise your baseline tolerance.
6. Write to understand: writing exposes gaps in your thinking.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Treating toughness as innate or as "alpha" chest-beating.
- Letting tiny inconveniences (Wi-Fi, traffic, a comment at a party) spiral.
- Feeling bad without changing behavior.
- Hunting for the latest tactic when the real problem is inconsistent execution.
