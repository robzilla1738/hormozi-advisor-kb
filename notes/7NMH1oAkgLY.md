## Core thesis
People buy on a prediction of future value; the easiest way to create that prediction is to give value before they buy. Most businesses are short on leads because they don't give away enough (or good enough) free stuff. A lead magnet is "the thing before the thing": a complete solution to a narrowly defined problem that, once solved, reveals the next problem your core offer solves. Preview of a chapter of *$100M Leads*, plus a 7-step lead-magnet checklist.

## Frameworks & models
- **Core offer vs lead magnet**: core offer = whatever makes you the most money (if the lead magnet starts making more, it *is* your core offer). Lead magnet = the thing before the thing.
- **Complete solution to a narrow problem, not a partial solution**: don't give "the first 6 minutes of a 12-minute video"; that loses trust. Break the big outcome into micro-steps (house painting: size up → estimate → design → 3D visualization) and fully solve one painful step at a massive discount.
- **Solve problems → create next problems**: if there are six steps, solve the first two free; they hit problem three, which becomes the paid thing.
- **Before / during / after** mapping of the customer journey to find lead-magnet steps (weight loss: grocery list → clean/prep food in under 60 min → store/portion it).
- **Lead magnet lowers total CAC even if it costs money**: CAC = cost of delivering the lead magnet.
- **Three ways to solve (from the book)**: reveal a problem (free assessment), trial (they're problem-aware; it comes back unless they keep paying), and others/combinations.
- **Delivery mechanisms**: information, service, physical product. Pick whatever the prospect sees as highest value and that most raises the chance they buy.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Massage math: offer a $100 massage to 100 people → ~1 yes. Offer a $10 massage → ~20 yes; ~1/3 later buy the $100 package → ~6 customers. If the trial costs $20 to deliver, losing $10 × 20 = $200 to get 6 customers worth about $2,000.
- "Your free stuff should be better than their paid stuff": what competitors sell as their core offer, do so well you can give it away.
- Free work that actually costs you money is usually valued much higher by the market than a free PDF/video series (unless that is exceptional).
- If a lead magnet isn't good enough that people tell others about it, "you have failed."
- "Put 100 in if you've never put more than 10."
- Book title tests: "Advertising" beat "Promotions and Advertising" 69–31; "Leads" beat "Advertising" 58–42; "Leads" beat "Marketing" → chosen.
- *$100M Offers* consumption is roughly an even quarter split across formats (~350K copies data set).

## Playbook steps
1. Pick the problem and **who** you solve it for (that changes the naming, approach, marketing, packaging and delivery).
2. Decide **how** to solve it (reveal a problem / trial / combination).
3. Decide **how to deliver** it (info, service, physical product).
4. **Name and package** it; test title, subhead and image. He calls this the most important step.
5. Make it **fast and easy to consume** (audio, written, hardback, e-reader, video).
6. Make it **really good**, not "sucky fluff."
7. Make the **next step easy**: clear CTA ("don't be clever, be clear"), with multiple ways to act (phone, URL, QR).

## Mistakes he warns against
- Partial-solution teasers that break trust.
- Fear of giving away too much value ("give more value").
- Fluff lead magnets: the 99% who never buy judge you on them and tell others not to buy.
- Only offering cheap free stuff (PDFs) when costlier free work converts more.
- Forgetting the CTA, or making it clever instead of clear.

## Examples / case details
- $10 trial massage → massage packages/subscription as the core offer.
- Weight loss: pre-loaded Instacart grocery lists per gender/weight/goal (one-time work reused for ~100 profiles), then prep in under 60 min, then storage and portioning.
- A dating book that tanked was relaunched with only a new cover and title and went on to sell 10M+ copies.
- *$100M Offers*: ~15,000 five-star reviews, ~400K copies (stated in the ad section; ~350K elsewhere).
