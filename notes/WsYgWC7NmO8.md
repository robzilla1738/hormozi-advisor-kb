## Core thesis
Ten (really 11) lessons from watching the Acquisition.com portfolio. Few things matter, so focus on them. Give equity to grow the pie. Big things take time and compound. Brand gives you pricing power. Keep it simple. Don't let emotions drive decisions. Respect downside risk. Build what customers want, not what excites you. His recipe: make promises → keep promises → build brand → pricing power → reinvest in talent and channels → give slices of the pie → add time.

## Frameworks & models
- **Few things matter a lot**: a PE partner on the Gym Launch / Prestige Labs board cared about only two levers: building in technology (for a higher multiple) and accretive M&A (rolling up similar companies). The goal was a 250M+ exit. He called everything else "blocking and tackling."
- **Weight of the slice > shape of the pie**: 1% of Facebook beats 100% of a small business. Profit sharing and bonus pools for leaders produced "outsized returns in excess of what we had given up." Bezos owns ~10% of Amazon; Musk owns ~20% and ~17% of SpaceX and Tesla (as stated).
- **Brand = promises kept in excess**: brand is "what people say behind your back." Positive word of mouth is an "invisible hand" that raises ROAS. Negative word of mouth is stronger and more viral, and it raises CAC; people wrongly blame platforms or pixel changes. Brand gives premium pricing power: plain coffee vs Starbucks (4–5×), white tee vs Nike (10×) vs Gucci (100×). The extra margin is a moat when COGS rise or supply chains break.
- **Simple scales, fancy fails**: scale creates complexity on its own. Simplify units, offer suite, customer journey, who you serve and pricing.
- **"Nothing is ever as good or as bad as we expect, except the things we don't expect"**: make conservative estimates in both directions. Outsized returns often come from the unexpected.
- **Level 10 talent only joins level 10 opportunities** (Stephen Schwarzman): make the vision big enough for others' dreams to fit inside it. "Your best talent isn't even on your team yet."
- **Compounding + time**: 10% growth per quarter ≈ "40x over a decade." [inference: 1.1^40 ≈ 45×, so his figure is roughly right]. Hit singles consistently.
- **Anything × 0 = 0**: take a company-sized risk only when *not* acting risks the company more, and only with strong evidence.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Ruthlessly cut everything outside the few big levers.
- Ask "Will this matter in 10 years?"
- Set conservative goals you're sure to hit, with room to overachieve. His goals for portfolio companies have become more and more conservative.
- Don't bet the company at $3–5M on a non-core opportunity. Founders destroying fine businesses is far more common than Kodak or Blockbuster cases.
- Before a big new product line, survey customers and let data beat ego.
- "Do not risk the empire for a pot of gold."

## Playbook steps
1. Identify the 1–3 levers that drive value (e.g. multiple expansion, accretive M&A) and focus attention there.
2. Tie leaders to the vision with profit share and bonus pools.
3. Underpromise and overdeliver to build word of mouth → pricing power.
4. Simplify the offer, avatar, journey and pricing.
5. Plan in decades. Target steady quarterly gains, like 10%.
6. Improve the core product: accuracy, time to value, customer health score, segmentation (Dragon Dictation 2.0).
7. Survey customers before you launch upsells.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Chasing shiny objects from workshops, seminars and ads.
- Ego wanting 100% ownership.
- Obsessing over fast wins instead of thinking 10–20 years out.
- Big promises with under-delivery, which hides negative word of mouth.
- Adding complexity at scale.
- Emotional catastrophizing or exuberance: "the amount of times we have lost them is zero."
- Unrealistic goals.
- Betting the company without existential need.
- Building what excites the founder rather than what customers want.

## Examples / case details
- The portfolio does about $75K per working hour (40 hrs/wk).
- Andrew Cherng (Panda Express; transcribed "andrew chung"): ~2,600 locations, all 100% owned. It took 45 years, with most growth in the last ~5 years; ~600 locations were opened this year (as stated). His two keys: customers must love the product, and big things take time.
- Dragon Dictation 2.0 added zero new features, only accuracy, and was widely praised.
- Portfolio upsell survey: 85% wanted more of what they already bought; 15% wanted the new thing.
- About 80% of the portfolio is non-tech.
