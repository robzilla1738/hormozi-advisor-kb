## Core thesis
Five advantages make any business easier to grow and more profitable: **Sticky, Expensive (high gross margin), Expansion (growing market), Air (low operational complexity/low capex), Unique (moat)**. Few businesses have all five; each is a continuum, not a binary. Retention ("sticky") matters most. If you have none, fix retention first and backfill the rest. Includes two plugs for the acquisition.com/roadmap lead magnet.

## Frameworks & models
- **The 5 advantages (S-tier ranking of "opportunity vehicles")**, built for the "level-10 skill set, level-2 opportunity" problem.
1. **Sticky**: revenue retention. "Be in the resale business, not the sales business" (credited to John Paul DeJoria).
   - Logo retention (customer count, can't exceed 100%) vs net revenue retention (can exceed 100% if stayers spend more).
   - Structural/involuntary churn (they move, die, their business dies) vs voluntary churn (they think you suck). Avoid the voluntary kind.
   - NRR >100% comes from a clear path for cheap customers to spend more, plus qualifying buyers who actually need it. Skool example: $9 → $99 tier = 11×; even with 20% leaving the $9 tier, 10% upgrading gets you over 100% NRR.
   - **Skool churn curve (hundreds of thousands of memberships, all categories)**: month 1 churn is 20%+; the next drop is ~10% around month 3; the last big drop is at month 6; after that, churn is ~2%/month. Goal: get members to month 6.
   - **Company A vs B**: A sells 100/200/300 and loses the same each year. B sells 100 a year and loses none. Revenue is the same, but A acquires 2× the customers, and the marginal ones cost 2–3× more ("almost like 900 customers" from a cost view). B compounds and you can "excel sheet out your wealth."
2. **Expensive**: high gross margin (costs a penny, sells for a buck). Lets you pay people better, speeds up the cash conversion cycle, funds reinvestment, raises EBITDA margin. $100M at 10% = $20M at 50%. Ties to *$100M Money Models* and the Offers book chapter on decommoditizing.
3. **Expansion**: an *industry* that's growing. Don't need a tailwind, just avoid a headwind. Example: alternative education CAGR >20% (the Skool bet).
4. **Air**: low operational complexity (number of variables to manage per unit of growth: podcast ad read vs 100-restaurant chain) and low capex. Means less dilution and self-funded growth (Buffett: cash generators that don't need constant reinvestment).
5. **Unique**: a moat; barriers to entry. Capex can itself be a moat (the "shovel," power plant). Best moats: things you know how to do that no one else can (Nvidia, nuclear), then recipes/processes/patents (new, non-obvious, useful), then **brand** (Revlon vs CVS generic come off the same belt; the brand converts more people at a higher price and makes customers stickier).
- **Coke scored on all 5**: sticky, cheap to make, still expanding, middling on operations, unique through brand plus recipe.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you could have only one advantage, pick stickiness.
- Retention: focus first on the first 30 days, then get members past month 3, then to month 6 (~2% monthly churn after).
- Raising capital to grab market share only makes sense with real network effects. "We'll make it up in volume" is rarely true. Skool qualifies because extra users cost little and the network effects are strong.
- High ROIC makes you "a magnet for money." If you can't raise money, the problem is usually the core economics, not your pitch. Make the deal better (e.g., $100K franchise paying back in 3 years = mediocre; $300K back in year one = enticing).
- Once you have some success, prefer businesses that need capital to expand, because there are fewer competitors.
- Don't jump ship after watching this. Use the five as levers to improve the business you have. But if your industry has no retention and you're early in your career, switching may "not be the dumbest decision."

## Playbook steps
1. Score your business on each of the 5 as a continuum. 2. Fix retention first: a great first 30 days, a reason to stay past month 3, get them to month 6; add an upgrade path for NRR >100%; qualify buyers. 3. Raise gross margin by decommoditizing / money models. 4. Make sure the market isn't shrinking. 5. Reduce operational variables and capex. 6. Build a moat: capex/tech/equipment with ROIC, trade secrets/patents, brand.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Staying in the "sales business" with no retention.
- Hopping between vehicles because the current one feels month-to-month, which kills compounding.
- Entering shrinking markets even if you can market (newspapers, formal education −6%/yr, tobacco, alcohol, brick-and-mortar retail, clerical/data entry).
- Low-barrier markets (social media marketing agencies: anyone can do it, so prices fall).
- Blaming fundraising skill when the unit economics are weak.

## Examples / case details
- Not sticky: education alone, roofing, car sales. Sticky: term life insurance, alarm systems, internet/phone, banking; community and consumables.
- Low margin: grocery, farming, restaurants (food is elastic; commodities). High margin: media, information/education, community, data, software, pharma, "lotions and potions."
- Growing: energy, AI, healthcare, cybersecurity, e-commerce, alternative education.
- Portfolio did over $250M in revenue last year.
