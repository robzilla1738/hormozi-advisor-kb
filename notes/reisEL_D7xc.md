## Core thesis
Thirteen numbered marketing lessons in one video. Start cheap or free to get volume through the business. Scale with more, then better, then new. Put most of your effort into the first 5 seconds and the headline. Judge everything by LTV:CAC, using lifetime gross profit. Make lots of ads, then squeeze the winners. Spend real hours on the core four. Track your results and state them truthfully. Show rather than tell. Go narrower before broader. Give away your best stuff. Treat a "channel doesn't work" problem as a skill or awareness-level problem. Repeat yourself, because people need to be reminded more than taught. Opening claims: 9 companies built and sold, the last for $46.2M to American Pacific Group, and 2 books with over 1M copies sold.

## Frameworks & models
- **Start low or free, then raise prices**: free customers pay you back 4 ways. They convert to paying, they refer, they leave testimonials and reviews, and they give feedback. The real test is whether they stay and pay full price. Pitch it as: "I plan on going to this price point, but I'm willing to do it for free in exchange for" feedback plus a public testimonial (and a referral). Make the testimonial and referral conditional on "if I do a good job."
- **"Create flow, monetize flow, then add friction."** Also: "nail it, then scale it."
- **More → Better → New**: do more until a unit of better pays more than another unit of more. Example: going from 1 to 2 sales reps roughly doubles sales, but going from 10 to 11 adds only 10%. At that point, close-rate tweaks, headline split tests and CTA changes give more per unit of effort. After better, go back to "how do we do 10× more?" Do new last, because "the cost of change of doing something new is guaranteed, but the return on something new is not."
- **The 10× question**: "What would stop me from 10x-ing the business right now?" Find the first thing that would break, fix it, add volume, then repeat at the next bottleneck. "Growth is an outcome of inputs."
- **Paid ads are an auction for attention**: the money competitors spend in your market is your opportunity. A higher LTV lets you outbid them, which he calls an "ethical monopoly."
- **Optimize ads front to back, build the business back to front**: the front end gives the biggest multipliers. Raising CTR from 1% to 4% is 4× throughput. Raising show rate from 50% to 75% is only +50%. LTV (the back end) decides how much you can spend on the front.
- **Hook effort ratio**: about 50 hooks, 3–5 "meat" bodies and 1–3 CTAs per session. Of every hour, about 55 minutes go to the first 5 seconds.
- **Call-outs** (book p.136): they run from hyper-specific to broad. Dropped dishes make everyone look, "Mom" makes moms look, your name makes only you look. Make them as specific as possible while still broad enough to reach volume, e.g. "moms in Nevada" or "homeowners in Clark County."
- **LTV:CAC (he uses LTGP)**: lifetime gross profit vs. the all-in cost to acquire a customer (sales, marketing, software). He calls it "the foundational economic unit of any business."
- **Ad creation process**: never mute ads; keep a swipe folder; batch weekly and write the same day you record ("cram for the test"); rewatch your best historical ads (first 10 and 30 seconds); of 50 hooks, 40 are proven and 10 are new (80/20).
- **Power law**: the top 5% of ads make 95% of the income.
- **Kaleidoscope process** for a winning ad: change the background, add props, reenact it, reorder it, add filters, add VFX, change fonts and captions, change pacing (1.2–1.5×), add music, or keep the same hook with a different body.
- **Core four**: warm outreach, cold outreach, content and paid ads (one-to-one vs. one-to-many × people who know you vs. strangers). If you aren't doing one of these, you aren't marketing.
- **Scaling ladder**: $0–6 figures = 1 product, 1 avatar, 1 channel. 6–7 figures = the same, done consistently. 7–8 figures = 1 avatar, 1 channel, 2 products (an upsell). Multi-8 figures = 2 channels, 1 avatar, 2 products. Exception: AG1 (1 product, many channels).
- **State the facts and tell the truth**: "If you don't track, you don't care." Measurement alone improves results ("measurement as intervention"). Clickbait is fine if it's backed by truth.
- **Results claim formula**: % of people (median, average or overall) who achieve X outcome in Y time (or after X attempts) under Z conditions. Fewer conditions make it more compelling.
- **Say what only you can say, show what only you can show**: "do epic stuff, talk about what you did" ("how I" vs. "how to"). Value per second, not seconds of value. To sell to rich people: "promise the same result in half the time for twice the price."
- **Five ways to expand a market** (pyramid): up-market, down-market, adjacent, narrower, broader. Do this only after you're the best in your current market.
- **Vista Private Equity customer 80/20** (from Vista's head of pricing and packaging): rank customers by value, find the top 20% that drive 80% of revenue, copy the channels and offers that brought them in, and stop serving the rest. Profile them by psychographics, geographics, demographics and actions, then cut it down to 2–3 variables.
- **Value Equation**: dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ time delay × effort and sacrifice. Example: liposuction vs. a $20 PDF, and a 10,000-surgery surgeon vs. a first-day one. Think toward good things and away from bad ones, made risk-free, faster and easier.
- **Endless content wheel**: comments and questions become new content. Implementation friction points become problems to solve.
- **"Give away the secrets, sell the implementation"**: make your free stuff better than their paid stuff. A lead magnet is "a complete solution to a very narrow problem." Make CTAs about personalization ("help apply this to your business"), not "more."
- **"All advertising works. It's just a matter of efficiency."** Rephrase "X doesn't work" as "I don't know how to make X work."
- **Schwartz's five awareness levels**: unaware → problem aware → solution aware → product aware → most aware. Ad ceilings (e.g. $2K, $10K or $100K a day) usually mean the ads aren't good enough to reach the next level.
- **"The size of the plane is directly correlated with the length of the runway"**: a higher price and a colder audience both need more selling. Scaling trouble often comes from a mismatch, e.g. a warm message sent to a cold audience.
- **Watermelon slice / golf clubs**: there are fewer buyers per thousand views as you go colder. Each awareness level is another club you have to learn to hit.
- **"We need to be reminded more than we need to be taught"**: "The news never changes, just the names." Reuse topics across formats, media and contexts, and tell new stories about the same concept.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- After your first case-study batch of 10–20 customers, raise price about 20% every 5 sales (e.g. $0 → $1,000 → $1,200 → $1,500). Stop when conversion rate × price starts to fall. Measure total revenue per number of sales calls.
- Spend about 90% of your time on more and better. Try new only when nothing is working or a channel is truly saturated (e.g. a local gym spending $10K a month already reaches everyone in its radius several times a week).
- Give a new channel 3–12 months. Measure progress, not dollars: clicks → opt-ins → right people → shows → sales ("walk the dollar over the bridge"). A second channel doubles the business and makes it more stable.
- Write copy for someone who doesn't know you, is in a rush and has a third-grade education. "Clear beats clever, deletion beats explanation." Run copy through a reading-level calculator until it scores third grade. He says about 30% of people can barely read.
- Minimum LTV:CAC is 3:1, using gross profit. Spend at most a third of LTGP to acquire a customer. Try to get CAC back within the first 30 days, e.g. with an upfront one-time charge.
- LTGP estimate: last year's gross profit ÷ number of customers. It underestimates, which he says is the safer way to be wrong.
- His biggest money came from 30:1 up to 100–200:1. "Maintenance" ratios are 5:1–10:1. A large starting ratio signals room to scale: 100:1 at $1K a day might become about 5:1 at $100K a day. He cares about absolute return over relative return.
- If you're under about $1–3M a year, spend the first 4 hours of every day on the core four.
- When presenting results, prefer fewer conditions even if the numbers are lower.
- Fix being the best in your current market before expanding. He says more than half of Acquisition.com's portfolio companies made their avatar narrower, not broader.
- Down-sells: if 10% of calls buy something at 1/10 the price, revenue moves about 1%. Give that product away free instead and use it to attract better customers.
- 20:1 at a warm niche might drop to 5:1 in the middle and 3:1 at scale. That's fine at 100× the spend.

## Playbook steps
1. Launch a new product or division free or at cost, framed as "I'm new at this," in exchange for feedback and a testimonial (plus a referral if you do a good job).
2. Raise price about 20% every 5 sales until revenue per call peaks.
3. Do far more volume on content and ads (benchmarks: 35 ads a week, 50+ content pieces a day across platforms).
4. Weekly ad session: review the swipe file, rewatch past winners, write 50 hooks (40 proven, 10 new), 3–5 bodies and 1–3 CTAs, and record the same day.
5. Run the kaleidoscope process on any winner.
6. Track customer data at day 1, 30, 60 and 90 and at the end. Improve until the data is compelling, then publish it in the % / outcome / time / conditions format.
7. Find your top 20% of customers, narrow to them, and tell sales to sell only to them going forward.
8. Give away your low-ticket product and your best secrets, with personalization CTAs, and have your team follow up and move qualified people up.
9. When ad spend hits a ceiling, write ads for the next colder awareness level.
10. Repurpose core concepts across formats and new stories. Don't stop running an ad just because you're bored of it.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Trying to sell too soon without proof, or starting at a high price you can't come back down from.
- Asking how to make content "better" when you're barely making any.
- Thinking you've "saturated" a channel at a tiny share of the market.
- Spending 80% of your time on new things, or quitting a new channel after one month of ad spend.
- Putting most of your effort into the back of the ad or the end of the funnel.
- Using a lead-cost benchmark instead of gross-profit math, or confusing lifetime revenue with lifetime gross profit.
- Testing lots of new hooks and too few proven ones, or moving on from a winner instead of squeezing it.
- Spending zero hours a day on promotion.
- Not tracking results ("if you don't track, you don't care").
- Claiming epic results with no work behind them.
- Taking every avatar ("can't say no to fast money and so they never get the big money"), or making revenue the goal and adding cost to inflate it. "Better creates bigger. Bigger creates bloat."
- Treating free content as an afterthought. 99% of people who consume it never buy, and they make your reputation.
- Chasing ego metrics. His 6-month broader-content experiment got more views but fewer business owners. After switching back, opt-ins, book sales, workshop sign-ups and portfolio applications all went up.
- Getting sick of your own ads before the audience has even seen them.

## Examples / case details
- First fitness business: clients donated $500 or $1,000 to a charity of their choice in exchange for 12 weeks of training. That brought about 13 first customers, who later paid and referred others.
- Gym Launch: 2+ years of free turnarounds (he paid for the marketing and his own travel and was paid only on sales) across 30+ markets. The system was later licensed to 5,000+ locations and the company sold for $46M. At its peak he took home over $1M a month in personal income. He also started his software company (named "Allen" in the captions) with free customers.
- A content advisor showed him they were posting 3–10× more volume than he was. A billionaire friend posting once a day vs. Hormozi's 50+ a day.
- A chiropractor agency ($2M a year, spending $40K a month) thought it had saturated Facebook. The industry is $10B, and the agencies serving it probably spend about $4M a month combined.
- A portfolio CEO made 15 ads a month. Hormozi made 35 a week (about 140 a month, roughly 9×) when he was scaling 5×.
- His team went from 1 to 4–5 YouTube videos a week and learned faster.
- Dean / Larry King infomercial: $250K spent with nothing back. He re-shot the first 30 seconds (paying Larry King again) and it did $100M in sales.
- A top creator stopped another creator's video 5 seconds in: "you didn't confirm the thumb." A thumbnail change can move average view duration by 2–3 minutes.
- An 8-figure portfolio company changed only its landing-page headline: booking rate rose 62% while opt-in rate stayed the same.
- Bow maker ($20–30M a year): a $20 lead with 1 in 5 buying gives a $100 CAC against $500 gross profit (5:1). A $10 lead would give 10:1. He says the $500 figure is made up for illustration.
- Meals: $10 price, $9 cost, $1 gross profit each. 5 weeks of meals = $50 LTGP, so CAC should be about $15 or less. [inference: that implies about 10 meals a week]
- Starbucks: about $14K LTV per customer, so even a $500 CAC is 28:1.
- Gym Launch winning ad by Cal, now its president: 6 months earlier he worked a full-time job to pay the gym's rent, with a pregnant wife and a 2-month-old. Now the gym is full and outsourced, and he has a $250K offer to buy it.
- GymCon audience (owners at $500K–$3M): nobody spent the first 4 hours of the day promoting. "This is why you're small."
- Gym Launch results: the average gym makes $100K+ in added yearly profit after 12 months. The bottom 20% average about $40K in added revenue (roughly break-even). The top 20% make $500K+.
- Skool: 30.44% of people who finish their first month make their first dollar online. After a new feature, 35% more succeed, so he expects a bit over 40%.
- Showing instead of telling: an agency plays a 1-minute recording of a real lead call ("could you take 50 of these a month?"); a software demo runs on the prospect's own video; a door-to-door salesman got gum out of his gym's turf and sold him $100 of cleaner in about 13 minutes.
- A YouTuber ("Erac" in the captions) packed 100 dates, a season of The Bachelor, into 16 minutes.
- A coach of coaches at $2M a year thought the market was saturated. Hormozi's answer: get better. Gym Launch served only microgyms doing weight-loss before-and-afters (no personal trainers, no big box for 4–5 years, no yoga, spin or Pilates) and still does $30M+ a year.
- Two workshop agencies: a generalist found that IT clients were 8% of customers but by far the most profitable, so he told them to sell only to IT. A professional-services agency was told to sell only to family-law (divorce) attorneys.
- A registered dietitian with 5,800 Instagram followers and low engagement made just under $1M a year in profit teaching dietitians to bill insurance: 5–10 cheat sheets a day at about $99, plus 2–3 high-ticket clients a week.
- A friend with a multi-nine-figure SEO firm posts deep tactical content with little engagement, and his leads are Fortune 100/500 executives asking to hire him.
- A-list celebrity estimate: about $50M (backed out from blockbuster marketing budgets).
- The largest portfolio company (about $100M a year) spends millions a month marketing to completely unaware audiences.
- Henry Ford's marketing director, after 3 months of Ford seeing the mock-ups: "We haven't even started running it yet." The Friends reruns are watched more now than a few years ago.
