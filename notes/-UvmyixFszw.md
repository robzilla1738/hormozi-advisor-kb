## Core thesis
If a service business can't afford ~$100–200 to get a customer in the door, it doesn't have a marketing problem, it has a monetization (cash flow) problem. The "cash flow quadrant" (from his in-progress book "Building a Better Mousetrap"): stack (1) products, (2) add-on services, (3) prepay/financing, (4) continuity onto any front-end offer so cash per show is high enough to outspend competitors on every channel. "It comes from the back, not the front." Older Gym Launch-era video.

## Frameworks & models
- **Three offer types**: free, discount (e.g. $19–29 new client special), value (no discount). All can feed the same "mousetrap."
- **Cash flow quadrant**:
  1. **Products**: high-margin physical product that goes with the service (gym → supplements; chiropractor → orthotics, pillow, brace; beauty → masks, serums, creams).
  2. **Add-on services**: upsells inside the core offer (gym: nutrition coaching, accountability, semi-private) and a **menu close** ("let me show you what else we offer": med spa coolsculpting, filler, microdermabrasion, skin tightening).
  3. **Prepay / financing**: third-party financing (e.g. CareCredit in healthcare); or offer ~10% discount to prepay. Say "prepay," not "paid in full."
  4. **Continuity, "downsell the upsell"**: charge more upfront (acquisition and onboarding cost more than maintenance), then offer the same thing on a recurring basis at a decent discount.
- **Two growth numbers**: 30–60 day cash per customer sets what you can spend to acquire; LTV × monthly sales sets the revenue ceiling (20 sales/mo × $2,000 LTV → caps around $40K/mo). Stalled growth = weak back end or too little inflow.
- **Cash per show unlocks channels**: at high enough cash per show, referral partners, cold traffic, affiliates, direct mail and social all become affordable.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Can't make ~$100 per customer walking in (service business) → fix the monetization process, not the market.
- Even a free front end breaks even if you sell $100–200 in product per person.
- Package minimum (e.g. $500) is why you close ~half instead of everyone.
- Continuity example: $200/mo at 10% monthly churn ≈ $2,000 per continuity customer.

## Playbook steps
1. Pick front end (free/discount/value). 2. Close onto core package. 3. Sell take-home products ("to maintain/extend these results"). 4. Menu close on other services; prescribe a total package. 5. Offer prepay (10% off) or financing. 6. Downsell to recurring continuity at the end of the package. 7. Choreograph handoffs so every team member passes the baton to the next sale. 8. Use cash per show to add acquisition channels.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Believing you have a marketing problem when it's a monetization problem.
- Saying "paid in full" instead of "prepay."
- Feeling bad about "selling all the time": retention research points to expansion revenue/ascension; making offers is your duty if you believe in the value.

## Examples / case details
- Prepay math: of 10 walk-ins, 5 buy at ~$500 avg; one prepays $2,500, which he says lifts average ticket from $500 to ~$1,000. [inference: with 5 buyers it's (4×500+2,500)/5 = $900; his figure is approximate.]
- $19 offer worked example: close 50% onto a $2,000 package → $1,000; +$200 products → $1,200; 2/5 buyers add $1,000 services → +$400 → $1,600; 2/5 take $200/mo continuity (10% churn ≈ $2,000) → +$800 → "$2,400 per person who walked in the door." [inference: he mixes per-show and per-buyer figures. Strictly per show (10 shows, 5 buyers) it's $1,000 + $100 + $200 + $400 = $1,700; per buyer it's $2,000 + $200 + $400 + $800 = $3,400. Either way it's far above the $100/show business.]
- Med spa: discount Botox covers cost per show → take-home serum kit → menu close → 6-month total beauty package → discounted recurring.
