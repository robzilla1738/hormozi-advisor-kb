# How to Get SO Rich You Realize Money Has No Meaning

Signal: high

## Core thesis
Three ways to get rich, and then to see that money was never the point. (1) Build a brand, meaning trust that people grow from past experience with you. It raises CTR, conversion, price and repurchases. (2) Know the inputs and outputs of your money-making system down to the smallest activity. Do only what drives customers or LTV, then scale it through automation, other people or one-to-many. (3) Get "lead getters" (employees, agencies, customers, affiliates) to advertise for you, ideally decentralized through a product good enough to be referred. Closes with a personal section: after selling (Gym Launch/Prestige Labs, $46.2M), he lost a year to sadness and decided "hard work is the goal."

## Frameworks & models
- **Branded bull analogy**: you treat a cow differently if its brand is one you like, one you hate, one you don't know (it still belongs to someone), or no brand at all. The point of branding is to change the behavior of whoever sees it.
- **Brand = trust = "predictive power based on past experiences"** (he quotes Jobs: brand = trust). You build it by making promises and keeping or exceeding them. With no history of your own, buyers judge you by how you look, compared with businesses like yours.
- **Four economic payoffs of brand**: 1) Conversion: illustrative close rate at a given price is ~0% for a hated brand, 20% neutral, up to 80% for an exceptional one. 2) CTR, meaning conversion earlier in the funnel: illustrative 1% negative, 3% neutral, 5% positive. 3) Price: the premium works as "insurance" that pays down the buyer's risk. 4) Repurchases: you pay to acquire a customer once and they buy again and again (See's Candies, Apple's ecosystem).
- **Give:ask ratio, extended**: Jobs framing: a product people love is a deposit, a mediocre one a withdrawal. Ideally the ask itself becomes a give. Starting people with his books is how he does that.
- **Media measures word of mouth**: a brand compounds because people share it, not because of logos or fonts. The arbitrage is giving a small amount to a million people at once, many times over ("fractional gives").
- **Money-making system box**: price above cost, spun as many times as possible. Inputs are either feeders (attention) or delivery. Growth comes only from more customers or higher LTV.
- **His own inputs, as actual activities**: script, record, check status, review and give feedback, review deal metrics (Acquisition.com / Acq Ventures), present new direction, review portco metrics and allocate resources.
- **Three ways to scale an input**: automate (including AI), other people (in partial chunks, not all or nothing), or one-to-many.
- **Three questions for an employee**: What do you do (in concrete terms)? What do we do? How does what you do help what we do? Example: a finance person flags spend that's above benchmark, which shapes what we spend to get customers and make them worth more.
- **Leverage levels / lead getters**: you doing outreach yourself is fixed throughput. Each lead getter you add becomes an ongoing node. Employees and agencies are centralized. Customers and affiliates are decentralized and keep advertising beyond what you could manage.
- **Sadness vs anxiety**: sadness = no perceived options; anxiety = many options and no priorities.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you'd have to "say a prayer" before raising prices 10%, you have a bad business (citing Buffett). Charging 10% more on the same costs "basically doubles" profit in his drawing. [inference: that is only true near a ~10% net margin. At higher margins the lift is smaller.]
- The data litmus test: if the number going up or down wouldn't change what you do, don't track it. ("Are you data driven or data distracted?") Ad CTR is worth tracking because a drop means new hooks or targeting. Social tags aren't.
- Meeting cost: annual pay ÷ 2,000 = hourly cost. A 10-person meeting can cost ~$500, and it should return 4–5× that or not happen.
- Cancel recurring meetings that only share data nobody acts on.
- You can reach about $10M just by marketing and selling to strangers. Getting "mega rich," especially B2C, takes decentralized word of mouth, because paid media eventually hits diminishing returns.
- High-LTV B2B can still scale one-to-many through in-person events or conference circuits.
- Ranking of what gets lead getters going: an exceptional product first, an amazing incentive second, reinvesting in whatever is working third.
- Referrals aren't 1:1. Typically about 1 in 10 people refers about 10. You can't tell who that will be, so over-deliver for everyone.

## Playbook steps
1. Build brand by keeping promises, giving at scale (content, books) and making the paid product feel like a give.
2. List your weekly activities and how each one ties to making money. Cut the indirect ones.
3. Define everyone's inputs in concrete terms using the three questions.
4. Scale each input through automation, people or one-to-many.
5. Add lead getters, moving from centralized to decentralized: customers and affiliates.
6. Fix the product before you push incentives. Use incentives to turn "I'd refer anyway" into "I'll refer everyone" (one to n, not zero to one).
7. When a referral or affiliate channel works, keep reinvesting in it: remove steps, add training, lower the effort it takes.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Using incentives to cover a mediocre product (the soggy-sandwich "50% off if you refer" example). Most incentives, like 20% or 50% off the next item, aren't actually strong, and one so big people buy just to collect it defeats the purpose.
- Putting a referral or affiliate system on autopilot instead of reinvesting in it.
- Pet projects that aren't the constraint, like reorganizing project management or redoing the website when you could split-test headlines. He calls it "arts and crafts" while doing business.
- Too many meetings, and employees meeting with each other once the company gets big.
- Tracking data that doesn't change behavior.
- Over-relying on marketing skill: "marketing is gasoline," so a weak product just gets known faster. That still pays, and he says it's why the info space has a bad name.
- Saying "I only do this to help humanity," which sets you up to be attacked later for making money.

## Examples / case details
- ALN/Allen software launch to his gym base: gyms that missed the webinar called and texted "sign me up for whatever it is" without seeing the pitch.
- Leads book launch: ads ran at ~6% CTR, with signups at about $1 each. The first book had ~26,000 five-star reviews. Its Amazon page converts at ~30%.
- See's Candies raised prices every year, above the inflation index. Buffett: "we've got you for life."
- He writes 2 Mozi Minute emails a week, about an hour each, and calls them very high leverage.
- He took over a department and cancelled every recurring data-sharing meeting.
- He mentions a public CEO (name garbled in the captions) who says about 1/10 of employees drive most of a company's economics.
- Musk: "zero marketing" because he is a one-man marketing department.
- Skool: last year 55% of customers came from affiliates or referrals, so they're doubling down.
- First gym (Huntington Beach): one nurse in the first ~10 customers brought about 20 more, enough to decide whether he made rent.
- First Christmas away from the gym, a sales hire went 2-for-4 on his script. He calls it his first leading indicator of going from a job to an asset.
- At 27 he had ~$1M cash and at 29 over $20M. In college he dreaded winning a $1B Powerball because he wanted to prove he could do it himself.
- He started down the charity route, decided charities aren't self-sustaining, then took the idea of benefiting all stakeholders from Mackey's *Conscious Capitalism*.
- He loved fitness, but two weeks after opening a gym he found he loved business more.
