# Selling with Logic To Make Lots of Money [Alex Hormozi]

## Core thesis
A live talk to gym owners (Gym Launch event) on closing, meaning everything after you present the offer (the "red zone"). He calls himself a "logical closer." Emotion fades within weeks, but logic is still there when the buyer looks back, so buyers who decided on logic regret it less. Sales is "helping people make decisions to help themselves." Prospects hand their power to three things (circumstances, other people, self), which he calls the "onion of blame." The closer peels those layers back until an empowered person makes a decision, either way. His single most-used frame: "will this get me closer or further from what I want?" (not "will this get me what I want?").

## Frameworks & models
- **Help vs manipulation = intention.** Test: if the prospect knew everything you know and would still choose not to buy, you're deceiving them.
- **Selling vs closing:** selling happens before the ask; closing is everything from the moment you present the offer.
- **Obstacles vs objections:** obstacles come before the offer. You're on offense there, so knock out as many as you can. Objections come after. Obstacles are easier. Example: "I just want to find out more" is an obstacle; dig to the real problem ("I'm overweight").
- **Selling = transference of belief over a bridge of trust.** Belief and trust are continuums, not binaries ("how much rapport did you build," not "did you").
- **10 / 10 / 80 split:** 10% will never buy, 10% would buy from a scarecrow, 80% need help deciding. Training is for the 80%.
- **Three beliefs the prospect needs:** (1) the product gets them to the goal the way they want, (2) you and others will support them, (3) it will work for *them*. Give enough proof that not believing becomes more unreasonable than believing.
- **Albert Ellis's three causes of emotional disturbance → onion of blame:** circumstances, other people, self. A circumstance objection is the outer layer, so expect 2–3 more layers underneath.
- **Five obstacle types:** time ("now's not a good time"), value ("can't afford it"), fit ("not sure it's for me"), authority ("talk to my spouse"), avoidance ("need to think about it" / "I don't make fast decisions").
- **Time:**
  - Macro: "busy is the best time." You'll be busy again for the rest of your life, so learn to do it while it's hard.
  - Micro (hour by hour): his wife checked his screen time, 3.5 hrs/day on social media. "Obama runs every day." Everyone has the same 24 hours, so it's a priorities problem. "We'll cut the 90% that isn't working."
  - **When-then fallacy:** "when I'm healed I'll go to the hospital."
- **Value/money:** "can't afford it" means not valuable enough (example: a Ferrari offered for $5K if you can pay within 24 hrs; anyone finds the money).
  1. Why "a lot for you" is good: his best testimonials came from people for whom it was a lot of money. "Would you believe me if it were 1/10 the price?"
  2. Why it's not actually a lot: separate price from belief ("if you got to your high school weight, would it be worth it?").
  3. What's money good for anyway: you'll spend it in the next 12 months regardless. "You're buying the lessons either way," with time or money, and "we spend the thing we value least."
  4. You don't need money: "self-made millionaires and you both started at zero." Be resourceful, not resourced. Shoe Dog story: Phil Knight got his manufacturers to cover payroll. Everyone finds money for a surprise tax bill.
- **"Don't let a bad decision burn you twice"** (seventh-grade breakup analogy).
- **Fit:**
  - New identity → new priorities: Sephora story, where girls told "now that you're women" happily budgeted for makeup. "We vote with our dollars for what we care about."
  - Pain of change: "that breakfast got you that body"; "you gotta change to change." Tony Robbins quote: you change when the pain of staying the same exceeds the pain of change. The "come back 50 lbs heavier" provocation. Everyone has a "thermostat" you can reset.
  - Hypothetical: "If the program didn't have X, would you do it?" Isolate → hypothesize → overcome. Also ask "what is it missing?" to surface the hidden fear.
- **Authority (3 steps):**
  1. Isolate and collapse the foil: "what part do you think they wouldn't approve of?" The absent person can't answer, so the prospect gives their own real reason.
  2. Realize they've already given permission: "do they approve of your current struggle?" Plus role reversal: would you oppose your spouse getting help?
  3. Realize you never needed permission: "you're looking for permission, not support." Three years from now, is it fair to blame them? Close with a three-day no-sweat guarantee: "if he says he wants you to die 10 years earlier, have him call me."
- **Avoidance:** translate "I need to think about it" into "I want to avoid this decision for fear of making a mistake." "Decide" comes from Latin for "to kill off": which future are we killing off today?
  - Past: "this isn't a fast decision, you've been deciding for years." "Came this far": stack every action they took to get here. "Another year of almost?" "What has not deciding cost you over the last 5 years?"
  - Present: the **rocking chair close**. Nobody actually goes home and thinks; life takes over. "You don't need time to make a decision, you need information." Ask for their criteria, then teach a decision framework: **product / us / you / access to money** (believe it works? trust us? will it work for you? have or can get the money?). Then the **informed-decision close**: "how can you make an informed decision without doing it? Start, and if you hate it 30 days in, I'll give your money back."
  - Future: future pacing. Add the last 5 years' gain onto now (230 → 270 lbs; −$10K → −$50K).
- **Best case / worst case (risk-free) close:** both options are risk-free (walk out, or sign up with a guarantee), but only one gets you closer. One of his top three closes for weight loss. If they still refuse, it's emotional, so confront the fear. Also: "you won't struggle forever, so why not be in shape for 30 years instead of 3?"
- **Bonus "that's the reason" close:** whatever reason they give is the perfect reason to do it.
- **Closer vs further** (he says it "made me more money than anything in my life"): lowers the bar for taking action and takes the pressure off a perfect decision. "The perfect decision maker makes none; the directional decision maker makes many and course corrects."
- **Hundred-bricks model of education:** people credit the last brick. An education purchase is only a bad investment relative to your expectations.
- **Cost of ignorance close:** target income minus current income is what not-knowing costs you each year (live example: $1M − $185K = $815K/yr). "Ignorance is the most expensive debt." He credits this close to someone whose name he couldn't remember.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- "If you didn't get a gasp from the price tag, you didn't go high enough."
- Expect a no; it isn't failure. If they could decide alone, they wouldn't need you.
- Reward reps for getting a decision (either way), not just sales. Reward activities, not outcomes.
- Bring the spouse or decision-maker to the meeting whenever possible.
- Have prospects circle the times they'll work out before you pitch (heads off the time objection).
- Don't prejudge ability to pay ("equal opportunity salesman"). Budget walk-throughs only work with real rapport.
- If belief is the problem, fix the product and onboarding first; scripts won't matter.
- Record every sales call.
- The higher up the business world you go, the more buyers are logical rather than emotional [his observation].
- Gym owners who can close can afford the most expensive leads and charge higher prices. NFL stat he cites: top-5 red-zone offenses make the playoffs 90% of the time.

## Playbook steps
1. Reset your intention before each call: human first, not the sale.
2. Before the offer, uncover the real goal and destroy obstacles, and line up proof (testimonials).
3. Present the offer and expect a no.
4. Stay curious, never argue; ask "what are the main criteria you're thinking through?"
5. Classify the objection (time / value / fit / authority / avoidance) and expect more layers under it.
6. Use the matching reframe, peeling the onion down to the self.
7. At avoidance, run past → present (decision framework) → future pacing.
8. Finish with the risk-free / closer-or-further close. If they still decline after an empowered decision, no resentment.

## Mistakes he warns against
- "Lazy selling": leaning on your own story, so you only close people like you.
- Commission breath; arguing to be right ("no one wins an argument").
- Reps losing belief as they learn the product's flaws; they then stop pushing at the close.
- Getting rattled by "no" or "I need to think about it."
- Prejudging prospects by appearance.
- Not documenting sales (his regret: ~4,000 unrecorded sales).
- Emotional-only closes that leave buyers regretting it once the feeling is gone.

## Examples / case details
- Mortgage-lead sales floor turnaround: asked a rep "how good are the leads?" The hesitation showed no belief. He tore up the script he'd rewritten; the team then "closed a bunch."
- Gym Launch had ~1,600 testimonials counted about two years earlier. He recommends a floor-to-ceiling testimonial wall ("takes an afternoon").
- Proudest closes: single moms with four kids driving Uber on weekends.
- A woman who'd had a sales call with him ~6 years earlier was in the same spot, regretting not joining.
- He calls his move from a consulting job to sleeping on the floor ~10 years ago his "do or die" decision.
- Says he would use a hypothetical-based process in his upcoming sales book.
