## Core thesis
Six problems keep small businesses stuck: too many avatars, underpricing, overcompensation, shiny objects (the "woman in the red dress"), overextension, and no data. The fix is to narrow, reprice, rework comp, get better instead of bigger, find the "who," and track everything. A portfolio-company case shows it: fixing the back end (team, data, renewals, ascensions) took profit from 5% to 30% before any scaling. "Build businesses back to front."

## Frameworks & models
- **Too many avatars** (common under $3M/yr): saying yes to anyone with a credit card makes marketing, sales training and delivery harder. Narrow the front end (call-out, lead magnet). "Free report on the hair industry" draws everyone; "first 5 cut & color clients" draws stylists; "fill all 10 chairs in 30 days" draws salon owners. Narrower messaging gets a higher response, and LTV:CAC can improve. He would invest in a vertical agency (painters only).
- **Price raise sequence**: new customers first, then existing ones. Existing-customer letter: brace email ("big news tomorrow") → remind them of the value delivered → state the increase plainly → say it's to keep your promises and reinvest, framed as what's in it for them → soften it with a loyalty discount equal to their current price for 3–6 months (a disappearing discount stings less than a raise, and the pain moves to the future) → P.S. "if this materially hurts you, call me" (then extend, ratchet up slowly, or peel off service). For communities, turn comments off and "keep this IRL." The letter template is in Gym Launch Secrets.
- **Overcompensation fix** (top-line rev share with no responsibility for overhead, CAC or retention, e.g. 50/50 with therapists): mirror the price-raise steps with employees. Brace them; remind them of the value; admit "the business isn't profitable, my mistake"; give long-tenured staff 3–6 months' notice; show how they can earn the same by doing more. They have 3 options: accept, leave within the notice period, or take on more work. Test the new comp on new hires first; that sets precedent and gives you backups.
- **Hire break-even math**: $1M revenue at 25% margin = $250K profit. A $100K hire eats 40% of it and needs $400K in new revenue to break even. "Overhead walks on two legs": you'd demo $100K software 7 times but hire after one lunch.
- **"The swamp" ($1–3M)**: either kill profit betting on a hire who frees you to make rain, or work two jobs until profit covers them.
- **Woman in the red dress**: shiny objects carry "uninformed optimism." Leila's test: are the things you stress your team about missed opportunities, or problems? "You will grow bigger by getting better."
- **Value = claim minus friction**: once the claim is wanted, everything else is removing delay, effort, sacrifice, risk and inconsistency. A phone that fails 1 in 20 times is hated. "The iPhone is what's left after you remove everything that sucks from a phone." List the problems and solve #1 fully before #2.
- **Overextension and the "who"**: expanding without a good-enough who (someone to own the new thing or take over the current one) is greed or envy. Litmus test: the business runs 6 months without you and holds or grows. Level 1: 2–4 weeks of vacation with your phone off. Level 2: a co-owner deal for the manager (e.g. 2% a year for 5 years up to 10%, plus profit share now) in exchange for "this phone doesn't ring" (a pipe bursts → they call a plumber).
- **Measurement as intervention**: daily weighing alone causes weight loss; tracking profit or close rate improves them. "If you don't track, you don't care." "Data is dope."
- **Two pipelines to track**: (1) click→close: CTR, traffic, opt-in, schedule, show, offer and close rates, cash collected. (2) close→resell: activation, time to value, churn, gross profit per product line, ascension by cohort. (3) Then lead source by channel.
- **Activation point**: find what retained customers did early and make everyone do it on purpose. Orangetheory: 5 workouts in the first month. Gym Launch: a gym that made a $2,000 sale within its first 7 days was worth 3× as much.
- **Onboarding**: "if you take one thing from this whole video," it's onboarding. Restate the customer's goals several times, then tie them to the actions that drive time to value.
- **Delete to improve**: overwhelm is the #1 churn reason across industries. List every feature and survey: "if we got rid of everything except one, which would you keep?" The 301-level move: quietly remove the lowest-ranked item and see who complains.
- **NPS vs CSAT**: NPS runs from -100 to +100; 0–6 count -1, 7–8 count 0, 9–10 count +1. Weight NPS higher. Average company about -2, Apple 61, Chick-fil-A 58.
- **Ascension metrics**: qualified ascension % (quality) and total ascension % (economics). For example, 80% of qualified customers ascend but only 10% of all customers do → you're selling unqualified customers or the core product isn't getting them qualified.
- **Churn math**: 5% monthly churn loses more than half your customers a year. Two businesses each reach 300 customers in 3 years, one with 0% renewal and one with 100%; the zero-renewal one must sell 400 in year 4 just to grow 25%.
- **Open-loop ascension timing**: offer upsells while the loop is open: within 24 hrs, at time to value, at the halfway point, at the first win, at the big win. The last chance is the worst time, yet most people try then. His 6-week weight-loss challenges had 3 upsell points: 24 hrs, halfway and end.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Raising price: doubling it while close rate falls from 30% to 20% is "a great deal." Make it a math problem. For one-time purchases, just raise; if close rate drops too far, back down one step or add a bonus.
- Choose the avatar with the best LTV:CAC relative to market size. Cut the other front ends to the best ROAS, or bridge to them.
- Profit share on the bottom line (e.g. a manager getting 10% of profit) is fine; top-line rev share for high-skilled labor is a red flag unless it's equity.
- If you need an add-on to be profitable, fix the core (unless the model was built that way, like burgers + soda).
- Pay CS commissions only where there's a real sales motion. A percentage of a book of business breeds complacency.
- Match job requirements to pay ("10 years of experience at $50K won't happen").
- Leaders must know "where the bodies are buried" in every report's department; if you don't, you're too far away.
- Don't scale ad spend on a broken back end. "If your stuff sucks you want as few people as possible to know." Fix the bucket, then pour. Stabilizing takes 6–24 months.

## Playbook steps
1. Narrow to the best avatar. 2. Raise price on new customers, then run the existing-customer letter. 3. Reset comp, testing on new hires first. 4. Fix product friction instead of adding new things. 5. Only expand when there's a "who" and the 6-month test is passed. 6. Install tracking (two pipelines). 7. Back-end fix: find the activation point → onboarding → engagement/NPS → churn → client goals → renewal process → milestone-based ascension journey with CSM set bonuses and closer commissions.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Saying yes to anyone with money.
- Pricing at the market average.
- Top-line rev shares; hiring after one lunch.
- Arbitrary revenue goals; opening a 2nd or 3rd location early (3 locations, 3× the work, $0 profit).
- Thinking "it runs without me" because you stepped out of sales and delivery.
- Adding bodies to solve problems. People hire dumber than themselves and delegate down until the most important work lands with the least competent person.
- Blaming customers ("complaining is not a strategy").
- Pitching ascension only at the end.

## Examples / case details
- **Portfolio company (higher-ticket B2B, tens to ~100 units a month)**:
  - Before: 5% profit, about 2% revenue retention, 0–1 ascensions a month.
  - People: delivery was underpaid; the CS leader turned the team against the founder (an existential risk). They hired an experienced CS leader, then ran "green dye" dilution, paying two teams briefly while new hires (better pay, 3+ years' experience, PTO and benefits) pushed out weak ones. About 90% of the baseline team ended up exiting or fired.
  - Data: they tracked close rates by closer, period and cohort, plus TTV, time to onboarding, engagement, NPS/CSAT, churn and client goals.
  - Renewals: a notes system so customers never repeat themselves; renewals framed around their goals; the live events where renewals already happened made regular and choreographed.
  - Ascensions: all of them had come from one superstar closer texting customers randomly. They built milestone triggers, with CSMs paid for sets and closers paid commission. Ascensions went to 5–6 a month (≈6×), adding about $2M in revenue.
  - After: profit went from 5% to 30%, and revenue retention from 2% to 30%. "I don't think this took more than 90 days."
- **Gym Launch**: deleted a 35-person real-time tech support department after a survey ranked it near the bottom. Churn and sales didn't change.
- **Carl Icahn story**: he fired an 11-story building of staff, and "nothing" happened.
- **Gym Launch client**: two months from closing, full within 30 days, signed a second lease by day 90.
