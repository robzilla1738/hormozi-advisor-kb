# 10 KEYS to a TERRIBLE Business Partnership [GUARANTEED!]

## Core thesis
Using Munger-style inversion ("how would I guarantee a terrible partnership?"), the video argues that a partner must bring knowledge, money, or time you don't have. Expectations must be fair and written down. Mission, values, and lifestyle must line up, because "when you're in a business relationship you're in a marriage." Equity is the most expensive thing you can give away, so don't hand it out to cover a need you could simply pay for.

## Frameworks & models
- **Inversion**: list the ways to guarantee failure, then do the opposite.
- **Big three of partnerships: knowledge / money / time**. The partner must have at least one you lack. If both of you have the same experience, knowledge, time, and money, "one of you isn't required."
- **Expectations → agreements**: expectations should be fair, well understood, and documented.
- **Mission / values / lifestyle alignment**: mission is where you're going, values are how you get there and how you treat people, and lifestyle covers similar interests and money habits (e.g., a Dave Ramsey saver paired with someone who lives above their means and goes into debt).
- **Contract as a dialogue tool**: "we write these contracts with the intention of never having to look at them." The contract exists to force the hard conversations and create shared expectations, not to bring in lawyers.
- **Reliability chain**: an unreliable partner leads to an unreliable relationship, which leads to an unreliable business.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- A need doesn't call for equity. If you can pay someone (e.g., an accountant) for the service, pay them instead of making them a partner.
- Give equity only to people who will really drive growth, and judge the deal 10 years out.
- If a would-be partner says "I don't do agreements," walk away.
- If you can't have hard conversations before any money or equity is at stake, you won't manage them at the end.
- Litmus test: given the same data, would you both make the same decision? If yes, expect far less conflict.
- Look for complementary skills with aligned mission, values, and interests.
- You should be *proud* to introduce your partner to others, not merely okay with it.
- Don't squeeze a partner, and don't over-give and overwork yourself at the start. Either one breeds resentment and later renegotiation. Thinking a few steps ahead and giving the other side more builds a relationship rather than a negotiated contract.

## Playbook steps
1. Check that they bring knowledge, money, or time you lack. 2. Ask whether you could pay for that instead of giving equity. 3. Have the hard conversations: who does what, and what happens if it goes wrong. 4. Document the agreement and keep it fair. 5. Confirm you share a mission, values, and lifestyle. 6. Run the same-data/same-decision test.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Partnering with someone who has the same knowledge and experience (e.g., coworkers from the same job starting the same business), which gives away half the equity for no extra benefit.
- Mismatched time commitment.
- Unspoken expectations and handshake deals.
- Lopsided early terms that lead to resentment.
- Partners who disagree on where the business should go. He says many partner pairs on calls with his team can't agree on direction.
- Messy personal lives, which "trickle into your business."

## Examples / case details
- Early on, someone told him they were the "handshake type," and he didn't want to be "that guy." He now says he doesn't trust people that much: "make sure the paper says what we need it to say, but then trust the person."
- Banks don't hand out money without written expectations.
