# The Talent Grid - How I Recognize Skill in Self & Team

## Core thesis
Talent (you, an employee, a department) moves through three levels: What → How → Who. First you do the work (individual contributor), then you teach it (manager), then you systematize it, "building the machine that builds the machine" (leader). Grade yourself, each person and each department on this grid to find the bottleneck. Your strengths are usually that bottleneck, because those are the things you hand off last.

## Frameworks & models
- **Three levels of talent**:
  - Level 1, *What*: individual contributor, spends time **doing**.
  - Level 2, *How*: manager, spends time **teaching** others to execute the sequence consistently. Managers don't need to do the work better than an IC, but they must understand how it works.
  - Level 3, *Who*: leader, spends time **systematizing**. He deliberately says "not delegating, systematizing": building something that does the teaching for you.
- **Department grading**: for each department (marketing, sales, product, shared services such as IT, legal and HR), ask whether you are still doing it, still teaching it, or have a machine that builds the people. Example: sales at level 3 means bringing in 1–3 salespeople a month and running them through "the machine that builds salespeople."
- **Quarterly review sheet**: do people know what to do (base skill)? Do we have the how (step-by-step process plus accountability)? Is someone systematizing so the skill is recreated over and over?
- **The "truly delegated" test**: he is not in that company's Slack, only one person can reach him, he has one meeting a week with the operator, and the company keeps growing between meetings without him.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- He says leaders (level 3) are usually needed around ~$3M/yr to break through to $1M+/month ($10M+/yr).
- Your phone blowing up means the business is not systematized, even if you don't have to answer right away. Any decision that still requires you counts as involvement.
- Don't assume managers are leaders, whatever their tenure. Fast-growth companies often have to swap out the people in charge (he credits the point about how hard it is to jump levels to Reid Hoffman).
- Great ICs are often poor leaders, and great leaders can be poor ICs. Assess each level separately.
- Discount founders' "my team is amazing" claims. He says ~95% say it, then tell horror stories and praise a different team 9 months later.

## Playbook steps
1. Place yourself on the grid (doing / teaching / systematizing).
2. Place each team member and their potential level.
3. Grade each department by your level of involvement.
4. Move each department up: hand off the doing, then build the teaching machine.
5. Repeat every quarter until you only provide vision and strategic decisions.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Claiming your business is "totally systematized" while you are still pulled into decisions.
- Keeping your strengths for yourself. You give away what you don't know easily, and what you're good at becomes the bottleneck.
- Fixed identity: people stall at a level because of beliefs about themselves. "Identity is very fluid."
- Not working on your own character. People follow someone they aspire to be like, not someone they feel they know more than.

## Examples / case details
- His track record as stated in the video: ~6 years to his first 8-figure company, then 1 year, then 6 months for later ones; >$120M in sales across industries.
- Portfolio companies are run through one operator with one meeting a week.
