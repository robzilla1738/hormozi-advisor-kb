# What I Learned After Countless Failed Business Partnerships

## Core thesis
This is a live member Q&A call. Alex says he has had eight failed partnerships. His view: only take a partner who brings skills, money or time you don't have. Usually a performance agreement (revenue or profit share, or phantom equity) beats giving away real equity. When you need to end a partnership, say directly what you'd otherwise say behind their back, then split equity and debt using simple documents.

## Frameworks & models
- **The only reasons to take a partner:** they have skills, money or time you don't have. "I'm cool, you're cool, we love the same thing" is a terrible reason, because one of you is unnecessary and you just dilute your equity.
- **The only two reasons anyone wants equity:** control, or a payout when the company sells. Otherwise "equity sucks," since it mostly brings liability.
- **Phantom equity:** equity that pays out only if the company is sold, and is forfeited if the person leaves. It can be paired with profit sharing.
- **"You just pay the accountant"** (from an early mentor): needing a skill doesn't mean that person should own part of the company.
- **Separation in two steps:** (1) equity: they transfer their shares to you, or you dissolve the company and form a new one; (2) debt: split what you both owe, with a promissory note back to you for their share.
- **Hard-conversation hack:** "the way that you talk [ __ ] about someone behind their back is literally what you should just tell them." Lay out the facts, then ask "what do you think's fair?"

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Before signing, talk through "all the ugly [ __ ]" while you're still in the honeymoon "lust" phase: exit strategy, who gets what in a breakup ("alimony"), roles and responsibilities.
- If you won't sell the company and they don't need control, no equity is needed. Use an agreement instead. Ask them directly what they want. A profit share is better for you, a revenue share is better for them.
- Performance kickers: equity unlocks at revenue milestones (in the caller's plan, up to about a third of the company). Alex suggested phantom equity for this.
- Benchmark: full-service e-commerce marketing firms take about 10% of top-line revenue attributed to their marketing, with no equity.
- A promissory note can be one page: borrower, lender, amount, payment schedule, interest (or none), what happens on default. Alex says he has done million-dollar loans on one page.

## Playbook steps
**Bringing on a partner:**
1. Identify the gap: skill, money or time.
2. Ask what they really want (control, an exit payout, or a share of what they bring in).
3. Structure a revenue or profit share, or phantom equity with milestones.
4. Define roles and how you'd split up before you start.

**Exiting a partner:**
1. State the facts plainly.
2. Ask "what do you think's fair?"
3. Have them transfer equity, or dissolve the company.
4. Sign a promissory note for their share of the debt.
5. Optionally let them keep selling for a commission.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Taking partners just because you get along. Alex himself took on "partners left and right" early on.
- Giving equity to fill a functional gap such as lead generation.
- Avoiding the breakup terms during the honeymoon.
- Worrying about the ex-partner getting rich on your product. "He wasn't getting rich on your product for the last two years."

## Examples / case details
- Jason has a 10+ year-old business (an S-corp) where lead generation was always the weakest function. He considered giving Kyle up to a third of the company at milestones. Alex said to skip real equity: use a performance agreement or phantom equity.
- Peter's partner owned sales but brought in nothing for six months while the LLC still had 50/50 debt. Alex's advice: Peter takes the business and the ex-partner pays his half of the debt over time on a note. Dissolving instead would save about $300.
