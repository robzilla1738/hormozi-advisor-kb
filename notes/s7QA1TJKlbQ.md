## Core thesis
You can beat 99% of competitors if you win clearly on ONE of four competitive "vectors": **speed, risk (reliability/reputation), price (cheap), ease**. Winning on several lets you dominate. He says the four are the value equation under other names. Pick the vector your customer values most, build the business around it from day one, and lead your marketing with that single angle.

## Frameworks & models
- **Four vectors / strategic moats**: Speed (faster), Risk (more reliable, consistent, reputable), Price (cheaper and still profitable), Ease (remove everything hard). He says strategy has to "ladder up" to one of these.
- **Mapping to the value equation**: risk = perceived likelihood of achievement; ease = effort & sacrifice; speed = time delay; cheaper = he maps it to "dream outcome" as said in the video [inference: the mapping of price is loose].
- **Speed** is the most important vector, because behavior is trained by low latency (likes, real-time pay; per-mile trucker pay was banned because drivers wouldn't stop). Sub-vectors: time from purchase to receipt, and time per recurring use (a 10-min workout beats 1 hour for the same result). Three levers:
  1. **Templates**: ads, emails, landing pages, presentations, steps. Removing decisions speeds things up because "decision-making is typically the slowest part" of an organization.
  2. **Pre-made**: McDonald's (The Founder); Panini Cafe grilling chicken before the lunch rush. Waste is outweighed by faster table turns.
  3. **Availability**: more bookable hours lifts conversion and willingness to pay. Better staffing of hours "almost always" drives 20–40% more through a business. Acquisition.com sells 12 h/day, 7 days a week, and is moving toward 20 h/day.
- **Risk** has three parts:
  1. Consistency/reliability after purchase (the cleaner "Rose" who has never missed a day in 10 years beats a competitor 20% cheaper).
  2. Reputation before purchase (word of mouth; negative word of mouth is "like 10 times as viral").
  3. Guarantees as the short-term fix.
- **Four guarantee types**: unconditional (good when starting); conditional (needs stakes, "here's my watch/shirt"); implied, meaning performance-based ("winners always want to compete on performance"); and anti-guarantee once reputation exists (McDonald's doesn't guarantee the burger). Reputation shrinks the "pile of risk" over time.
- **Control variables**: "If many variables exist, many variables must be studied" (attributed to B.F. Skinner). His team keeps a list of about 100 "golden BBs" for a good video and adds conditions as they learn them.
- **Cheap, by design from day one**, using three levers: AI (gives the best employees about 10x leverage, so pay them more), plain automation, and offshoring/nearshoring. Being cheapest makes sales and marketing easy; the hard part is profit.
- **Ease = subtraction**: easy is not something you add. You list what is hard (customers will tell you) and cross items off one at a time. The iPhone is "what remains" after removing what sucks about a phone. Audit every step from click to close to delivered.
- **Monikers**: "the best for the most for the least"; "unique, expensive, sticky, air" (no one else can do it, they keep buying, high gross margin, recurring).
- **Tech vs labor**: having all four vectors usually requires tech. A labor or service business can typically get three.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- You only need one vector. Choose the one the customer cares most about: speed if they value speed, risk if a failure is very costly, ease if the task is a big pain.
- Market ONE core vector. "Customers do not understand multiple benefits" in messaging.
- Don't respond to this by just lowering your price ("a terrible decision"). Cheapness must be the day-one strategy built into every step.
- A small-business marketing agency is usually a bad business because of high churn, except when it is extremely cheap: $100–300/mo for services others charge about $2,000/mo for, so clients won't cancel even in their worst month.
- "Fast beats free": in a market with cheap or free competitors, win on speed and risk (Spotify vs LimeWire/Napster).
- Using AI, automation or offshore staff doesn't need to be disclosed. "Don't say 'we're an AI design firm.' Just be a design firm."
- Niche down so delivery can be templatized and faster.

## Playbook steps
1. Start with the customer and work backwards; scan the competitive landscape.
2. Pick the vector they value most, and possibly secondary ones.
3. Operationalize it. Speed: templates, pre-made, availability. Risk: a variable checklist for consistency, build reputation, guarantee in the short term. Cheap: AI, automation and offshoring from day one. Ease: list the hard things and remove them one by one.
4. Make your sales process show the vector: reps take notes and pass context ("Charlie told me your revenue is X... does that sound right?"); call leads within 30 seconds.
5. On the back end, design onboarding, activation points, touch points and reporting. Example: a massage business records pain maps so any therapist knows the client, which also stops staff from taking customers with them when they leave.
6. Lead your marketing with that single angle.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Trying to win on everything in your messaging.
- Cutting price reactively.
- Making customers repeat information across multiple calls.
- Underestimating how many variables affect a customer encounter.
- Assuming negative word of mouth isn't raising your CAC when CPMs haven't moved.
- Thinking guarantees are the only way to reverse risk.
- Running a business where knowledge lives in individual staff, which makes it not sticky.
