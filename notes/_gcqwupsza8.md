## Core thesis
Alex explains how he decided to sell majority (2/3) stakes in Gym Launch, Prestige Labs and a third company (ALAN), for over $50M total; two were at a $46.2M valuation. The process took about 18 months to 2.5 years of deliberation. He worked through seven variables (story, money, energy/headspace, team/resources, future vision, best for the business, final call) as a devil's-advocate dialogue. Money was a wash either way. He sold because "anything that distracts from my Berkshire Hathaway [Acquisition.com] will be a net loss," and because he expected regret from paths not taken more than from failed execution.

## Frameworks & models
- **Seven sell/keep variables**: (1) the story you tell yourself internally and externally; (2) personal money; (3) energy and headspace; (4) team and resources gained or lost; (5) future vision; (6) what's best for the business; (7) the decision and why.
- **The "delta" test for selling**: sell if what the cash earns when reinvested, plus what you build next, beats what the retained stake (the 2/3 sold) would be worth in about 5 years.
- **Own many, run one**: "you can only have one active thing." Passive holdings (a building, Apple stock) are fine. "Make sure that what you're owning is not owning you."
- **Match the conditions** (from his closest friend): instead of the "nuclear option," picture life after the sale and create those conditions now while keeping the company. He rejected it because he couldn't stop thinking about the company, comparing it to kids still taking up "shower time."
- **"Buy it from yourself"**: run the private-equity playbook and incentives yourself and keep 100%. He judged his resistance to this as fear-driven.
- **Soft decision frameworks**: (a) acknowledge there's no wrong answer, since he'd be wealthy and would rationalize either way; (b) the boy-and-horse parable, "time will tell"; (c) bird in the hand vs two in the bush, his three hardest decisions; (d) "What am I most afraid of?"; (e) the 85-year-old-self test, which he calls his most effective decision framework.
- **Buffett contradiction**: "never sell wholly owned companies" vs "get into the best boat." He calls both valid.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Bad reasons to sell, in his own self-rebuttals: needing to be "legit," a rite of passage, proving a model is sellable, proving net worth, "F you" to doubters, and applying the wrong lesson from a past sale.
- "Diversification is a hedge against ignorance." If you know your space, concentrate.
- "Rich people sell, wealthy people never sell." He lists this as an argument for keeping.
- Don't use debt to fund M&A you don't know how to do or lack the lender relationships for. Don't "bet the empire for a pot of gold."
- Bankers inflate valuations to get you to sign with them.
- For a sellable asset: separate the brand from the founder's face and add a second acquisition channel. His banker gave this feedback, and buyers in the first round said the same.
- Regret from untaken paths is more certain than regret from failed execution. If you believe your life can be bigger, you're guaranteed not to get there without big jumps.

## Playbook steps
Pre-sale de-risking, as he did it in 2020–21:
1. Build an outbound call center as a second channel; it reached 50% of sales volume.
2. Promote client gym owners to be the face of the consulting brand.
3. Founder stops attending product meetings, client calls and client events.
4. Founder's role shrinks to a weekly CEO 1:1, a 90-min weekly exec call and quarterly internal meetups.
5. Keep the sale process running through the trough; PE buyers bid at the bottom.
6. Sign the LOI; interest rose from 2 to 4 parties as the business rebounded.

## Mistakes he warns against
- **Optimizing for a hypothetical acquirer instead of customers.** Decisions shifted from "long-term goodwill with our customers" to "what would a potential acquirer think," and he believes it hurt the business.
- **Cutting price to cut churn.** He lowered prices so an acquirer would see lower churn. He says it cost about $5M in profit: profit went from $17M on $26M revenue to $13.4M on $37M, when he says it "should have" been about $19M on $37M. [inference: arithmetic check: revenue +42% and profit −21% ($3.6M lower than the prior year, ~$5.6M below his $19M expectation); he states "+40%" and "shrunk by 25%."]
- Hiring a lot of people to "look legit" who "sit around and do nothing" (peaked at about 135 employees across two entities).
- Splitting attention: revenue flatlined when he started a second and third company.
- After the $150M banker call, about 50% or more of his mental bandwidth went to the sale for 2.5 years, and growth plateaued.

## Examples / case details
- March 2019: $4.4M revenue and $1.7M owner dividends in one month. Leila: "Let's shut it down… We don't need the money."
- Banker Mike Bakumian: "You grew from 0 to $17 million in profit in two years… I can sell this for 150 million easily," and more if the business were de-faced from Alex and had a second channel. Alex was 28 with about $20M saved.
- Went to market Feb 2020. COVID cut quarterly profit from $3–4M to about $500K in Q4 2020. Bankers suggested pausing; they pushed on and got 2 offers at the bottom, then more as 2021 rebounded. The final price was $46.2M; the third company's price is undisclosed (it did $12M revenue the year before).
- He had already taken about $45M in dividends, so the sale "is not really going to materially change" their living, and the tax hit made it roughly net-worth neutral.
- His three hardest decisions, in order: quitting his job to start a gym, selling the gyms to start Gym Launch, and selling these companies for Acquisition.com.
- After the sale he felt the loss of resources but "didn't feel the gain of the money." The relief lasted about 3 days.
- Acquisition.com's criteria, as stated: companies with $1–10M profit in e-learning or services, brick-and-mortar chains that want to license or franchise, or niche business-service firms, scaled to $30–50M+. Offer is the first lever they pull.
