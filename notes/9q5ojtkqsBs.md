# How to Win With AI in 2026

## Core thesis
AI will never be worse than it is now, so learning it is the top priority for Main Street businesses, not just tech. Stop thinking in roles and start thinking in workflows: break each job into tasks and automate them one by one. Train agents the way you'd train an employee, by defining what good looks like. Prices lag cost drops, so AI-first businesses can keep incumbent pricing at a fraction of the cost, with huge operational leverage. Use a barbell strategy: go fully AI-first on one side, and bet on things that won't change on the other.

## Frameworks & models
- **Adaptability wins**: "the most flexible system survives." You risk more by not adopting than through the rare downsides; an agent misusing permissions is like getting hacked once and swearing off the internet.
- **20 hours to proficiency, but people delay the first hour for decades.** Spend one weekend building an agent.
- **Role-based → workflow-based thinking**: for every hire you're considering, list the 4–10 concrete things the person does, and ask whether each could be a workflow instead of headcount ("hire an editor" becomes "what are the five things an editor does to create a video?"). An org chart organizes humans; the ideal is a linear manufacturing line from inputs to outputs.
- **Automate your own job**: spend ~20% of your time trying to put yourself out of a job. A friend spun up a division whose mission is to put his larger business out of business.
- **BYOS / BYOA (bring your own software/agents)**: one person with agents can be "your entire marketing department." He cites reports that Anthropic has one marketer. This can be sold as an agency, a role with equity, or for higher pay. Erase "titleism."
- **Train AI like a human, through reinforcement**: "taste" is pattern recognition plus feedback. Remove intangible words and define outputs. Example: give it 12 rules plus 16 writing samples, for roughly 5× better copy. 100 feedback cycles take ~1.5 years with a person and ~100 minutes with AI.
- **Humans + better tech beat humans + worse tech** (Stone → Bronze → Iron Age). Compete against humans with tools, never against the machine.
- **Endgame**: when labor and intelligence cost approaches zero, the last thing humans get paid for is taking risk.
- **Barbell strategy**: (high risk) fully AI-native, raise the bar, and cut automated roles; (safe) Bezos-style bets on what won't change: bodies/health, fitness, food, supplements, housing, and entertainment (more leisure time).
- **Price lag arbitrage**: buyers used to $2,000/mo have labor costs built into that price. Deliver it at $50 or $5 instead of $500, for huge margin and fewer people per dollar of revenue. Coordination between humans is a major scaling cost.
- **Porn as a leading indicator** of tech adoption (AI avatars plus chatbots).
- **"Phase shift"** analogy from Bryan Johnson: the water boils, so swimming skill stops mattering.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- When a role gets automated, don't invent work for "Danny." Raise the company-wide bar; those who meet it stay.
- Don't judge an agent by its first bad output; train it.
- Start an AI-first company now, because incumbents are too busy running their businesses. He says his own day-one AI-first companies have revenue per employee "in the millions."
- Prepare for sunshine and rain. In the doomsday scenario, nothing matters anyway.

## Playbook steps
1. List everything you do daily at the most granular level (e.g., "run ads" → campaigns, budgets, analysis, creative, copy, landing-page and headline tests).
2. Take the first task to an AI: "Help me automate this. What steps would you take?"
3. Do the first step. When stuck, screenshot the screen and ask "What do I do now?"
4. For each hire request, list its tasks and convert each to a workflow before adding headcount.
5. Give agents rules plus examples, and iterate the feedback loop many times.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Saying "AI-first" without being AI-first, while keeping a large org chart.
- Avoiding hard conversations about automated roles.
- Short-term thinking about the learning cost, the same mistake as refusing to train an employee.
- Prompting with no rules or samples and then calling it "AI slop."
- Fear-driven inaction (e.g., not making AI films that could be "all margin").
