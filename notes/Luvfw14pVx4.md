## Core thesis
Bias exists and always will, because it is how humans learn from patterns. Proving a bias is "good for society, bad for the individual." Life is "a single player game," so assume nothing will change and ask "what would it take?" Then overcompensate until you are undeniable. Blaming bias protects your ego, while "I'm not good enough yet" is the only explanation you can act on. Includes a Skool Games promo.

## Frameworks & models
- **Bias as learning**: the red faces / green faces tipping analogy. Wishing for no bias is wishing humans didn't learn.
- **Two possibilities**: (1) The bias is real and you don't fit it. So what? You've proven a disadvantage and your life isn't better. (2) The bias is accurate about you (immature, inconsistent). Then fix it.
- **Overcompensate / be better than the bias**: his college friend over-tipped to counter a stereotype. Breaking the stereotype makes you stand out more ("this one is John and John's cool").
- **"What would it take?"**: ask it for a promotion, a raise, a team spot, or a deal.
- **Volume negates luck**: find the best performer and do twice as much as he does.
- **A bigger disadvantage makes a more compelling story** (e.g., Oprah).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Assume the bias won't change; control only your own performance.
- If you're behind, do the best person's work plus another full day's work.
- If one person won't deal with you because of bias, move on; other people will.
- [inference] For owners and managers: when someone blames bias, redirect them to the objective bar: "what would it take?"

## Playbook steps
1. Admit the bias may be real, and that it may also be accurate about you.
2. Ask the decision-maker what objectively it would take.
3. Benchmark the top performer and do twice their volume.
4. Leave no room for doubt that you earned it. Accept starting "6 feet underground."
5. Keep moving to other buyers if someone refuses.

## Mistakes he warns against
- A victim mentality and using bias as an excuse ("no single person has ever gotten better by proving that they were the victim").
- Believing the bias yourself, which "make[s] it true."
- Quitting a job over perceived bias while keeping the excuse.

## Examples / case details
- Jacob, a 15-year-old neighbor, was failing at wholesaling because he "sounded like a child." Alex got him an interview at Gym Launch. Doing 2× the best rep's volume, Jacob became the top outbound setter, then the top qualifier. At 18 the sales manager made him self-generate and close three deals end-to-end, a test no one else got. He became the top closer.
- Alex looked too young at his early gym, so he claimed to be the owner. A penthouse neighbor called him "Beverly Hillbillies."
- A portfolio company conflict where a "racial sensitivity" complaint was really personal dislike.
- A restaurant on the "wrong side of town" can still win with the best pizza or curry; the bar is just higher.
- Leila gets less credit as a female co-CEO and wife; she just has to be better (and "loves it").
