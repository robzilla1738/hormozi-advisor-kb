# Why I chose to disappoint my dad
id: To8jcTDwcxc / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=To8jcTDwcxc / duration: 848s (~14 min) / tags: 13-quit-or-pivot, 12-mindset-leverage, 01-what-business-to-start
Signal: low

## Core thesis
Interview clip (Alex is the guest; the host is unnamed). Alex left a prestigious management-consulting job at 23 against his immigrant father's wishes. Using mortality as his decision lens ("die to myself or die to my father"), he concluded most decisions don't matter, legacy is illusory, and other people's expectations are chains you can drop. He uses this as a reusable decision algorithm, plus inversion ("what would destroy my life?") to fix what you hate first.

## Frameworks & models
- **Mortality lens** as a routine decision tool: it shows that most decisions don't matter and removes other people from the playbook.
- **Subjective well-being "smiley face" curve** (as he describes it): high at 7, drops sharply from 20–30, lowest around 45, rises to 55–60, then shoots up near death when people stop worrying about others' opinions.
- **Legacy test (go backwards)**: do you know your great-great-great-grandfather? His great-great-grandfather was Iranian ruling class with ~400 children. Alex can't remember his name, and the revolution seized the land, buildings and accounts.
- **Logical vs emotional decision**: the logical case was that ~$200K over 2 years (the economic equivalent of an MBA) would teach more and might yield a business earning as much as the job offer. The emotional case was that he'd prefer not to be alive if this was his life, which opened up his decision calculus.
- **Reuse good decision algorithms** as many times as possible.
- **Inversion**: it's easier to name what you hate than what brings joy. Ask what would give you the worst possible life, then reverse it. Fix the hated job, relationship or city first.
- **Context of advice**: parental advice reflects their era and lifestyle. His father later apologized: "to be fair, in my time, I would have been right."
- Joy (internal) vs happiness (circumstance).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you know a path doesn't make you happy, you can leave before you know what does ("I didn't know what made me happy. I knew [this] was not making me happy.").
- Try options on for size rather than demanding certainty first.
- [inference] Weigh advice by the era and circumstances of the person giving it.

## Playbook steps
None as a business process. His path: quit the job → sold everything, drove to California for fitness → mentored under someone for a few months → opened his first facility and slept on the floor for 9 months.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Living the life others expect (approval and validation seeking).
- Building for "legacy".
- Overweighting what people think: "in our 60s we realize no one was thinking about us to begin with" (quoted by the host).

## Examples / case details
- Background: graduated college in 3 years, club president, management consultant in space, cyber and intelligence for the military (top-secret clearance). Quit after 2 years, at 23.
- Father: born in Iran, an only parent raising an only child, came to the US with $1,000 and a medical degree, didn't speak English. Education let him leave the country. Alex: "what he did was harder than what I've done."
- Early drive was all fear: failure, disappointment, judgment. He was contemplating not living at the time.
- His father's line: "You only get one name, so invest in it accordingly."
- Book opening: "there are no rules."
