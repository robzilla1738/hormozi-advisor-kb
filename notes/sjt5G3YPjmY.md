## Core thesis
Businesses under $10M fail or stay unsellable because of four concentration risks: **key-man risk, single-channel risk, key-customer risk, single-vendor risk**. Each is a single point of failure for dollars getting "across the bridge." Fixing them (he says fixing key-man risk let him sell for $46.2M) turns a high-paying job into a sellable, safer asset. Redundancy looks like waste but is insurance.

## Frameworks & models
- **Key-man risk**: one person whose loss would materially cut revenue or kill the business. It shows up in marketing, sales/conversion, product/delivery, and operations (the "glue", e.g. a leader everyone reports into, like Leila at Acquisition.com). "Key man is key human": it can be an employee. It counts double if the key person is the owner who leaves at sale.
- **Two fixes for key-man risk**: (1) process + people; (2) incentives such as share vesting over 3–5 years. Option 2 only works if the key person isn't you.
- **R&D department (Gym Launch)**, replacing himself as product innovator: identify problems by asking customers and having them rank them → deploy pooled resources (customers paid ~$3–4K/mo; he spent ~$50K/mo on tests, e.g. filming ~30 ads with models) → test in ~20 representative markets (demographics, size, wealth, operator quality), not favorite customers → hand the 2–3 winners to the whole customer base.
- **Document → Demonstrate → Duplicate**: write the checklist; do it in front of them using only the checklist (add anything you do off-list); they do it in front of you. The lower the learner's skill, the more steps the checklist needs.
- **Delegation litmus test**: delegation worked only if the function's performance stays neutral or goes up after handoff. Handing off with no feedback loop is abdication.
- **Supply-constrained solo service, in order**: (1) raise prices; (2) increase the service ratio (1:1 → 1:5/1:10; or a 4-person pod going from 20 to 40 clients); (3) process + people to hand it off, the only step that removes key-man risk.
- **Hiring sources** (from $100M Leads, employees chapter): warm outreach, cold outreach/recruiting, content (job posts), paid ads for job postings, employee referrals, affiliates (associations, guilds, listservs), staffing agencies.
- **Single-channel risk**: more than half of leads/customers come from one place. "If you have only one way to get customers then you have one way to lose them." Volatility matters more as you grow: a 20% channel dip barely touches 1 rep but empties 20 calendars out of 100.
- **Small business channel mix**: one primary cold channel (outbound, affiliates, or ads) + light organic (even 1 post/week) to amplify the others and "look like you have a pulse."
- **Larger business: sequence for adding a channel**: (1) shore up long-term nurture (content + email follow-up to the existing list; he cites 36:1–42:1 email ROI "depending on the source"); (2) build a referral engine (ask several times across the journey); (3) use that cash cushion to invest in a second channel. Expect no results for a while, then a jump.
- **"Don't do two news"**: new channel + known/proven person. Never a new person on a new channel. First transfer the existing channel to someone else without performance dropping, then move yourself to the new channel. An outside expert can be a thought partner.
- **Key-customer risk**: any customer (or related customers) whose loss would drop revenue by 20%+. At typical 10–40% margins, that's half or more of profit.
- **Four paths for a whale**: (1) add more minnows to dilute; (2) lock in a long-term contract (3/5/7 yrs) with bonuses or a discount + breakup fee (~10–20% of the contract, e.g. 1 year on a 5-year deal, or repay the discount) or 6–12 months' notice; (3) **get more whales**, his preferred path ("I'm in the whale business"); (4) decline the whale if it came through no repeatable channel and needs custom pricing or delivery.
- **Single-vendor risk**: an outside vendor owns a key function (SEO, ads, manufacturing, dev, payment processing). Fixes: (1) **redundancy** (insurance; also gives negotiating leverage); (2) **mutually assured destruction** (become a big share of their business so you're their key customer; "owe the bank a billion, you own them"); (3) **covenants & terms**: 6–12 month notice, breakup fee, SLA fines (response time, shipping schedule, ad cadence); (4) advanced: acqui-hire or acquire the vendor once you're much bigger.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Single-channel risk: >50% of leads from one source.
- Key-customer risk: one customer ≥20% of revenue.
- Breakup fee ≈10–20% of contract value; notice period 6–12 months.
- Keep multiple payment processors approved and able to handle full monthly volume at a moment's notice.
- Marketing, sales, and delivery (the core economic engine) should be in-house and understood deeply by the founder. Accounting, HR, and payroll can be outsourced.
- Software company: keep dev in-house ("at least it be key-man risk that you can control").
- Tiny businesses needn't worry about channel risk yet. It matters more as you grow. He reconciles this with "one channel, one avatar, one product to $1M" by calling light organic high-return and low-effort.
- Unicorn talent you can't delegate (Taylor Swift, Elon): align incentives heavily, or create liquidity (borrow against shares) instead of selling.

## Playbook steps
1. Audit for key people across marketing, sales, product, ops. 2. Turn each into process (document/demonstrate/duplicate) + people; vest shares for non-owner key people. 3. Verify delegation by performance. 4. Check channel concentration; build nurture + referrals, then a second channel staffed by a proven person. 5. Check customer concentration (≥20%) and pick one of the four whale paths. 6. List critical vendors (acquisition, conversion, product, processing/banking); add redundancy, terms, and SLA fines.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Thinking you own an asset when you own a high-paying job.
- Restructuring your business around a whale that came from no repeatable channel. The recruiting firm (~$600–700K/mo) was offered +$200K/mo for 20 hires/month with custom demands; he advised declining.
- Putting a new person on a new channel.
- Fully outsourcing core product or dev to one vendor.
- Running on a single payment processor.

## Examples / case details
- Portfolio teeth-whitening chain: outbound platform rule change → 50–70% fewer sales from that channel almost overnight. It was cushioned because recurring revenue had grown from ~5% to 60%+ of revenue, so total revenue held despite new sales dropping by over half.
- Agency owner: 10 clients, one was Google = 70% of revenue.
- Prestige Labs (~$20M/yr): manufacturer took ~$400K of prepaid cash (prepaid for 10–20% discounts) and couldn't deliver. That caused ~6 months of stockouts and ~3-month lead times, which hurt affiliates and reputation.
- ALAN (software, sold 2021): outsourced dev shop raised fees from ~$100K/mo to $200–500K/mo as sales grew to ~$1.7M. He built an in-house team quietly in parallel.
- Payment processor frozen at age 25 (national billing from one California gym). Later a processor charged $150K to export recurring customer data, which he calls "extortion."
- Total exit referenced: $46.2M (Acquisition/Gym Launch + Prestige Labs to American Pacific Group, 2021).
