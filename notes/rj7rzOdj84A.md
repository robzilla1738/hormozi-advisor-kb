# Your Business Is NOT What You Think It Is

## Core thesis
Most businesses plateau because the owner works on the problem they like instead of the "big hairy problem" that actually drives the economics of their business, which is rarely the one they assumed. Entrepreneurs beat the same early "bosses" (usually marketing and sales), hit a new kind of boss, then chase the "woman in the red dress": a new venture where their current skills seem to fit. So they restart at the same revenue ceiling. Find the real business you're in, put a value on what's behind the wall, and push as long as your first-principles assumptions still hold. Pivot only when data disproves one of them.

## Frameworks & models
- **"What business are you really in?"** (his own businesses):
  - Gyms: he thought it was results and workouts. It was really sales and marketing. Even $10/month gyms churn 5–6% a month and survive on volume.
  - Prestige Labs supplements: he thought it was product efficacy. It was really brand, media and distribution. With flavorless capsules it's pure brand and distribution; AG1 keeps customers because it tastes good and becomes a habit.
  - Allan (software): he thought it was marketing and sales. It was really product. Software sells easily if it truly delivers good, fast and cheaper. An outsourced dev shop owned the product and raised fees when they saw the revenue. It was sold all-stock to a strategic buyer.
  - Cleaning: it's really recruiting and training. Customers were easy (LTV high, CAC around $6); the constraint was reliable talent.
  - Consulting/coaching/professional services: really recruiting machines (McKinsey, Bain, BCG, EY, KPMG). The arbitrage is what you pay a person vs what you charge for their expertise. Firms become partnerships so trained stars stay rather than leave with the clients.
  - Investing: really saying no faster. 22 deals in ~2.5 years; 4 produced ~90% of returns.
- **Flip the customer funnel into a talent funnel**: generate leads → applications; sales call → interview; onboarding → employee onboarding; retention → training, management and promotion.
- **Woman in the red dress**: the shiny opportunity that promises a better outcome with your current skills. "Uninformed optimism" hides her problems until you're in bed with her.
- **Levels and bosses**: each stage of growth needs a different skill. People keep re-beating the bosses they already know.
- **Concrete wall + sledgehammer**: put a price on what's behind the wall. "This might be $10M hard to get $200M in enterprise value — that's a steal." You don't know how thick the wall is, so keep swinging.
- **Good hard vs bad hard**: good hard is iterating while your first-principles assumptions still hold (e.g. accountants are on TikTok and can be reached profitably, so debug clicks → opt-ins → lead quality → messaging, targeting and lead magnet). Bad hard is continuing after an assumption has been proven false (e.g. TikTok bans accountants). Then you pivot, and the loss is "learning," the "ignorant tax."
- **A new acquisition channel raises enterprise value twice**: more profit plus lower risk for the buyer, so a higher multiple. Example: $2M EBITDA × 5 = $10M. Adding a second channel might add at least $10M, realistically ~$15M of enterprise value, so losing $100K over 6 months testing TikTok is a $100K bet on a ~$10M return.
- **Scale premium**: unlike buying in bulk, in businesses you get paid more per dollar of profit as you grow, and growing $100M → $150M can be as easy as $0 → $1M.
- **Missionary vs mercenary**: a 10–15-year grind needs a missionary heart. A mercenary chasing market trends won't last.
- **"When money comes easy I go hard; when money comes hard I go easy."** Follow the "nose for the money": Gym Launch began by accident from licensing, and an agency owner switched to business financing when it out-earned his services.
- **Reframe "it isn't scalable" as "I don't know how to scale it."** That turns a circumstance into a problem you can solve. Break it down to physics: why is it physically impossible to scale?

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Before entering a new market, ask people in it what the hardest part of the business is.
- CPG: the #1 metric is the percentage of ideal customers who repurchase after the first try. With no repeat purchases there's no LTV, and marketing only tells more people about a mediocre product.
- Pivot when a foundational assumption is proven untrue with data. Otherwise push and iterate.
- Pivot test for an underfunded founder: if there's no money for the big-brand play and no missionary heart for a 10–15-year grassroots grind, give one zero-fail "Hail Mary." If it fails, shut down and move on rather than waste 3 more years.
- A narrow niche means out-competing only 2–3 players.
- Almost any business can reach $1B in value with a long enough horizon (roofing, restaurants, dry cleaning, e.g. Zips).
- Professional services at scale need ownership paths (partnership/LLP) so that staying beats leaving on a risk-adjusted basis.

## Playbook steps
1. Name the business you think you're in, then find the constraint that actually drives the economics.
2. If it's talent, build a recruiting and training funnel that mirrors your customer funnel.
3. Price the payoff behind the wall (enterprise value) and decide whether it's worth the swings.
4. Write down the first-principles assumptions before investing. Iterate while they hold; pivot when data breaks one.
5. Count losses on new channels as investment in diversification and enterprise value.
6. Watch for where money comes easily and lean into it.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Side-stepping the hard problem for an opportunity that only looks better.
- Restarting businesses and hitting the same revenue ceiling.
- Concluding "TikTok/paid ads/marketing doesn't work for us" after a failed first try.
- Outsourcing your core product to a dev shop with no technical person inside to check them.
- Gurus hiring "minions" far weaker than themselves, which dilutes service quality.
- Doing deals that waste time and money instead of growing your biggest winner.
- Telling limiting stories ("that business isn't scalable," "no one sells like me").

## Examples / case details
- Gym Launch client: made money in gyms → bought Airbnbs → in-house cleaning → a cleaning business. After reframing it as recruiting and training, he went from ~$30–40K/month to >$150K/month within 12 months.
- Allan: $1.7M/month run rate within 6 months, but the product couldn't deliver.
- Portfolio CEO: a new product line that could add ~$200M of enterprise value made the hard work feel worth it.
- Canned-cocktail founder (UK): ~$1.5M/year, ~1,000 stores with low sell-through, 5% ABV premium product with no premium brand, zero profit, staff let go, no repeat-rate metric. Alex's paths:
  - Big brand: needs capital and a celebrity sponsorship.
  - Regional on foot: sample in stores, pay staff commission; a 10–15-year grind.
  - Hail Mary: small cans suit airline, train and cruise carts, so land one transport contract and use it as leverage with others. If that fails, pivot.
  - She earns her real money easily from penthouse property development, which she called "not scalable." Alex says it's one of the most scalable businesses; her real problem was not knowing how to hire and train people.
- Jesse Itzler's "Sheets" strips (with LeBron): sales rose for 4 weeks, then fell, because the product wasn't good enough.
