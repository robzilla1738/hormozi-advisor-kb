## Core thesis
Sell very expensive to a select few or very cheap to everyone ("the middle is where people die"). When starting, the easiest expensive offer is your own capped 1-on-1 time: 100% margin, fast learning, brand lift, and cash flow that funds growth. Every business should list a 10× (or 100×) tier. Even a small uptake can double revenue and supply most of the profit.

## Frameworks & models
- **Business = arbitrage** between cost to get customers and what you make from them.
- **Start high, work down** (Tesla: $250K Roadster → Model S → Model 3).
- **Why 1-on-1 when starting**: learn more from fewer high-value clients; shifts your money beliefs; everyone earns per hour (last year's income ÷ 2,000); flexible delivery/quick iterations (beta-testing); time can be capped; "when you have demand, cut supply"; ~100% margin; brand lift/anchor for the scalable product; marketing ("one of my private clients") and best case studies; better people around you.
- **Acquisition.com logo**: fulcrum (leverage) + supply/demand curve, which he calls the two most powerful concepts in business.
- **Three frames to design the premium tier**: (1) if they paid 10×/100×, what would you include? Cross out hard-cost items and see what's left. (2) If growth came only from word of mouth from this one customer, what would their experience be? (3) Remove everything unscalable but make it worth 10× anyway.
- **Value-equation tactics**: pick the right avatar (the $1K buyer is usually a different person than the $100 buyer, not an upgraded one); describe their pain better than they can, e.g. by mining reviews of niche books; 1-on-1 raises perceived likelihood of achievement, and your reputation acts as an implied guarantee; **speed** ("latency beats magnitude"): halve or cut delivery time by 2/3, give priority access and first response; **ease**: log every action the customer takes and delete friction one point at a time.
- **Priority ring with vendors**: at 10× price you can pay vendors and partners more to prioritize your customers.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Have a $1,000/month tier and a $10,000 one-time option even if the core is $100/month.
- Allocate about 5–10% of your time to 1-on-1 at 10× price; cap it and use the income to "go on the offense" with business money.
- Price so it's worth your time, rather than asking whether it's worth your time.
- Anchor rule: list the price wherever you sell, or say it out loud. Confront it ("It's 10 grand an hour"), then shut up. If they balk, offer the scalable version with "90% of the elements."
- Sell wealthy buyers on time saved more than money saved.

## Playbook steps
1. Pick a clear extreme (premium few or cheap mass). 2. Create a capped 1-on-1 or super-premium version (even lawn care can offer a personal cell or owner-designed garden). 3. Design it with the three frames. 4. Target the premium avatar's specific pain. 5. Add speed and priority, and remove friction. 6. Present the high price first, pause, and downsell to the scalable offer. 7. Use private-client results as case studies and marketing.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Sitting in the middle of the price spectrum.
- Refusing 1-on-1 because "it's not scalable" or "selling time is what poor people do."
- Mumbling the high price and moving on (the anchor fails).
- Designing the premium tier around the current $100 buyer.
- Vague pain: "pain and persuasion only exist in the specific, never the vague."

- Huntington Beach group gym: a referred client trained with him 1-on-1 for 90 minutes, 5 days a week, at about $125/hr, paying cash. Alex says this funded reinvestment. He states ~$180/day and "something like $45,000 a month," which don't reconcile ($125 × 1.5 h × ~22 days ≈ $4K/month); likely a misstatement or caption error [inference].
