# This is why I was Poor

## Core thesis
Recap of a coaching call with one of his salespeople, whose family said sales was "too risky." The lessons: volatility isn't risk. Wealth is an income-to-expense ratio, not a number. Live on your base salary and invest the rest. Pay off high-interest debt first. Dollar-cost average into the S&P 500. Track net worth obsessively. To grow income, tie yourself to an acquisition channel. A $200K salesperson effectively "owns a $1M business" with far less risk. This is an older video (Gym Launch/Prestige Labs/ALAN era, "$120M+" portfolio).

## Frameworks & models
- **Ask "what problem are we solving?"** before advising.
- **Don't take money advice from people poorer than you.** Listen to people who make far more (via Grant Cardone: "listen to billionaires").
- **Volatility ≠ risk**: an insurer that loses big one year in nine but profits the other eight isn't risky, just volatile. Salespeople generate revenue, so they're the lowest-risk people in a company despite volatile paychecks.
- **Live on base, bank the variable**: plan as if salary is all you'll earn; commissions are "gravy."
- **Wealth = ratio of inflow to outflow.** Example: earning ~$140K and living on $25K (~5.6×) saves ~$115K/yr, about 4.5 years of expenses per year. He says ~2 years gets ~$230K, which he frames as a "technically retire" level at 10% returns covering $25K. [inference: a 10% withdrawal rate is aggressive; he concedes he wouldn't retire on $230K, but a decade gets to "2-point-something" million.]
- **Debt order**: kill high-interest credit card debt first (a 16% guaranteed "return"), then optionally low-interest student loans (an emotional choice, not a mathematical one), then invest.
- **DCA into the S&P 500 monthly** (Buffett's instruction for his estate). Don't time the market. (Includes a "not investment advice" disclaimer.)
- **Tie yourself to an acquisition channel**: pick a channel the company doesn't use, crack it on your own time (self-sourced sales pay more), then pitch building a team of ~5 and taking an override. Rainmakers are always paid the most.
- **$200K salesperson ≈ owning a $1M business**: at 20% net free cash flow, without the HR, legal, fulfillment and lead-gen burdens. Employees wrongly assume revenue = owner's money.
- **"Track, don't slack"**: track net worth (with debts included) daily or weekly. He used a spreadsheet updated every morning and now gets a weekly email with one number. It builds a pulse on cash flow and catches leaks like forgotten subscriptions.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- He cites the average small business owner making ~$78K/yr, while his salespeople make $150–200K.
- If you're miserable or underpaid, understand the entrepreneurial risk before leaving. He says he's risk-averse and might not have started a business had he understood the risk.
- Lifestyle upgrades (jets, exotic cars, fancy restaurants) didn't raise his happiness. He was happy in a $1,200/mo Albuquerque apartment.

## Playbook steps
1. Track net worth daily or weekly.
2. Pay off high-interest debt.
3. Cut expenses; live on base salary.
4. DCA the surplus into the S&P monthly.
5. Raise income by opening a new acquisition channel, then lead a team on it.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Confusing volatility with risk ("a classic poor person thing").
- Thinking of wealth as a target number instead of a ratio.
- Assuming company revenue goes to the owner.
- Trying to time the market.

## Examples / case details
- 20+ person sales team. The salesperson had ~$20K of combined student loan and credit card debt.
- He claims a $40K earner living on $25K and investing the difference would be top 1% within 20 years. [inference: $15K/yr at ~10% for 20 years ≈ $0.86M, which is likely well short of top-1% net worth, so treat this as hyperbole.]
- The closing line ("work your ass off two months in a business… get richer") appears to swap employee and business owner. The intended point: an owner can work hard and still get poorer, while a salesperson who works hard gets paid.
