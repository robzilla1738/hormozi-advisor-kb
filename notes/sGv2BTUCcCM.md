## Core thesis
Start every business diagnosis with one triage question: is it **supply-constrained** (customers but not enough capacity) or **demand-constrained** (capacity but not enough customers)? It is never both at once. Most stuck businesses are working hard on the wrong one: either they never diagnosed it, or the constraint moved and they kept working on the old one. The constraint swings back and forth like an accordion. Fixing the problem you don't have wastes money and often makes things worse. The captions cut off just as he introduces the Ashley and Raymond case studies.

## Frameworks & models
- **Double test**: if you doubled ad spend, outreach or content tomorrow, would sales double (demand problem) or would you create a mess (supply problem)?
- **Supply checklist**: Could you serve more customers tomorrow at your standard? Are you turning people away or keeping a waitlist? Are your most profitable slots or products always sold out? Are you running out of inventory or shipping late? Any "yes" means you likely have a supply problem.
- **Supply fixes, in order**: (1) raise prices, the fastest fix; (2) add capacity; (3) clean up ops / leverage. For services, the levers besides price are **technology** (one person does 5× the work), **training** (B-level talent becomes A-level), and **headcount** (twice as many B players). For a consultant specifically: raise price, change the delivery ratio (1:1 → small group or one-to-many), or hire and train more consultants.
- **Demand side**: an acquisition channel (outreach, affiliates, ads, content) and then conversion. The leaks are lead capture (landing page, headline, images, copy, lead magnet), **people** (not following the process), **process** (selling to the wrong person, the wrong thing, at the wrong time), **offer** (the right people at the right time, but the offer isn't compelling), and **price**, which counts on both sides: undercharging means you can't afford to acquire customers.
- **The stalemate**: you can't afford more staff without more revenue, and you can't serve more customers without more staff. He calls this a strategic problem, usually about price or the product mix and upsells.
- **Potential vs throughput** (a highway that goes from four lanes to one and back to four): widening the part that isn't the bottleneck adds potential but not throughput. His example is redesigning a website that already converts fine.
- **Principal–agent problem**: the person who signs the check isn't the person who has to do the work or makes the call. Ask "what customer do you really serve?"
- **Network surface area**: product quality alone drives growth only when users have a lot of exposure to other likely users (Facebook). For niche, high-end products (IT consulting for the Fortune 100), a great product keeps customers but won't grow the business, so you have to go out and get customers.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- When supply-constrained, raise prices "by a lot": 50–100%. If you double the price and 30% of clients leave, you make more money serving fewer people and free up time to build capacity. (Someone was nervous about adding 25¢ to shipping; he said "add $3 and see what happens.")
- Raising price can also fix a demand constraint when you can't afford to acquire customers.
- He'd rather be supply-constrained than demand-constrained, but says both suck.
- If retention is great and growth is flat, leave the product alone and move people and cash into acquisition. Products often get worse through addition, and constant change erodes product and service quality more than the incremental improvements add.
- When supply-constrained, sometimes accept mediocre marketing and unworked leads; growing more would hurt your reputation.
- Before doubling the sales team or ad spend, check every stage of the funnel for leaks.

## Playbook steps
1. Run the double test and the supply checklist. 2. If supply: raise prices → leverage through tech, training, or more headcount. 3. If demand: pick an acquisition channel → fix lead capture → find the conversion leak (people, process, offer, price). 4. If you're in a stalemate: fix price or upsells first to free up cash. 5. Once it's fixed, expect the constraint to flip and move your attention with it.

## Mistakes he warns against
- A fully booked consultant making more content and running more ads.
- A SaaS spending 5% on marketing while competitors spend 20–30%, and pouring money into features.
- Marketing a restaurant harder when the weekends are already full.
- Running ads while sold out (shipping went from 2 to 8 weeks, plus angry emails).
- "If you build it, they will come" (an app that blames its features when the real problem is awareness).
- Sending paid traffic to a pretty homepage instead of a page built to convert.
- Spending your limited time on work you love but the business doesn't need.

## Examples / case details
- **Restaurant (steakhouse)**: 90+ minute waits on Friday and Saturday, empty on Tuesday; really a pricing and allocation problem. Options: a weekend menu with higher prices; frame it as "weekdays 20% less" rather than "weekends 20% more"; paid line-skipping; stay open later. All three together could raise profit 100%+. Margin example: at a 10% margin, a 20% price increase triples profit on those days. With weekends at half of revenue, he says that makes the business 1.5× as profitable. [inference: by his numbers, total profit would go from 10 to 20, which is double; the 1.5× is probably loose wording or a caption slip.]
- **Fitness equipment during COVID**: demand went up 4× and they sold out. Fixes: sell something with no supply limit (warranty/insurance, white-labeled or affiliate apps, add-on services like flooring or gym design), then use the cash to pay vendors about 20% more to get to the front of the line. Physical-product businesses need redundant suppliers. A friend's core edge is buying raw materials better than anyone.
- **Plumber**: fully booked and overtime is squeezing margins. The real constraint is sales skill (no upsells, memberships, repair schedules, or checking other units in the house). Fix: a 2–3 line script, daily role-play, and tracking the upsell rate; a 50% upsell rate means about 50% more revenue, and most of it drops to profit. He classes this one as technically demand-constrained.
- **Enterprise software for commercial buildings**: stuck for 8 years, about 10 demos a week (~500 a year) closing 2 a year, with 98% revenue retention. Leadership's plan was to double the sales team. The real issue was presenting to the owners while the building managers or foremen decided. The fix was a new script that raised the manager's status and made it about them. He calls it a "$10M+ question." Getting to 100 deals out of 500 demos would be 50×.
- **Course creator breaking even**: raise the price from $1K to $3K with a 15–20% drop in sales (he says revenue "nearly triples"; [inference] 3× the price on 80–85% of the units is about 2.4–2.55×); add a $10K tier bought by ~10%, which is about +$1K per sale, for roughly $4K total. If he can't raise price: run his best organic content as ads with a 5-second CTA.
- **Mobile app**: optimize the app store listing (higher conversion leads to better ranking and more traffic from Apple), paid user acquisition, banner ads in adjacent apps, a referral program; he says the user base could grow 20–100× in a year.
- **His own pattern**: "I've almost always been able to out-advertise any business I've ever had," so he's usually supply-constrained. He keeps acquisition.com's "hideous" homepage because it converts fine and isn't the constraint.
- He says roughly two-thirds of the owners he asks have been at the same revenue for two years.
