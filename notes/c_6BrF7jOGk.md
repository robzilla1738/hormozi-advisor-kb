## Core thesis
This is a case study of one of his 13 portfolio companies: a mid-priced consumer service sold on a two-call close, run by three young founders. They instrumented the sales funnel (show rate → offer rate → close rate → % cash collected up front → units). They compared each stage to a benchmark, then fixed the biggest gaps in the right order, **who before what**. In 60 days, units went from 56 to 93/month and cash collected up front went from 47% to 82%. Part 2 (product/customer success) is in a separate video.

## Frameworks & models
- **Sales funnel metrics**:
  - Show rate: shows ÷ appointments.
  - Offer rate: offers ÷ shows. This is a lead-quality signal; unqualified people get no offer.
  - Close rate: track it on both shows and offers.
  - % cash collected up front.
  - Units sold: the product of the four rates above.
- **Cross-check stats at team and individual level**: if the team offers at 70% with 30% close, and one rep offers at 30% with 100% close, he is cherry-picking.
- **Close rate × cash collected tells the story**: a high close rate with low cash means reps "getting anyone to say yes and taking $10 down."
- **Benchmarks** he uses:
  - Show rate: 70% for any appointment business.
  - Close rate: depends heavily on context. 80%+ for low-ticket in person, about 5% for investments by phone. He set 40% for this two-call, mid-priced consumer service.
  - Setters: at least 3 sets/day. He later says expectations went from 2 to 4.
- **Constraint focus**: put effort at the constraint. A 40% stage lift is "4 years of S&P" growth.
- **Show-rate drivers**: #1 is the total number of available time slots ("take that to the bank"). Others are targeting and the offer itself.
- **Surface vs iceberg discovery**: ask about intention (why, what changes, who else it affects), not just "how much do you want to make?"
- **Obstacles vs objections**: obstacles come before price is mentioned ("just want info"). Objections come after it ("too much," "think about it," "spouse," "send a brochure"). Smoke screens are fake reasons.
- **Killing zombies**: it's easier to kill a zombie far away. Bring likely objections forward and defuse them before the close.
- **Looping**: handle the concern → ask again → repeat. "The number of sales you make is directly proportional to how many times you ask."
- **Tone and emphasis**: "I didn't say he hit his wife," stressing different words. Childlike curiosity: tilt your head and say "huh, what are the main things?"
- **Educate on the prospect over the product**: "sell me this pen" → pocket the pen and ask "what brought you in today?"

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If the same problem recurs for quarters in the same department under the same person, it's a **who** issue, not a what issue.
- The best closer is often a bad sales manager. They are different skills.
- Optimize ads on **cost per sale**, not cost per lead or cost per appointment.
- Hiring a sales director:
  - Founders judge culture fit.
  - Subject-matter experts run tactical interviews.
  - Judge candidates by the quality and quantity of data they plan to collect.
  - A good director marries building people up with tracking specific metrics.
- Sales team utilization: if reps can take 10 calls/day but take 4, you have too many reps. Cut the lowest closers. Reps with full calendars "sell from the back of their heel"; reps starved of calls get desperate and hard-close.
- "Nothing demotivates a high performer like a low performer who's still on the team."
- Low standards make people shrink to the bar.
- Pre-appointment: "Is anyone else required to make this decision?" If yes, reschedule so every decision-maker attends.

## Playbook steps
1. Get baseline data on the 5 metrics.
2. Fix the **who** first: hire an experienced sales director (a former sales trainer for a similar consumer sale) because the CEO-as-sales-manager was micromanaging.
3. Fix ad targeting: fire the negligent media buyer, re-target 25–35-year-olds who are employed and love their jobs (it had drifted to 18–24), and optimize on cost per sale.
4. Downsize the sales and setter teams and raise the setter standard (2 → 4 sets/day).
5. Split multitasking: promote one setter to lead-nurture specialist with a checklist.
   - Make a 3-way intro (setter + closer + lead), by iPhone if possible.
   - Closer sends a personalized voice memo or video text the morning of the call.
   - Double-dial, contact leads fast, book same day or next day, and send same-day reminders.
6. Re-script discovery for deeper questions, bring objections forward, and drill looping.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Setters doing both outbound calls and nurture.
- No double-dial, 30-minute to 3-hour speed to contact, and no morning-of reminders.
- Optimizing for the cheapest leads. They had to cancel 75% of appointments before even reaching a 49% show rate: "about the closest thing to literally burning money."
- Shallow discovery, which leads to objections blowing up at the close, argumentation and hard closing.
- Salespeople talking more than prospects.
- Offering to unqualified people because reps need commissions.
- Focusing on the how and not the who.

## Examples / case details
- Results after 60 days:

  | Offer rate | 83% | ~80% | about flat |
  | Close rate | 27% | 41% | about +52% |
  | Cash up front | 47% | 82% | +74%; he says "almost doubled" |

- He says up-front cash flow went "four x." [inference: by the stated numbers, 1.66 × 1.74 ≈ 2.9× cash collected up front. "4x" and "doubled" appear to be rounded or rhetorical, or the title may count part 2 changes. Treat his multipliers loosely.]
- Allan (his earlier company) ran about 4,000 appointments/day with an ML team studying show-rate drivers: number of touches, delay between replies, spacing.
- Looping example: "Seems complicated" → "Which part?" → "Tech" → "Our vendor handles that for a few bucks" → ask. Then "Feels fast" → "How long have you wanted to solve this?" → "Then you decided long ago."
