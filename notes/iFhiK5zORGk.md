## Core thesis
When demand rises, cut supply — scarcity lets you move up the demand curve. Marketing shifts the demand curve itself, so scarcity + stimulated demand lets you charge more AND sometimes sell more units. Most owners cut supply but leave price low, leaving "juice in the squeeze."

## Frameworks & models
- **"When demand increases, cut supply"** (from an ultra-high-end jeweler at the event).
- **Marketing shifts the demand curve:** textbook curves assume static conditions/perfect information; marketing = artificially stimulating demand, moving the curve outward, enabling higher prices.
- **Pressure-cooker capacity:** "full capacity" is whatever you decide; cap supply (even to zero), build a waiting list, and periodically "let off steam" — that pressure is pricing power. Don't release constantly or pressure never builds.
- **Seductive delay:** tease, hint, waitlist; delay satisfaction — the point where people feel rejected is much further away than most think.
- Waiting list creates allure ("must be good, they're at capacity").

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you cut supply, raise price to match the new point on the curve, not the old price.
- Fewer, higher-paying clients = more revenue, less operational drag, more profit, ability to over-deliver.

## Playbook steps
1. Create an ultra-scarce tier/product/service level with a hard cap.
2. Stimulate demand (marketing, hints, teasers).
3. Run a waitlist; release slots periodically.
4. Price at the top of the shifted curve.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Spending too much time on supply and not enough generating demand.
- Prices so low a buyer is relieved ("thank god, I'd have paid more") — missed opportunity.

## Examples / case details
- After-School All-Stars charity at Arnold Schwarzenegger's house: poker ticket went $10k → $25k and sold more; Alex donated $1M that night.
- Chanel stores get 1–2 bags, unknown mix, no online sales — engineered scarcity.
- A day of his time: 5 people x $20k = $100k vs. 3 people x $100k = $300k, easier to fulfill. Top 1–2 of the 5 might have paid $200k.
- Limited-edition t-shirt drops sell more units because scarcity shifts demand.
