# I SPENT $100,000 on courses, so you don't have to...

## Core thesis
A course usually "didn't work" because the skill it taught wasn't your weak link, not because the course was bad. Business is a pipe (or bridge) from eyeballs to money, and throughput is capped by the narrowest section. Find the missing brick and attack it. Disappointment with courses comes from expectations, so treat learning as a two-way responsibility. Also: don't judge people you don't know firsthand.

## Frameworks & models
- **Subset/superset**: math and copywriting are needed for business but don't make you good at it alone.
- **Bridge of bricks / pipe**: eyeballs → headlines → ads → copy → clicks → conversions → sales → upsells → money. The narrowest tube limits the whole flow.
- **The "last course" illusion**: the course that finally "worked" was often just the last brick in a bridge others helped you build.
- **Expectations explain the anger**: nobody is furious about four years of Spanish they can't speak. Students sit on a bell curve, so about half are below average. A good teacher shifts the curve but doesn't remove the tails.
- **Two-sided responsibility**: as a seller, own 100% of outcomes and never blame customers. As a buyer, also own your side. Winners take responsibility for both.
- **If-then decision algorithms** (with Leila) so the same decision isn't remade: "No diligence, no deal"; if they don't both agree, they don't proceed; "If I don't know someone firsthand, I don't make a judgment."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Study what you lack, not what you enjoy, especially as a solo founder or with a team of 5–10. Every function must exist, and you need to know enough to judge a hire.
- Don't write off a whole person. Ask "what can I learn from them?" instead of "good or bad?"

## Playbook steps
1. Map your eyeballs-to-money pipe. 2. Find the narrowest section or missing brick. 3. Buy or learn specifically for that gap. 4. Set realistic expectations and do the work as a student. 5. Pull lessons even from bad courses.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Taking six courses on a skill that isn't the broken link.
- Playing the victim about past teachers.
- Blaming customers as a business owner. He calls it "possibly the stupidest thing you could ever do."
- Judging people secondhand. He says it cost him years with people he now does deals with.

## Examples / case details
- Graduated Vanderbilt magna cum laude in 3 years. He says nothing he learned there beat any e-learning course he bought (YouTube ads, copy, sales, mentorship, seminars).
- He compares $100K on a degree vs $100K on vetted courses plus 4 years, and says it's "not even close" in earning power.
- Over half the portfolio is education/training (e.g. how to run a gym, auto shop, plumbing business).
- Paid Grant Cardone $120K for 4 hours (the first hour was $30K).
- Dairy Queen analogy: a new employee drops your shake; you don't blame Warren Buffett.
- Portfolio at about $85M/yr at the time.
