## Core thesis
The most important concept is a ratio, not a tactic: lifetime gross profit (LTGP/LTV) vs. cost to acquire a customer (CAC), plus how fast you're paid back. "Methods expire; models endure." Rule #1 of business is don't go out of business, and cash flow prevents that. The business that makes more per customer can outspend everyone in the attention auction. "The model is the competitive moat."

## Frameworks & models
- **Model vs method**: methods (DM hacks, hashtags) are one-trick ponies; the model (unit economics) is the engine.
- **Tale of two gyms**: industry "LBO" (low-barrier offer) $21/21 days → 1/3 convert to $99/mo staying ~6 months. ~$100 CAC, so effectively ~$300 per member, and the marketing budget has to be capped. His model: 6-week challenge $600 upfront + ~$200 supplements within 48 hrs + annual prepay (~$2,000) pitched at week 3. That's ~$1,000 blended in the first 30 days. When $100 makes $1,000, there's no budget: "spend as many dollars as you possibly can."
- **Self-funding launch "black box"**: $5K in the bank + lease → $100/day ads → 10 leads → 2 closes × $600 = $1,200/day back. Scaled to $500/day → ~$100K pre-sales in month one, which paid for a <$100K gym fully outfitted on day one. Roll into recurring 6 weeks later, cash-flow positive. Six locations in his early 20s.
- **30-day customer value**: what a customer is worth in gross profit in 30 days. That's how long most small businesses can float cash, and it's the interest-free credit card window.
- **Back-of-napkin math (annual)**: CAC = total acquisition spend (ads, marketing labor, commissions, content) ÷ new customers. Lifetime revenue = revenue ÷ total customers. Cost per customer = annual COGS/delivery ÷ customers. LTGP = lifetime revenue − cost per customer (or × GP%). He finds yearly napkin math most accurate because it smooths good and bad months.
- **Required LTGP:CAC by automation** of the 3 components (lead gen, conversion, fulfillment): all 3 automated/one-to-many → 3:1; 2 of 3 → ~6:1; 1 of 3 → ~9:1; none → 12:1+ (minimums). Leverage = content, ads, virality/word of mouth. Manual outreach or people at a step counts as "not automated."
- **Why the ratio must be padded**: CAC always rises (CPMs up, competitors, colder audiences up the interest curve); management layers add cost; later customers are worth less; new hires (6th salesperson, new marketer, new rep) cost from day one but take months to perform, so metrics dip.
- **Raise LTV levers**: raise price, decrease costs, upsells, downsells (careful about cannibalizing buyers of the expensive option), financing, change collection terms (e.g. work starts when paid in full), cross-sells (fries, not a bigger burger), warranties. Drills: a book, a table.
- **Lower CAC levers**: better offer, more and better ad creatives (volume + hooks, remake winners), CRO (pages, follow-up, phone scripts, sales training), cheaper CPM placements. CAC ≠ cost per lead.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Recover acquisition cost within 30 days (rule of thumb).
- Optimize the ratio, not the cheapest customer: LTV $5,000 / CAC $500 (10:1) beats LTV $1,000 / CAC $200 (5:1).
- Today's CAC is likely the best you'll ever get.
- If you can't run the LTV-lever drill on your own business, "you don't understand your business well enough." It's harder with physical products than services.
- Would you spend $1M to get a customer who pays $20M? Yes: "pay to play."

## Playbook steps
1. Compute last year's CAC (all acquisition costs ÷ new customers). 2. Compute LTGP (revenue per customer − delivery cost per customer). 3. Score your automation (lead gen / conversion / fulfillment) to set the target ratio (3/6/9/12:1). 4. Restructure the offer to front-load 30-day cash (upfront price, quick upsells, prepay, financing, terms). 5. Pull LTV and CAC levers. 6. Once the 30-day payback is positive, scale spend without a fixed budget.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Blaming the method ("Facebook/outbound/content doesn't work for me") when it's a model problem.
- Chasing cheaper leads/broader targeting, which brings more volume and more "trash" customers.
- Running at 3:1 with manual processes, so hiring for growth tips you into losses.
- Most owners want more leads; "it's usually the last thing they really need."
- Being intimidated by "third grade math."
