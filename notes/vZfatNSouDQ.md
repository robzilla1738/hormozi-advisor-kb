# Hardcore Business Lessons I Learned From A Dealer

## Core thesis
Lessons from his 18-year-old summer job at a fifth-generation fur coat dealer: validate angry customers, turn the off-season into a low-cost "insurance" continuity product, use no-based selling, give byproduct ("sawdust") bonuses as bait for upsells, manufacture scarcity, and do the boring basics forever. Being the dealer for life beats the one-time sale.

## Frameworks & models
- **"Only one person in the angry boat"**: when you join the customer's anger and take it seriously, they climb out. Downplaying it makes them escalate.
- **Insurance product (doesn't have to be real insurance)**: charge for keeping a purchase valuable longer. Summer storage and conditioning of coats makes them last 2–5× longer. Analogy: a $50K car has $100/mo insurance. Very low cost to deliver (one 18-year-old, AC, storage).
- **Core + upsell**: storage (core) plus brushing and repair (upsell).
- **No-based selling**: "We're going to store and condition the coat; you don't want anything else, do you?" Their reflexive "no" is a yes to both.
- **Sawdust**: excess you normally throw away, made into value (IKEA particleboard, whey protein). Fur scraps became earmuffs and little purses, given free as a bonus for turning in a coat; then "you want to store that too, right?" is charged.
- **Perceived demand/scarcity**: "no dealers" sign, "limit two coats per customer," multiple price strike-throughs, "final price, all sales final," single-file entry with capped occupancy. He says this doubled coats bought.
- **Dealer for life**: continuity between sales means customers come to you first instead of price-shopping.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Even if a complaint is ridiculous, it's your responsibility. Saying "not a big deal" means you're not taking ownership.
- For big one-time purchases, add small between-sale services even at no profit. If customers then buy about 2 more products on average, it more than pays.
- Make an analogy that compares an unfamiliar product to a familiar one.

## Playbook steps
1. Find your dead season or idle capacity. 2. Build a cheap "protect your purchase" continuity offer. 3. Sell it no-based, with the upsell implied. 4. Use byproduct bonuses to bring people in, then charge to service them. 5. Add scarcity signals. 6. Keep doing the basics consistently for years.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Downplaying an upset customer.
- Treating selling as evil: "capitalism is the only system where both people say thank you after an exchange."
- Skipping known basics (blog, consistent email follow-up, content, a sales team) because they take time.

## Examples / case details
- Owner John rolls his eyes, then goes "into character": "Who sold you that coat? We'll get rid of them." The customer backpedals, the coat is fixed in 5 minutes, and she apologizes.
- Coats about $25K. The business survived two world wars, recessions and PETA pressure by never changing the fundamentals.
- Acquisition.com portfolio is about $200M/yr. Big owners "do the same thing for a very long period of time."
