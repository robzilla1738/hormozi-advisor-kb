# Top Signs You Have The Wrong Business Partner

## Core thesis
From a self-described "partnership killer" with nine failed partnerships, who says he made all his money after going partner-free for four years. Starting out, it's usually better to have no partner. The only reasons to take one are money or skills you lack. Two promoters who like the same things means one is unnecessary. Every business needs three legs (acquisition, product, operations). Put a written agreement in place first, and give each person clear, non-overlapping ownership. Often a strong operational second is a hire, not a partner, so you don't give away half your equity.

## Frameworks & models
- **Only two reasons for a partner**: money or skills you don't have. Buddy partnerships satisfy an emotional need, not a business one.
- **Three-legged stool**: Acquisition (rainmaker: marketing and sales), Product (fulfillment/delivery), Operations (payroll, taxes, legal, IT, finance, HR). The legs aren't equally valued: rainmaking and product are scarce, while good operators are more plentiful and often obtainable without equity. Skill stacking lets one person cover 2–3 legs.
- **Agreement as stress test**: go through lawyers first. It shows how the person acts under stress and simulates scenarios (one stops working, leaves, wants out). Most partnerships end over unmet, unspoken expectations.
- **Draw the line on the board**: list every activity (do a 15-minute time study for a week if needed) and assign each to exactly one owner. "If everyone's responsible for everything, no one's responsible for anything." Specialization is the start of an organization.
- **Equity is the most valuable thing**: giving away half on day one is the biggest dilution of your future earnings.
- **Married couples** often work, because they naturally split hunter/front-end and organized/back-end roles and have an emotional sounding board.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you both have the same skills, either specialize in different areas or split up. "Better to recognize an error early than late."
- If you can't afford two lawyers, use a free partnership template plus one paid lawyer hour for guidance.
- Also agree on work schedule, vacation, weekends, hours and responsiveness expectations.
- Most of his audience are promoters, so they need a strong operator.
- If starting solo, acquire the skills yourself and make your first hire a strong operational second.

## Playbook steps
1. Ask what money or skill the partner brings that you lack. 2. Map the three legs and find the gap. 3. Draft the agreement with scenarios before starting. 4. Time-study and list all activities. 5. Assign single owners. 6. Fill the missing leg by hiring where possible rather than partnering.

## Mistakes he warns against
- "Jump into bed together" partnerships with no contract, avoided because it might mess up the vibe.
- Splitting all the work in half.
- Promoters with no fulfillment or ops (customer emails go unanswered).
- Thinking small and acting small, which keeps businesses small.
