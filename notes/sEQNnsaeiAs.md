## Core thesis
Most businesses never sell. Alex says 99% eventually close and about 1% sell. That isn't a marketing problem: most businesses are worthless because they're jobs. To be sellable, a business has to run without you. That means a productized offer, a predictable acquisition channel, and every hat replaced (sales, marketing, product, decision-making, spokesperson). The process takes years and costs margin, but it turns you into a shareholder and creates leverage in negotiation.

## Frameworks & models
- **Kiyosaki-style progression**: employee → self-employed → owner → investor (as Alex draws it). Going from employee to self-employed usually cuts your pay at first because you have to be every part of the machine. An LLC with employees is still self-employed.
- **Sellability ladder**: (1) stop doing fulfillment; (2) productize, because custom work gets valued as a service business and comes with a big earn-out; (3) replace acquisition with one reliable channel: referrals, affiliates, manual outbound, owned media, earned media (content), or paid; X effort/dollars in → Y out; (4) remove all the hats, including VP Sales, marketing director, chief product, decision-maker and face/spokesperson; (5) become "chief shareholder / director of the board."
- **Earn-out trap**: you work 3–5 more years for the same pay, the buyer gets the upside, and if you miss milestones they keep the business.
- **SDE illusion**: owner profit includes unpaid salaries for the roles you fill. Replacing yourself lowers margin, which is fine.
- **Hire ladder**: early hires are worse than you (train fully), then hires who've done the role (train on your way), then hires who are better than you and come with solutions pre-loaded, which happens once you cross multiple eight figures (~$10–30M+). At that point your job is recruiting and casting vision.
- **Negotiation**: the most leverage goes to whoever needs the deal least. Build to the "position of FU," where you'd happily keep a business that pays you without you doing anything.
- **"Sell the business, not the services"** (an MIT speaker quoted in John Warrillow's book; caption reads "john warlow"): the CEO's product is the business.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Litmus test: step away and don't answer calls for two months. Does it still run?
- You may need to hire six people to replace the six things you do.
- Scalability needs enough gross margin and volume to pay for high-quality talent.
- Turn "I wish I had X person" into a solvable problem: "I don't know how to find, hire, direct a marketing director," then solve it.
- Acquisition.com portfolio investing applies above ~$5M/yr.

## Playbook steps
1. Self-inventory every hat, including fractional ones. 2. Productize the offer. 3. Install one predictable acquisition channel. 4. Write processes for each hat, hand them off, and keep improving. 5. Hire VP Sales, marketing and product leaders, expecting several misses per role. 6. Remove yourself as the face of the business. 7. Hold a clear, distilled vision so you attract strong people. 8. Only consider selling when you don't need to.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Thinking you're a "business owner" because you stopped fulfilling while you still do sales, marketing and decisions.
- Assuming the buyer will figure out the replacement ("they're not going to").
- Starting another business instead of solving the people problem.
- Doing it only for money: you'll burn out once personal needs are met, and your vision gets diluted down to employees.
- Selling customized offers to everyone.
