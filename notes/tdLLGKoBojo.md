## Core thesis
Eleven costly mistakes, ordered roughly by size. They share a few themes. Money is lost the further he strays from his core competence (marketing, sales, acquisition, pricing, product). FOMO, "too good to be true" deals, badly structured or bad-faith partners, splitting focus, distance from the details, and reacting to competitors instead of customers drove most of the losses. "If you know how to create wealth, just create wealth; don't [mess] with investing" (a mentor's advice).

## Frameworks & models
- **Circle of competence** (Buffett/Munger): the further from your core, the more you lose; the closer, the more you make.
- **Partnership rules**: (1) a partner must bring something you don't have: time, money, or skills; otherwise one of you isn't needed. (2) You lose the most equity on day one; equity needn't be equal (95/5 is fine) and should match each person's contribution of time, money, and skill. Have the hard conversation up front. (3) Whoever works in the business gets a salary separate from ownership. (4) The rainmaker deserves a disproportionate share. (5) Needed functions (an accountant, say) can be hired or vendored, not given equity; equity goes to the core engines, getting customers and delivering. Good split: one person on product, one telling the world.
- **Bad partnership vs bad partner**: ethical people in a bad structure, versus people with nefarious intent. Both the person and the structure must be aligned.
- **ROIC**: every expense line must answer "how is this making me money?" Small businesses should get 3:1, 5:1, 10:1 on spend (e.g. $1 leads → $500 sales at 1-in-5).
- **"Don't be cute / backyard football"** (a PE CEO mentor who tripled companies): fundamentals executed obsessively beat fancy strategy (Amazon 4.7 vs 4.4 stars ≈ 10× sales).
- **Push vs pivot**: push = take a harder challenge, keep learning; pivot = fundamental assumptions were wrong, so change direction. The worst option is neither (coasting).
- **Know where the bodies are buried**: be close enough to each department to know its current problems and go one layer deeper.
- **"Just because you don't lose money doesn't mean you didn't lose money"**: count opportunity cost and the profit multiple at exit.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Bet only a small share of net worth or income on things outside your core; treat it as gambling.
- When everyone thinks something's a good idea, or you feel FOMO, pause.
- Dip a toe: buy one unit before buying 5–10.
- Only invest with someone who has done the deal for 2–3+ years, and co-invest alongside the operator ("buy a property that you buy"). If they refuse, no deal.
- "I'd rather have a very sure 20% than a really risky 40."
- If a seller offers outsized returns, ask why they don't keep the assets themselves.
- Believe red flags the first time; skip partners with fraud histories.
- Not taking profit "because I'm reinvesting" only counts if you can state the return on that capital.
- Software business: have a technical founder (with equity) holding outsourced devs accountable and requiring documentation. Never outsource the core value generation.
- Vendors: demand references "like you" at your scale and use case. Treat referrals as meaningless ("I'll act as though I've never met you"); walk away if they're weirded out. Bad news is always worse after the fact.
- Before entering a new industry, talk to people who make money in it.
- Focus on customers, not competitors (Paul Graham: talk to your customers).
- Any price change triggers complaints, even a price cut.

## Playbook steps
1. Stay within core competence; size bets small outside it. 2. Partnerships: define each person's time/money/skill, set unequal equity if warranted, pay salaries for jobs, give core-engine equity only. 3. Justify every expense by return. 4. Run fundamentals obsessively. 5. Stay close enough to know each department's problems. 6. Diligence vendors with like-for-like references. 7. Anticipate the market by talking to customers rather than copying competitors.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Crypto FOMO: put $1M in near the top and lost $500K in weeks; earlier, skipped ETH at ~$100 over a ~$7 dip.
- An out-of-state Ohio flip bought for $50K: the seller paid ~$19K and did no work; fake third bedroom, stolen electricity, ~$7K driveway, a family in a hotel ~30 days, sold for ~$20K. He estimates $30–50K lost plus time, while making ~$50K/day profit.
- Nine failed partnerships, including a 45/45/10 split between identical skill sets, and selling 2/3 of a profitable gym for its startup cost.
- Second gym: $250K fancy retail buildout vs the first at $37K; same revenue.
- A bad-faith gym partner (prior fraud indictment) had him personally guarantee the lease, front costs and do the launch (370 sales in ~6 weeks), then withdrew the whole account, sent it abroad and filed bankruptcy. He lost all the money from his gym exit.
- Coasting at a consulting job (neither push nor pivot): cost time and self-confidence.
- A food business: ~$600K first month, $10 meals at $9 cost, ~7% margin; 80% of support complaints vs supplements' 90% of profit. Split focus, estimated $10–25M cost via the lost exit multiple.
- An HR director's unilateral cost-of-living raise (blast email): ~$600K/yr profit. Lawyers reviewing every licensee asset and training: ~$120K/month, ~$1.2M total before the CFO caught it.
- Outsourced Allen software: ~$1M+ spent, a sunk-cost spiral, undocumented code, rising fees ate margins. (Product worked: 19% vs 10% of leads booked.)
- A vendor paid $270K to "insure" launch capacity lied; 75% of launch traffic was lost, about $15M.
- Biggest: in 2019, reacting to a competitor, he added complex 1-on-1/group accountability AND cut price $1,000/wk → $800/wk for everyone. Customers complained anyway. With ~40% margins, a 20% revenue cut (plus extra delivery cost) halves profit, roughly $5M/yr profit never recovered, multiplied at exit.

## Examples / case details
