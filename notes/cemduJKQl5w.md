## Core thesis
Really about the role of HR and compensation (title oversells "hiring tips"). A former HR director spent ~90 days and ~$100K of payroll on a salary analysis, then emailed raises to the "underpaid" without cutting the overpaid — costing about $600K/yr in profit for no value. Lesson: HR's customer is the company, not employees; the company's job is to buy the most value at the lowest price; only change comp when you can't attract or retain talent.

## Frameworks & models
- **Three stakeholders**: shareholders (own it), customers (pay it), employees (run it). All considered; the company itself is everyone's customer.
- **Price vs value**: employees are human capital; profit lives in the gap between value received and price paid. Paying more for the same value turns good deals into bad ones.
- **Core vs operations**: marketing/sales bring revenue in, product delivers value; operations (IT, HR, finance, legal) must happen but is supporting — it should "always enable sales, marketing, and product."
- **Two kinds of market value**: what the market pays for the role vs what *this* person yields for you.
- **"Disappear tomorrow" test**: how much less would we make if this person were gone? That sets their real value.
- **HR's value**: (1) protect the company (default "no" to new hires until utilization proves need); (2) recruit the best talent — "the who, not the what."
- **Assimilate-or-reject culture**: high-performance teams spit out low performers fast and retain winners ("Gym Launch is a dangerous place to work" — borrowed from Grant Cardone).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Only raise comp to fix an attraction problem or a retention problem. If neither exists, "you're paying fine."
- Pay stars more to keep them: a star salesperson often out-produces 2–3+ normal reps; a 10–20% raise is cheap vs losing half your sales.
- Non-core roles earn raises only with a quantified case (e.g., a CS rep who saves 25% of the team's saves → incremental raise justified).
- Hiring request → first answer "no": show hour-by-hour utilization; many "overworked" people don't know how to work.
- When he put operations first on mentors' advice, the company declined; he reverted to sales/marketing/product primary.

## Playbook steps
1. Before any comp change, ask: what problem are we solving (attract or retain)?
2. Value each role by its contribution to the bottom line (disappear test).
3. Make an economic argument for any raise.
4. Gate new headcount on proven utilization.
5. Invest HR effort in recruiting top talent; build a high-performance culture.

## Mistakes he warns against
- "My job is to make employees happy" — people are responsible for their own happiness.
- "Employees are my customer" / "I'm a middleman between employees and ownership."
- Raises with no explanation and no value returned (irreversible).
- Overstaffing support functions (6 HR people in a ~100-person company), which then invent busywork.
- Treating it as "just the company's money" — it's the owner's profit.

## Examples / case details
- Salary study: ~20–25% overpaid, ~20–25% underpaid, ~50% fair; underpaid got raises by email; overpaid kept pay. Estimated ~$600K profit cost.
- Law-firm internship: senior partner told him "you don't even know what work is yet."
- Gym Launch: very low attrition — "we fire people like crazy, but people don't quit."
