## Core thesis
Alex's year-in-review of what grew his portfolio (the caption itself says "crush 2025" and recaps 2024–25, even though the title says 2026). Growth came from three things: **talent** (key-player recruiting), **top-down focus** (one priority, delete everything else), and **CRO**. Stagnation usually comes from one of the "six horsemen", each a choice between "pain today vs pain forever." Throughout, he puts problems ahead of shiny opportunities, recommends pay-for-performance and "kind not nice" culture, and says to build brand over years with a 3:1 give:ask ratio. Solo talk by Alex (mentions Leila's input).

## Frameworks & models
- **Plan 12 weeks, not years**: only the next 12 weeks plus 1–2 year-end targets. Growth is a staircase (flatline → figure something out → flatline).
- **One biggest domino**: of tasks A–E, pick the one that most advances the vision, put all people on A, then B–E get easier. Agency example: invest in brand → solves leads, show rate and close rate at once. "That's called leverage."
- **Acquisition.com logo**: the cross is the supply/demand curve and the triangle is the fulcrum of leverage. "What are the fewest things that have to be true for me to win?"
- **Supply- vs demand-constrained**: supply-constrained businesses (cleaning, accounting, legal, niche consulting, home services) are really in the business of attracting, hiring, training, managing and incentivizing talent. Demand-constrained businesses (ecom, B2B SaaS, consumer, weight loss/fitness, music lessons) need customers.
- **Price talent like an asset**: a plumber worth $400K in gross profit a year is worth paying up to ~$400K minus a risk discount. Pay staff big referral bonuses ($25–50K, not $1K). Every month of delay costs that month's gross profit (~$35K/mo, so ~$105K for 3 months).
- **SCALE loop**: Start → Compound (do more) → Augment (do better) → Leverage (make consistent, automate) → Expand (next channel, product or territory). Content example: 1 post a day → 3 → better → AI/prep so every post clears 100K–250K → TikTok/Snapchat.
- **Optimization sequence (credited to Elon)**: question the requirements (define each department's output) → delete → optimize (most result for the least resource first) → pull up timelines → automate.
- **Six Horsemen of Business Stagnation**: (1) underpriced, (2) overcompensation, (3) serving infinite avatars, (4) overextension, (5) running more than one business, (6) "no data daddy."
- **"5 and 20" price ladder** (borrowed from lifting progression): sell 5 at a price. If most say yes or you net more money, raise 20% and repeat until you net less.
- **Missed opportunities < problems** (frame from Leila): problems are finite and opportunities are infinite. About 9 times in 10, the urge is a missed opportunity. Half-built bridges get no dollar across. "Missed opportunities are the gateway drug to mediocrity."
- **"Rush is imaginary"**: urgency comes from the reference point you choose to compare yourself to (5-year-old analogy; Buffett bought his first stock at 7). Only winner-take-all network markets justify rushing.
- **Brand ratio**: New Balance's CMO flipped spend from 70% bottom-of-funnel / 30% top to 30/70. ROAS fell for 18 months and rose from month 19. Keep content:ads at about 3:1 (the TV/radio/feed norm), meaning 3–4 positives before any ask.
- **B2B brand buckets**: help them reach their goals for free (content); show you helped someone like them (proof); achieve an aspirational outcome yourself (North Face/Everest, Range Rover, Red Bull space jump, CBum, his $46M exit, now a goal of crossing $1B).
- **Sawdust**: build products from waste or excess capacity (plywood, earmuffs from fur scraps, Airbnb/Uber, a gym empty from 8:30pm to 4:30am). Look for moves that check 4 boxes at once: deal flow, cash flow, content, talent flow.
- **IRL + AI barbell**: double down on both ends. In-person customers are more loyal, refer more, rate higher and buy more. "Host the party" effect. "Not the AI company — a company that uses AI well."
- **Flywheels** (Jim Collins): Acquisition.com's loop is business media → interested owners → deals → outcomes → more media. Amazon's is price/reviews → customers → suppliers → selection. Find and smooth the friction points in the loop.
- **Insult vs criticism**: an insult attaches a negative label. Criticism is the gap between desired and actual ("expectation 9:00, you arrive 9:05"; "channel average 100K, yours 75K"). Follow it with behavioral steps and "do this instead."
- **What / Who / When**: ask "how many hours will this take?", not "what day?". Recap every item after each new one (the first item gets heard about 7 times). With a weak team, have them repeat it back; about 1 in 3 get it wrong.
- **Run every department like a sales team**: game tape → role-play → half calendar → full → uncapped calendar if quality KPIs hold. Daily huddle reviews good and bad work, with the person self-correcting first and the team adding praise.
- **Technical vs management track**: if the only way up is headcount, people hoard skills, ask for more hires, kiss up to whoever promotes, and show work instead of doing work. Build a pay track for top individual contributors.
- **Problems and solutions have delays**: today's problems were made 6+ months ago. Before starting a fix, set how long it gets before you judge it. In good times ask "what did we do 6 months ago?"
- **Culture** = rules that govern reinforcement. Keep 3 values, each with hundreds of behaviors under it. "Kind not nice." A pro sports team, not a family (Reed Hastings).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you're at or near full capacity and not making money, raise the price. Selling 3 at 2× beats 5 at half: more revenue and fewer people to serve.
- Overcompensated employees: first hire someone who can do the role at the new pay, then have the conversation ("sleep on it"), and keep their old pay for 2–3 months before switching.
- Infinite avatars (common under $3M): 80/20 your customers, build messaging and an offer only for the top 20%, and price for the ~5× value you give them.
- Overextension (common at $1–3M): don't open a second location until the first passes at least a 1-month away test. His preferred bar is 6 straight months of the business holding or growing without your direct involvement. Otherwise hire impressive people (giving up some of your income) or prune and reconcentrate.
- More than one business: pick one.
- Only track data whose change would change your behavior.
- $1–10M businesses should grow far faster than 10%/yr. If they don't, it's usually "high headcount, low talent count" plus no focus.
- Fixing a department: delete all recurring meetings first ("you can always add it back") and replace them with a daily huddle plus ad hoc stand-ups.
- Pull deadlines from end of week to end of day; on a weekly cadence that's ~7× speed. Use renewable capacity now because the future won't match today.
- Never accept "we can't." Ask "what would it take?" and then decide whether that trade is worth it.
- CRO: run 3–10 split tests at once on the key pipeline steps. Expect most tests on an already-optimized page to lose (Skool: 2 of 16 won).
- Test 3–4 competing customer-facing third-party tools on conversion rate.
- Pay for performance: existing staff keep their salary with performance pay added on top, and new hires start on the performance plan. Flat bonuses by tier work if budget is tight. Back-test the plan on last month's numbers. Buying 3× output at 2× cost is still a deal.
- Fire low performers when things are good, not when they're bad.
- Remote vs in person: decide role by role (e.g. media in person, finance remote). His portfolio is ~30% in person / 70% remote. For relocating hires, offer a signing bonus and a 30-day hotel trial.
- When a metric declines, look at where growth first flattened, not where it started dropping. The cause is often someone you added, not someone you lost.
- Talk to a key hire's spouse. Neutral support turns negative over time.
- Content: stay on your avatar for long-form. Adjacent topics are fine in short-form.

## Playbook steps
1. Set the 12-week priority: pick the one domino and delete the rest of the discretionary work.
2. Diagnose the constraint: supply or demand. If supply, build talent attraction (big referral bonuses, community, comp).
3. Check the six horsemen and take the short-term pain: raise price with 5-and-20, fix comp, niche down, prune locations, drop extra businesses, start collecting data.
4. For a broken department: question requirements → delete meetings → optimize → pull up timelines → automate. Then add pay-for-performance and sales-team-style management.
5. For a working department: run the SCALE loop.
6. Recruit key players personally as CEO, align incentives ruthlessly, then get out of their way.
7. Run CRO sprints continuously. In his example it was one person plus a couple of devs running ~100 tests.
8. Brand: keep the 3:1 give:ask ratio, measure in years, and use the three B2B buckets.
9. Keep a "big money ideas" list. Let ideas marinate (Lincoln's unsent letters) and pick one only when you have bandwidth.
10. Content intros: proof, promise and plan in the first ~30s, a lower-third introduction, heavy pre-production for education, and examples matched to the avatar at a low grade level.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Over-detailed long-term projections ("mental masturbation").
- Spreading priorities across people, or adding objectives without deleting any.
- $1K referral bonuses for roles worth $400K a year.
- Taking your foot off the gas once money feels "good enough."
- Unsupportive spouses or partners. In his retroactive look, the star founders had full spouse support, and fizzles often followed a relationship "upgrade."
- Chasing missed opportunities and building half-built bridges. Rushing on arbitrary private timelines.
- "No man left behind" training cultures, and being nice instead of kind.
- Headcount-only promotion ladders.
- Judging a solution after 7 days.
- Wide, vanity-view content. His 16-week experiment with meals, workouts and vlogs brought in fewer absolute business owners.
- Frankenstein products built from every customer request. The #1 reason people cancel is overwhelm, so remove rather than add ("value per second").

## Examples / case details
- Three companies he started since 2020 reached $100M+ valuations. Portfolio went from ~$50M EBITDA to just under $100M (from the intro recap).
- Diligence example: a downsell almost nobody took (~5% of revenue) was turned into a free lead magnet, and lead flow tripled the next week.
- Largest portfolio company (8 figures+ a month): CRO lifts of +20%, +50% and +28% from ~100 tests.
- Top portfolio company had about 6 great leaders placed. Best companies (#1, #2, #4) had worked with Acquisition.com for 6–12 months before the investment, which led to workshops using spare office desks.
- Bought aq.com from Yahoo for $480,000. Launched ACQ Ventures (SaaS and tech-enabled services, family-office capital, minority-style); first 3 deals.
- Free $100M Scaling Roadmap: 8 departments × 10 stages, 14 hours, at acquisition.com/training.
- Skool: average paid community makes $1,360/mo. Games offer plus a Cybertruck prize. His view: every business (gym, realtor, HOA, ecom) should have a community.
- Took over media and conversion after ~5 years away from day-to-day work. Output more than doubled after removing meetings and aligning incentives. TikTok was up 300% the week the incentives changed.
- Ascension rates dropped; the cause was a bad new department head.
- Gym Launch: he was richer the day before the sale than after it (taxes), but got more kudos for selling than for owning.
- Personal: 100 days straight with no days off, ~10–12 days off in the year, ~90 hrs/wk. Sleep: pitch black, cold (a ~$2K temperature mattress pad), foam earplugs (resting HR down 4 bpm). Two desks plus a ~$100 kneeling chair. Trains only on weekends. 203 lb, down from a 250 lb peak, focused on longevity.
- Platforms: email was the MVP or most improved. Facebook had the highest book sales per follower/view. IG and YouTube drive the most traffic. Amazon books drive ~15% of traffic. Books: 2,000+ hrs each, ~1M copies, 26K five-star reviews.
- Covid: tight headcount beforehand meant few cuts and morale held.
