## Core thesis
Three questions he uses "like weapons" when making bets in the business: "What problem are you trying to solve?" (when someone proposes an action), "What would it take?" (when he wants something), and "What do you want to have happen?" Probing follow-ups test the quality of the thinking behind a proposal. These usually reveal that the best option is to do nothing and keep putting resources into what already works. The title says "get rich," but the content is about decision-making questions. [inference]

## Frameworks & models
- **Q1, "What problem are you trying to solve?"**: ask it when someone wants approval for an action. It exposes actions driven by emotional discomfort rather than a real problem. Example: an employee engagement program proposed with no retention or attraction problem behind it.
- **Q2, "What would it take?"**: ask it instead of "Can we/Could we?" It assumes success and gets you the inputs, so you make the worth-it call with full context instead of letting others say no on your behalf. Use it to narrow down: a billion dollars? No. A million? No. → "We need to hire someone" ($100K hire for an extra $1M/yr = do it). A "no" often just means "not with existing infrastructure/priorities," and you can re-prioritize.
- **Q3, "What do you want to have happen?"**: cuts past the emotional noise to the desired outcome.
- **Probe sequence**: "What does that mean?" → "How do you know that?" (how would we measure it, e.g. before/after well-being scores) → "So what?" (how does this connect to a business outcome?). Then: "What else could we do with this time and energy that would yield a higher return?"
- **"Do nothing" is the undefeated heavyweight champion**: among options A, B, or do nothing, doing nothing is so often correct. Keep allocating to the proven highest-return thing rather than a maybe-equal, uncertain one.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If a proposal can't name the problem it solves, don't do it.
- Lack of focus = intellectual laziness: people do what they know how to do instead of staying "incompetent" on the task that actually matters.
- If you're distracted, you're avoiding the hard thing you don't know how to do.
- A sure return on a known channel beats a possibly equal return on a new one.

## Playbook steps
1. When asked to approve something: "What problem are we solving?"
2. If it's unclear: "What do you want to have happen?"
3. Probe: What does that mean? How do you know? So what?
4. Compare against the best alternative use of time and energy; default to doing nothing / doubling down.
5. When you want something: "What would it take (to do it in half the time / 24 hrs instead of 30 days / buy this company / double content from 10 to 20 a day)?" Then decide whether it's worth it.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Taking action to relieve emotional discomfort with the present moment.
- Asking "Can we?", which invites people with incomplete context to say no for you.
- One-off "kumbaya" morale events. He believes an ongoing environment that rewards high effort and removes people who underperform is what makes people enjoy work.
