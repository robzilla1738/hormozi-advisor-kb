## Core thesis
"A good book is a complete solution to a narrowly defined problem." *$100M Offers* sold 200K+ copies in a year through word of mouth alone (no publisher, no ad campaign, launched before he had an audience). His process: narrow the book to one problem, split it into 3–6 solution buckets, attach a lived story to each, and stress-test the framework until it can't be broken. Then edit to ~3rd-grade readability and get honest reader feedback. Storytelling works the same way in copy and ads. This was recorded while *$100M Leads* was being written.

## Frameworks & models
- **Purpose test**: write to add to the body of knowledge and compress years into hours, not for money or accolades (he cites ~1.6M books published a year).
- **Word-of-mouth test**: "If people aren't sharing it on their own then it's not worth promoting because it's not good enough." Design for the moment someone says "I wish I had more leads" and a friend replies "read this book."
- **Book architecture**: one problem, then 3–6 buckets, then the experiences that shaped each belief, then a framework per chapter. Insert stories where the content "starts to get heavy." Example sequence: *Offers* (what to sell) → *Leads* (who to sell to). The *Leads* sections: understand leads, get leads, get others to get you leads, get more leads, do it.
- **Story frame**: setting, character, desire, struggle, eureka moment, victory, resolution. Tell both the external and internal journeys (a victory can feel empty).
- **Craft rules**: use all five senses; show, don't tell (Stephen King's example of the pale ring line on a finger instead of "his ex-wife"). Include a detail only if changing it would change the story (deer vs buffalo pelt).
- **Stakes and struggle make a story interesting**: the Uber Eats story becomes gripping once you add a diabetic about to pass out. Game shows and poker need real stakes.
- **Seven newsworthiness factors**: recency, impact (bridge to the reader), prominence, proximity (name the specific suburb in local ads), conflict, unusualness (break the expected pattern), updatability. Two or three per story is enough.
- **Frameworks = documented decision checklists.** Judge them on usefulness and validity, where validity means how many circumstances they still work in (E=mc²). Test in new situations, revise to cover both, repeat until you can't break it. If the model breaks mid-draft, restart the book.
- **Editing**: go from 12th-grade to 3rd-grade language plus more pictures (he says the average American reads at a 5th–7th grade level). Use the Hemingway app for instant feedback. Cut sentences that don't change meaning, prefer short sentences and words, and remove adverbs (use the right verb: "ran," not "walked quickly"). Use simple tenses. "If you can't make something sound simple it's because you don't get it, not them."
- **Feedback**: 4–8 ideal readers who value you but won't flatter you. If every reader gives different feedback, you're fine; if they all flag the same thing, change it (King). Then a third/final draft and a copy editor.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If a section doesn't serve the single problem, cut it ("kill your darlings").
- He spends ~1,000 hours per book, 20–25 hours a week (writing 4–5 hours before starting the workday). Eight drafts, ~600 pages written for ~200 kept. Aims for a book a year.

## Playbook steps
1. Define the single problem and the reader's desired outcome.
2. Outline 3–6 buckets with a story and a framework each.
3. Draft until you hit an inconsistency, then restart.
4. Simplify the language with the Hemingway app.
5. Send to 4–8 honest readers, revise, then copy-edit.
6. Launch with an early-bird list (the last launch sold out in under an hour).

## Mistakes he warns against
- Books that name hundreds of problems and solve none.
- Treating a book as word count plus a cover.
- Telling instead of showing in copy and ads.
