# How to Build a Valuable Company You Can Sell Someday

## Core thesis
Separate the goose (the engine that creates value, often valuable but not sellable, e.g., a founder-led education or media business) from the eggs (sellable, faceless, recurring assets it produces). Keep the goose and sell the eggs. For coaches and educators, this usually means rolling up the best students' businesses into exits. If you are bigger than the company, you're key-man risk, so don't exit: raise a minority stake at the holdco level instead.

## Frameworks & models
- **Goose vs eggs**: "There's something that creates value and then there's the thing that is valuable." Example: acquisition.com is his goose; the portfolio companies are the eggs.
- **What makes something sellable**: no dependence on the founder (no key-man risk), sticky/recurring revenue (or reoccurring consumables, like Coca-Cola), faceless operations, predictable acquisition, and proven buyer demand.
- **Validate by M&A activity**: search for "<your business type> M&A activity." If there are few deals, there's no market. If there are some, study how those businesses differ from yours. Treat the business as a product and the acquirer as the customer.
- **Coaching-to-rollup model**: if you teach a business model (event venues, Amazon stores, accounting firms), standardize students on your systems and price points. Then roll up the top ~10% in groups of 20–50 every year or two, taking a 20–30% promoter slice, while keeping the goose that brings in more students.
- **Volume premium in investing**: "big money is lazy," so it pays higher multiples for bigger aggregated profit to avoid many small deals. Example: firms worth 4× alone that sell together at 12× and give up 30% still net ~8.4×, about double (math checks).
- **Franchise law test (name + system + fee)**: all three together make a franchise, and operating one without franchise registration is illegal. Pick two: name + fee (CrossFit), systems + fee without a shared name (Gym Launch), or name + systems without a fee.
- **Raise, don't exit, when you're bigger than the company**: sell 5–10% at the holdco to take chips off the table or fund a big acquisition. That's a liquidity event, not an exit (MrBeast's Beast Industries; Elon via public companies).
- **Media companies: two paths**: push the stable's impressions into one owned (ideally reoccurring) product, or sell ad space. The stars can't be sold; the product (egg) can. A minority raise can front inventory and logistics when fame outstrips wealth.
- **Collaborate, don't compete**: PE aggregators ignore local feuds and roll competitors together. "We're competing against the couch" (he says ~11% of Americans have a gym membership).
- **Group-buying co-op**: a dental association of ~700 dentists (each spending $20–30K/yr on consumables) pooled purchasing so the savings exceeded the membership fee.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If anything depends on you, the business is inherently worth much less.
- Micro-gyms rarely sell (key-man risk at their level too). Franchise chains and accounting firms trade often (high revenue retention).
- Don't try to sell a product into a market that doesn't exist (no buyers for that business type).
- When building toward an exit, grow the company bigger than your personal brand. Gym Launch was bigger than him when he sold it.

## Playbook steps
1. List your business lines and customer types; mark each as goose or egg.
2. Check M&A comps for each egg type.
3. Remove yourself from delivery and ads; build a leadership team, recurring revenue and multiple acquisition channels.
4. If you coach operators, pre-standardize them for a future rollup.
5. Aggregate eggs into larger packages to capture the volume premium.
6. For the goose, consider a minority holdco raise rather than a sale.

## Mistakes he warns against
- "Killing the goose": trying to sell the founder-dependent engine.
- Coaching businesses with key-man risk, low stickiness and volatile acquisition expecting a big exit.
- Accidentally running an illegal franchise.
- Wasting energy on local competitor feuds.

## Examples / case details
- An event-venue operator sold 3 venues, then started coaching. Advice: roll up the students' venues.
- An Amazon store operator turned educator. Same advice.
- An accounting-firm coach. Advice: roll up 10–20 firms and exit every 1–2 years for ~$100M.
- The dental association rolled up its top 11 dentists and sold for $120M. The PE buyer (they were bidding against ACQ) also bought the association itself as a pipeline of "eggs" for the next 5 years.
- Gym Launch: sellable because it had no key-man dependence, recurring revenue, good annual retention and multiple channels. He says he has built and sold nine companies, the last for $46.2M.
- Promo: ACQ workshop division.
