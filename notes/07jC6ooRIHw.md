## Core thesis
Most entrepreneurs are in a different business than they think. Once the basics are solved, the real business shows up as a "big concrete wall": the second- or third-order constraint that unlocks enterprise value. That wall is exactly when the "woman in the red dress" (a shiny new opportunity) appears. Price the wall (what is breaking through worth?), commit for years ("marry" the business), and keep swinging the hammer instead of jumping vehicles.

## Frameworks & models
- **What you think vs the business you're really in** (his examples):
  - Gyms: you think fitness; it's actually marketing and sales (acquisition machines; fitness is "almost an afterthought" for big franchisors).
  - Supplements (Prestige Labs): you think what's in the bottle; it's actually brand plus a media/traffic machine.
  - Software: you think marketing and sales; it's actually product and delivery. "Software tends to sell itself," yet he had outsourced the dev, which was the core.
  - Cleaning: you think acquisition (CAC ~$25); it's actually recruiting and training low-skill labor at volume.
  - Professional services/consulting: you think marketing and sales; it's actually talent: recruiting the best, retaining them, and partnership/LLP ownership tracks so they don't leave with clients.
- **Business as a car**: every part is needed, but ask which part gives the most return in this vehicle.
- **Take your model to its natural extreme**: study the biggest players in your space to see what they actually optimize. "Your customer has to realize what the differentiator is"; if only you know it, it isn't one.
- **Paired incentives/metrics** (Andy Grove, *High Output Management*): pair quality with speed. Support: resolution % plus completion speed. Cleaning: pay per clean (speed), plus the cleaner redoes bad jobs free (quality), which also became the customer guarantee.
- **Price the wall**: imagine the pile of money on the other side. For example, "if we solve it we get an extra $250M" for a portfolio company's media-to-software shift.
- **Woman in the red dress**: shiny distractions get "hotter" the better you get at business.
- **Married vs dating your business**: marriage freed ~30–40% of his attention from "chasing tail"; committing to one business does the same.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Before switching vehicles, ask: is there a $100M or $1B version of this business? Usually yes. Then 10× the current one. Example: roofer at $4M top line / $1M profit → "why don't you just 10x?"
- The real wall only appears at scale, after the basics are solved, often a couple of years in.
- Expect the wall to take 2–5 years. Frame it as "if in 5 years this is a $100M enterprise, is it worth it?"
- Don't outsource the core constraint (product in software; acquisition, brand and media in consumer goods).
- Say no to side partnerships, networking lunches and businesses kept warm on the side.

## Playbook steps
1. Identify what limits growth now that the basics work.
2. Look at the largest companies in your category to see what they excel at.
3. Name the real business (the constraint).
4. Put a dollar value on solving it, and set a multi-year expectation with the team.
5. Build paired metrics for the core workforce.
6. Commit; refuse shiny objects.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Competing on what customers can't perceive (better workouts, better ingredients).
- Assuming the next business has the same constraint as the last one (the gym owner turned cleaning operator).
- Single metrics that get gamed (speed without resolution, or vice versa).
- Jumping to a new vehicle the moment the wall appears.

## Examples / case details
- Prestige Labs: Dr. Kashi (PhD biochemist) made top-tier formulas, but success depended on brand and traffic. People take AG1 for the taste and feel, not the contents. Placebo is real.
- A friend's Airbnb cleaning company: CAC ~$25. Constraint: hiring reliable cleaners. Pay per clean, plus a free re-clean guarantee.
- EY, KPMG, McKinsey, Bain, BCG and big law firms are talent businesses with partner tracks.
- Promo: ACQ workshop division (1–2 days a month in Vegas).
