# My Best Sales Tactic (to Make a TON of Money)

## Core thesis
Build trust quickly by recommending *against* buying part of your own list: tell them to skip an item or get it cheaper elsewhere (a "ghost product", which he used to call a "sacrificial lamb"). Then make a prescriptive close on the rest, with exact usage tied to habits they already have, and charge the card on file. He says that after he started doing this he closed nearly everyone in a retail supplement setting. He taught it to people who had never sold before, and they closed 80–90% of walk-ins.

## Frameworks & models
- **Ghost products / sacrificial lambs**: put an item on the recommendation list, then cross it out ("get it at Costco", "you don't need this one"). Acting against your own interest buys trust for everything else on the list.
- **Prescriptive close**: explain exactly how to take or use each item *before* asking for the sale. Then: "you're good to go, want to use the card on file?" This is a one-click upsell with no new purchase decision.
- **Habit piggybacking**: ask what they do every morning no matter what (brush teeth, coffee). Put the product next to that, e.g. tape a "2" on it next to the toothbrush. New habits are hard, so attach the product to an existing one. This makes you a better salesperson *and* a better coach.
- **Budget objection ladder**: "Are you on a budget? Want me to rank these by importance?" Remove the lowest-priority item first. If they still can't afford it, remove another but reintroduce the problem it solved ("you'll have to eat a pound of broccoli"). Often they keep it.
- **Old identity → found money**: ask what they'll stop doing on the program (drinking, going out, smoking) and how much they spend on it. "We just found some money."
- **Move to their side of the table**: you start across the table and should end shoulder to shoulder, looking at the decision together.
- **Manipulation vs help = intention**: the same persuasion with positive intent is help.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Use the lowest-margin items as ghost products. Give away trust on low margin and keep the high-margin items.
- Never present a removed item as unimportant, or the whole list looks like filler.
- Only use this if you believe in what you sell.
- For services (e.g. web design): send non-core tasks to a cheaper outside resource ("guys in the Philippines, I'll connect you").

## Playbook steps
1. Build a recommendation list from their goal. 2. Cross out 1–2 low-margin or irrelevant items, or send them elsewhere cheaper. 3. For each remaining item, give the benefit and exact usage tied to an existing daily habit. 4. Close on the card on file. 5. On budget pushback, rank by importance, remove the least important item, and reintroduce the problem if needed. 6. Find money from the old-identity spending they'll drop.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Skirting around a missing or unneeded item instead of openly saying they don't need it from you.
- Making people form new habits instead of piggybacking on existing ones.
- Acting as if the items you remove don't matter.

## Examples / case details
- Gym supplement sales after a challenge launch with about 100 new customers. A key product ran out, so he told a customer to get it at Costco, and then everyone bought the rest. He then deliberately sent two products elsewhere ("save $10–20") and crossed out a mass gainer for a woman who wasn't trying to gain mass. Over time he stopped even stocking the ghost products.
- Career advice: attach yourself to a high-volume, low-ticket business's sales process (car washes, massage, nails, hair) for rep after rep. He says this teaches more than any course and made high-ticket selling feel like "a breeze."
- He says this will be in the $100M sales book.
