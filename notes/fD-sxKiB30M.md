## Core thesis
Most millionaires come from real estate; most billionaires come from private equity. PE makes more because a business's value can be *forced* up much more than a house's. You can turn risks into "pillars of value." That raises both the profit (EBITDA) and the multiple paid on it. Value = how much profit × how reliable and growing it is. A small, founder-dependent, risky business can be picked up for almost nothing and, in 12–24 months, become worth tens of millions. It also takes patience: sticking with one business compounds enterprise value.

## Frameworks & models
- **Real estate vs private equity**: a house makes money from appreciation (natural or forced, e.g. adding a kitchen) plus rent. It's a simple, reliable model while population grows (Japan shows the risk when it shrinks). The upside has a ceiling: you "can't put it in Manhattan," and there are no 100× deals. Businesses can go from ~0 to hundreds of millions, and you can start one with almost no money.
- **Flip risks into pillars of value**: list every negative and risk, then flip them one by one. Each one is "counted twice": the discount goes away and it adds to the multiple. Examples: finding a reliable acquisition channel, or one key hire who opens a new territory.
- **Multiple = perceived risk**: the multiple says how likely the business is to keep making money if nothing changed, e.g. if the owner leaves. Near-guaranteed earns a high multiple. "Could burn down in 6 months" earns about 0.5× or zero.
- **EBITDA**: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. You strip out things a buyer could change, like the state of incorporation or a one-off equipment purchase. The two levers are EBITDA and the multiple.
- **Two identical $10M revenue / $3M profit businesses**: (A) transactional, one acquisition channel, founder in delivery, marketing and ops, declining from $20M/$6M. It is "basically unsellable" to outsiders. (B) ARR, growing $5M→$10M→pacing $20M, margins expanding. It might get 12× = $36M.
- **Size of profit × reliability**: lots of profit that isn't reliable gets a small multiple. Small but reliable and growing gets a big multiple. Lots of profit that is reliable and fast-growing gets a "monster multiple."
- **Only 3 ways to create value**: more customers, more value per customer (LTV/LTGP), less risk (more reliability). Every allocation of time or money must clearly tie to one of these.
- **Multiple levers (A–E)**:
  - A, Debt capacity: a function of cash flow. More cash flow means more debt the business can carry.
  - B, Organic growth: marketing, sales and pricing, as opposed to inorganic M&A. With steady 20%/yr growth the business doubles in about 5 years. With 20% down and debt, the buyer could make 4–5× their money.
  - C, Categorization: traditional service < tech-enabled service < SaaS.
  - D, Size premium: the opposite of a wholesale discount. More profit gets a higher multiple. Big funds have minimum check sizes, e.g. one bank's is $150M, so they pay a premium for aggregated assets.
  - E, Age: older businesses are worth more. "A business always becomes more valuable every single year until it doesn't, and then it's worth nothing" (attributed to the managing partner at APG).
- **Mediocre opportunity executed forever beats switching**: someone who switches loses the compounding of their current business's later years.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Around $5M profit/yr is where institutional investors begin to take notice. Below that, "you're not really going to get much." Crossing $10M profit adds a further size premium.
- Key-man risk at ~$1M profit is normal: "of course you have keyman risk, it's a tiny business." You solve it at institutional scale.
- Tech investment test: about $100K of tech/process that 5–10× delivery capacity might add about 3 turns (5×→8×). On $1M profit that is +$3M EV, a 30× return. Reinvest rather than take the cash to the S&P.
- If you can't clearly say how a spend gets customers, raises their value or reduces risk, question it.
- Real estate: buy where people are moving or having kids.
- Don't sell early if one more year can fix a few things and grow profit.

## Playbook steps
1. Audit every risk in the business: founder dependence, single channel, transactional revenue, mispricing.
2. Hire a CEO/COO and a marketing director, upgrade sales ops, and fix pricing. Better pricing funds more marketing, which brings more sales. He says this can take 12–24 months.
3. Shift toward recurring revenue and steady growth.
4. Invest in tech so the business is categorized as tech-enabled.
5. Grow profit past the ~$5M and $10M size thresholds.
6. Keep growing, even slowly, every year. Never let it decline.
7. Stay focused on one business for years.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Selling to an uninformed retail buyer (a local doctor or lawyer) isn't proof of value. "You pulled one over on someone."
- Building a business that is valuable only to you. A $200K/yr barber shop you work in isn't $200K/yr to anyone else.
- Spending on things that don't move customers, value or risk.
- Shiny-object switching: starting a new side hustle every 6 months, or neglecting the business that pays.
- Letting the business decline. Then "no one wants to touch it" except distressed-asset buyers.
- Getting greedy in real estate.

## Examples / case details
- $3M revenue / $1M profit business, roughly 10–15 employees and "riddled with risk": you might pay something like $500K down plus seller financing. Grown to $12M revenue / $5M profit at 8×, it's worth $40M in 12–24 months.
- Size and patience: selling at "25" versus waiting a year to grow from $5M to $8M profit and selling at $75M. That's +$50M of EV, 3× the price, for one more year. He also says that at $5M profit "you might get somewhere in the neighborhood of like 20 to 40." [inference: this is probably $20–40M EV, i.e. about 4–8×; the transcript is ambiguous.]
