## Frameworks & models
- **Roll-up integration = "the world's longest checklist"**: break an acquired business into components (route/customer list, field workers, admin; skip their sales/marketing if yours is better — "you're trying to buy the book of business"), write an SOP per component, add to the checklist after every integration. Recommends studying Waste Management and the book "How to Make a Few Billion Dollars." Rebrand under one brand if you want the big play ("you'll get a premium"). Keep sellers incentivized with a post-integration bonus.
- **Test-cost ordering**: every test has a guaranteed cost of change (mess-ups, retraining) but an unguaranteed upside. Rank tests by cost vs likely output (subject-line split test = cheap; offer change = expensive) and pull from the top. Avoid both extremes (test everything vs test nothing).
- **"Alex's big list of ideas"**: he had ~1 new idea/day, Leila said the business can absorb ~1/quarter. Write ideas down and let them sit; if still good "with a month of sleep," test it. Content is also his creative outlet.
- **Structural churn**: VSMB (very small business) customers churn by nature, close to consumer. Shopify-style dual monetization: low entry fee, upside from payments on the users who succeed.
- **Improve product by elimination, not addition**: the #1 cancel reason is overwhelm. Show one next action; reveal the next only after they complete it. Use cohort regression to find the activation point and put ALL resources there (no growth initiatives, no new features) while it is the constraint.
- **Enterprise selling**: the more money the buyer makes, the less they care about narrative; show the math/ROI. Make data sharing a contingent requirement of working with you ("our proprietary tech feeds off your data").
- **Chop the unicorn's horn**: key-man skills are rare because they're combinations; break them into components (phone sales, ads, retention) and hire a specialist for each. "It took three people to replace my unicorn."
- **Insurance / maintenance after a one-time big purchase**: charge ~5–10% of purchase price per year to protect it (fur-coat summer storage; "spent $20K on your mouth, spend $1K/yr to keep it"). Sell right after the sale, while they're looking in the mirror.
- **Every business incurs debt**: bootstrapped = leadership/management/technical debt (you do 2–3 jobs to fund the hire that levels you up); venture-backed = financial debt/dilution. VC goes faster; he thinks bootstrapped is most fundamentally sound long term. Best of both: self-fund new businesses.
- **"Your lifestyle is your competitor's opportunity"**: distributions you take can't be reinvested. Hiring timing = risk tolerance; the entrepreneur's dilemma is consumption vs investment. Full reinvestment matters mainly in network-effect races.
- **Learn marketing from agencies**: pay an agency more to teach you everything over a longer period plus a consulting period after (script in the agency chapter of *$100M Leads*). Prefers agencies to coaches because "agencies have to produce results or they get fired."
- **"Play it out"**: ask "what happens next?" repeatedly; fears are catastrophized ("a mile wide, an inch deep"). Cites hedonic adaptation research (paralysis → baseline well-being in ~3–6 months).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Med spa lead-with-popular vs lead-with-profitable is "a math question": Botox GP $58 + 40% upsell × $1,300 GP = $520 → $578 per customer vs CAC $202 ≈ just under 3:1. He judged CAC fine and upsell size the lever (bigger bundles, financing, menu close; he bets the ticket could go to $5–6K). Lead with the high-ticket item only if its CAC is low enough (e.g. ~$300) to beat that.
- 33%/month churn in VSMB is high (11× worse than 3%); he bets it can get closer to 10%.
- Sell-or-stay on an exit: a risk assessment. Sure you'll grow → don't sell; unsure → sell. Weigh deal terms (all cash and walk away vs seller finance/earnout/51% while you keep working). "If what you do every day doesn't change, why sell?" His terms: stop working the day the check clears. Have a clear plan for what's next; exiting with time, money and nothing to do is a bad combo.
- "Businesses will always go up in value until the day they don't, and then they're worth nothing." One-time-sale, ad-dependent revenue is riskier to hold.
- Agency selection: talk to ~10 before buying; you're ready when you stop learning new things on calls (for him ~15 expert calls to understand a space). Red flag: an agency that won't give best practices (speed to lead within 5 minutes, scripts, show-rate tactics). Offer: "no results in 30 days I'm out; results and I pay you a very long time."
- Referral partners: if you make $8K per customer you can pay ~$1K per referral and be happy. Win partners by converting their leads better than others so you can pay more; tell partners exactly who you want (e.g. loans $50K+) and pay fatter slices.
- Thin margin (~$1.5–2K GP per $7K loan) is tight for outbound; acquire affiliates instead.
- Culture: get rid of people who don't have the attitude; "if you're scaling a service business, you're scaling culture and training." How vague your directives can be is proportional to the person's skill.
- Seasonal business: never call it "the offseason," or everyone treats it like one.

## Playbook steps
1. Identify the constraint with numbers (cohort/activation, CAC vs gross-profit LTV).
2. If it's activation/churn, strip the product to the next single action and pour all resources into the activation point.
3. If it's upsell size, build bundles plus financing and use a menu close starting from the ideal.
4. For a one-time high ticket, attach a 5–10%/yr maintenance/"insurance" program at the moment of delight.
5. For new channels: hire a proven agency, pay extra to learn its system, or build referral/affiliate partners in adjacent businesses.
6. Keep a big idea list; run the cheapest high-upside tests first.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Changing delivery of something everyone loves to fit more people. His gym shortened sessions and "destroyed the thing that worked." Raise price instead: "product-market fit is much harder to find than pricing."
- Adding features to fix churn.
- Asking for answers that a test could give.
- Choosing an agency after talking to two ("dating two people and choosing one"): uninformed decisions → bad results.
- Selling a business without a plan for the day after.
- Comparing bootstrapped speed to grant/VC-funded speed.
