# how to outlearn everyone

## Core thesis
"Learning is same condition, new behavior," and "intelligence is speed of learning." That makes intelligence a rate you can raise by doing iterations faster, not only by needing fewer of them. His 7-step process: define learning; deconstruct the skill; define success measurably; ignore the black box (focus on observable inputs and outputs, not "why"); model the top 1–10%; analyze and iterate on your own top 10% "like coats of paint"; repeat. "On a long enough time horizon the speed of iteration is the only thing that matters." The opening claim, "$106 million in sales in less than 3 days" for a Guinness-record book launch, is as stated.

## Frameworks & models
- **Learning / skill / intelligence**: learning = same condition, new behavior. A skill = a chain of adapted behaviors. Intelligence = how fast you learn, which has two levers: fewer iterations, or iterations compressed in time. "I was able to do my 10 iterations faster than they were able to do their three."
- **Chunk down**: "business" isn't a skill. It breaks into marketing, sales, product and hiring, and each of those keeps breaking down until you reach behaviors you can quantify. Generalizable skills (reading, writing, speaking, learning itself) vs specific ones.
- **Define success per subskill**: e.g., foul shots: % in the box, % made, whether the follow-through finishes above the head, shoulders squared.
- **Ignore the black box**: manage the inputs before the desired output and measure the output. Don't theorize about emotional triggers: "We only know did he buy, did he not buy." The only "why" he'll commit to is reinforcement history.
- **Teaching razor**: "If someone cannot tell you what to do with either your mouth or your body, they are not giving you clear directions."
- **Model what top performers do, not what they say**: experts often don't know why they're good (Michael Jordan was a poor coach), and their explanations are rehearsed, rewarded answers.
- **First-party data loop / coats of paint**: do volume, find your top 10%, infer one or two differences, do another batch, repeat. "Your idea and observation density will increase like coats of paint." (He compares this to AI learning from real-world feedback, citing his interview with the founder of Replit.)
- **Testimonials are modeling**: "That person is like me. They got what I want… I will therefore buy this thing."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- "If you don't track, you don't care." Every skill worth learning needs a metric.
- For a new field, hire experts to "get me on iteration 9 as iteration one," then beat them with a faster feedback loop.
- When someone outperforms you, assume "they've done something I haven't done," which he says is true 99 times out of 100. Remove "luck" from your vocabulary.
- "It's better to fail and know why you failed than to succeed and not know why" (a quote he attributes to a Stanford professor).

## Playbook steps
1. Define the target behavior change. 2. Deconstruct the skill into subskills. 3. Write measurable success criteria for each. 4. Study the top 1–10% and list their observable behaviors (handshake timing, posture, nodding, stepping back when asking). 5. Replicate. 6. Do high volume, compare your top 10% vs the rest, and add the differences you find. 7. Repeat until people call you a natural.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Searching for a "recipe" without breaking the skill down.
- Psychology, NLP and "the universe is whispering" mysticism in place of concrete instructions.
- Spacing out iterations (once a week) when you could compress them into a day.
- Calling successful people lucky, which "makes me better in zero ways."

## Examples / case details
- At Vanderbilt he felt below average, so he studied 9am–9pm every day (library, class, cafeteria, gym only).
- Social skills: he learned by observing cool people as an outsider would.
- Most of his current learning is first-party: "We do lots of stuff, we look at what worked and we do more of the stuff that worked."
- At about 20, he was terrified of winning a billion-dollar Powerball because he'd "never be able to prove that I could do it."
