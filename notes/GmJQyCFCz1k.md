## Core thesis
How leaders communicate in a crisis and day to day. In a crisis: tell the truth, take responsibility, and be first ("bad news doesn't get better with time"). Then rebuild reputation through consistent behavior. Day to day, empathy-based messaging, goal-anchored feedback, prepared one-on-ones, and selling the job as freedom over pay are what attract and keep A players.

## Frameworks & models
- **Crisis response**: be honest, take full accountability, never compound the lie, get ahead of the story and "own the narrative," and frame the apology around the core value you violated. "There's no delete button in memory, only create new memories." Reputation = consistent behavior over time.
- **Apology framework (Leila, for her EAs)**: say what you did, take responsibility, say sorry, give no excuses, then say what you'll do differently ("future pace"). At home with Alex: "I would prefer you do X next time."
- **Market-crisis playbook (COVID at Gym Launch)**: work around the clock, then the next afternoon hold a call with all clients and one with the wider community. Switch marketing to messaging, own the narrative of your response, admit you can't control everything, commit that you'll work hardest, then live up to it.
- **First-responder advantage (Sharran)**: the industry was hit by a real-estate commission lawsuit on a Friday. He posted "come to the Zoom Monday" for the whole industry with no plan, and tens of thousands came. People associate you with the first solution, and competitors later played the recording in their conference rooms.
- **Market-leader messaging**: #1 talks to the whole industry and the greater good, while #2–4 attack each other. Start talking that way before you're #1.
- **"Treat your audience like clients and eventually your audience becomes your client"** (Leila). Respect changes how you communicate, including neutral replies to rude DMs.
- **"Consistently consistent"**: consistency creates safety. A scandal hurts because the behavior contradicts who you said you were (Sharran's Tate hypothetical). "How you do one thing is how you do everything" (Adam Grant, as quoted).
- **Goodwill deposits**: they give away the scaling roadmap, which could have been a $10–30K course. Goodwill positions them as the industry leader, opens doors and makes A-player hires arrive pre-sold.
- **Losing your cool (Sharran)**: his coach asked, "What are you telling yourself about yourself?" Fix it by saying "that will never happen again" (a promise to yourself) and, whenever you feel unhinged, allow yourself only one move: ask a clarifying question.
- **Company-wide change messaging (Leila)**: gather feedback from someone at every level. Build their concerns into the solution, say there's no perfect solution and this one may change in a year. For leftover concerns, ask whether they need a different perspective (explain your trade-off, e.g. no assigned desks) or you need a different procedure (rework it).
- **Weighting feedback**: account for each person's incentives and biases. People whose skills don't fit a new initiative will say it won't work, and a direct report may oppose a hire who would sit between you. Lower the stakes with "I'm asking many people and won't decide on your feedback alone."
- **Fears, Frustrations, Wants, Aspirations (Sharran)**: run any message through these four for a person, a team or a client avatar, then address each one. You can use AI for it, e.g. "raising price from $1,000 to $1,500/mo for home buyers, where am I misaligned?"
- **Rapport, Results, Reward (Sharran on keeping people)**: rapport means creating safety ("I'm assuming this is a safe room" rather than asking permission). Results means the say/do ratio. Reward means investing in the person's own goal. "People come for the opportunity, stay for the connection."
- **Anchor feedback to their goal (Leila)**: "You told me you want X. I'm your partner in getting there. I think doing Y gets you there faster. What do you think?" If you don't know their goal: "Do you know what would make you a weapon? This one missing piece."
- **What to delegate first**: Leila goes for what you're worst at and hate that also has the lowest $/hour (her early hires were IT and finance). Sharran asks what's the last thing you'd give up (for Alex, marketing) and what's farthest from revenue, and backfills from there.
- **Calendar blocks (Sharran's sales-coach frame)**: put 10 sales-call blocks on the calendar and make the job filling them, instead of "aim for 10 calls."
- **One-on-ones**: "No memo/agenda, no meeting." Keep a running log (a Slack canvas in each DM). An end-of-week report makes one-on-ones work. Leila's template, filled out beforehand: quick wins, challenges/blockers and why, and where you want feedback. Use less structure with senior people. For someone on one mega-focus, discuss only that. The manager preps too.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- In a crisis, respond immediately. Delay lets others fill in the story.
- When recruiting, undersell by about 15% (Leila) so reality meets or beats expectations. Overselling in the job description, the interview or the website hurts retention.
- If someone won't prep for a one-on-one, cancel it (Leila).
- Don't stack all one-on-ones after other calls. Protect your energy for them (she splits them across two days).
- "Talent trumps timing": when you find exceptional talent, engage in even the smallest way, even before the role exists.
- The founder should keep the last revenue-driving role until there's enough team. Leila still took top-level sales calls at Gym Launch with >$1M/month profit and a team of 50, so revenue wouldn't dip.
- A founder should reach their "sweet spot" around years 5–7 (Leila's estimate). Replacing a unicorn skill like marketing can take ~18 months and about six all-stars.

## Playbook steps
1. Crisis: tell the truth, be first, apologize against your values, commit to what you'll do differently, then keep behaving consistently.
2. Company change: gather feedback from every level, weight it for bias, build concerns into the solution, run fears/frustrations/wants/aspirations, then for leftover concerns either explain your perspective or change the procedure.
3. Performance conversation: anchor to their goal, assume they don't know how, and teach it ("Do you want me to do the V1?").
4. Hiring without top pay: sell freedom and autonomy, co-create non-cash compensation ("If money weren't on the table, what would matter?"), and recruit all the time.
5. Interviews, from the candidate side: treat it as a client presentation. Bring the research and a plan, and "create relief."

## Mistakes he warns against (here: Leila/Sharran)
- Doubling down on a lie, or spinning PR instead of owning it.
- Following an apology with excuses.
- Assuming underperformance is effort. "The number one reason team members underperform is they don't know how" (Sharran).
- Thinking you must pay top dollar for talent. Some people took 50% pay cuts from VC-backed companies for freedom.
- Offering every benefit, which leaves you no clear value proposition.
- Asking for feedback without accounting for incentives.
- Unstructured meetings, and managers who don't prep.
- Founders who hand off their world-class revenue engine (e.g. marketing) because it frustrates them.
