## Core thesis
A business that just makes money isn't necessarily a valuable asset. Enterprise value = (number of customers × lifetime gross profit) ÷ risk. Acquisition.com's "value acceleration method" fills 10 gaps (the "Mosey Lisa" painting): leadership team running day-to-day; marketing without the founder; delivery without the founder; multiple reliable acquisition channels; reliable recurring revenue; diverse customer base; automated metric tracking; high cash flow + profitable + growing with a good story; audit-ready financials; $5M+ EBITDA. "You get paid for what you do, you get returns on what you own."

## Frameworks & models
- **Value formula**: customers × lifetime gross profit / risk = Enterprise Value. 10× customers ≈ 10× value, all else equal.
- **Operator litmus test**: if you have more time and bandwidth, they helped; if they add new tasks for you, they hurt. **Local vs global work**: a good operator uses as few systems as possible to cut work company-wide (e.g. CRM notes add sales work locally but cut it globally).
- **Title ladder**: manager → director of ops → VP ops → COO. Start lower; over-titling blocks hiring above them.
- **Leadership checkboxes**: if I leave, do we still get more customers next month? Do customers stay, keep paying, and pay more? Someone must own each.
- **Founder-face transition (ads)**: Alex only → Alex + GM/CEO (Kale) together → separately → Kale only (~12 months). Brand = associations; transfer them.
- **Delivery "Hydra"**: replace yourself with several subject matter experts (sales, retention, marketing), edified to customers in their area; give senior ones small equity (0.1–0.5%) so non-competes hold.
- **Channel growth order**: 1 offer / 1 avatar / 1 channel until ~$1M/yr → more & better on the working channel (lowest-risk growth) → then a second channel, started by someone else.
- **9 C's of recurring revenue (stickiness)**: Consumption, Collateral (storage unit, payment processor data), Cost of switching (make it better, not harder to leave), Choice (patents/trade secrets), Control the money flow (Uber, DoorDash, payment processing), Cause, Community, Contracts/commitments, Communication (regular events give people something to look forward to).
- **Stair-step vs compounding**: one-off sales plateau; subscriptions with churn at month 4 step up then plateau; net negative churn (Salesforce-style seat/usage expansion) compounds. Consumer subscriptions lean on not cancelling (Netflix); B2B should build in expansion revenue.
- **Theory of constraints on funnel metrics**: add the same +5% to each stage and see which moves output most. It's usually the lowest number; attack in ascending order.
- **Metric-tracking setup**: platform → which data → one owner → game plan (constraint).
- **Accounting levels**: have financials (outsourced bookkeeper) → accountant + GAAP (accrual, e.g. $30K 3-month contract recognized $10K/mo) → audit-ready / quality of earnings.
- **Multiples drivers**: reliability of future revenue, expansion opportunities (Amazon vs Netflix), defensibility/moat.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Leadership test (Gym Launch): if you step away, the company grows for 6 straight months without you jumping back in. When they bring you problems, ask "what do you think I'll tell you?"
- A players cost ~25% more but produce ~5× more than B players. Expect them to negotiate pay; that's fine.
- Hire for a purpose: give candidates real stats plus the problem and hear how they'd attack it. Track record and relevant experience beat "would we be friends."
- Your 1× leadership team almost never survives to 10× size; prioritize the business and have the hard conversations.
- No customer above 20% of revenue; he prefers ≤5%. One whale at 20–30% of revenue can be 100% of profit.
- Whale decisions: (1) take it on at all? (2) If so, go up-market and get more whales, or stay with minnows? Off-avatar whales are distractions.
- New channel: don't kill your main channel to start one; be patient; track progress through the funnel (clicks → opt-ins → booked → shows → right people → buys), not outcome. Copy where same-size peers in your industry acquire customers profitably.
- Small businesses (<$10M revenue) typically trade at ~1–4× earnings; >$10M revenue / >$2M profit can move higher.
- Institutional investors mostly want $5M+ EBITDA; usually that implies a real management team.
- Even with high return on capital, he recommends founders take a fixed monthly amount out to de-risk.
- Know gross profit and gross margin per product and each product's share of sales; reorganize selling around the highest-profit items.
- You can judge skill by the number and quality of metrics someone tracks. Be as detailed about product/delivery metrics as acquisition metrics (low chargebacks ≠ good product).

## Playbook steps
1. Install a seasoned operator who reduces your load; title conservatively.
2. Fill leadership checkboxes for acquisition and retention/expansion; run the 6-month step-away test.
3. Transfer ads off your face over ~12 months via joint ads.
4. Split delivery across edified subject matter experts; small equity for key ones.
5. Max one channel with more/better, then have a new hire build a second channel from scratch.
6. Build recurring revenue with the 9 C's; aim for net negative churn.
7. Cap customer concentration (≤20%, ideally ≤5%).
8. Replace Google Sheets with an integrated CRM/dashboards; one owner; attack the lowest funnel metric first.
9. Keep cash flow high, profitable, growing; tie the story to a trend (e.g. AI as tailwind or as cost cutter); re-rank product mix by gross profit/margin.
10. Move to GAAP, then audit-ready financials / quality of earnings.
11. Grow to $5M+ EBITDA.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Thinking an operator is someone who manages systems; an operator leads, and people should look up to them.
- Putting an inexperienced friend in as operator.
- Over-titling early employees.
- Hiring people you'd be friends with / people just like you.
- Refusing to give equity (founders average ~12% at IPO; grow the pie).
- Founder as face of ads/delivery if you want to sell; selling time with yourself.
- Single-channel dependence (platform bans, domain deliverability).
- Building the business around one whale.
- Running on guesswork / manual sheets.
- Reinvesting 100% of profit and never taking cash out (his gyms).
- Trusting founder-friendly books; overestimating your business's value because of years of effort.

## Examples / case details
- Gym Launch: $0 → $4.4M/month in 20 months; licensed 5,000+ locations; "it also sucked"; his wife suggested shutting it down; an investment banker said it was worth millions but not yet.
- Portfolio contrast: inexperienced operator → plateau, rising costs, broke even for 9 months; seasoned operator (took a chain from 100 → 2,000 locations over 10+ years) → profits expanded much faster, spent a quarter just raising location profitability.
- A portfolio CEO almost didn't hire a candidate who negotiated; she became his all-time best hire.
- First 400 days of Gym Launch: Alex did a 90-minute Q&A daily.
- Gym Launch had only IG + FB paid ads, which underperformed in COVID. He hired a rep from a competitor (~$10M/month, all outbound, 30+ callers) to build outbound from scratch → ~50% of revenue within 12 months; the acquirer liked the half inbound / half outbound split.
- New-channel math: $10K/month for 6 months = $60K before the first $1K sale; once tuned, spend $30K → $300K (he calls that 5× on the initial investment: $300K / $60K).
- Gym events (e.g. themed boot camps) lowered churn leading up to them; churn rose after.
- Live demo: director of brand quoted real-time book-launch registrations (~292,000) and affiliates (10,813).
- Portfolio before/after: show rate 50% → 70% from a 22-item checklist (40% relative lift); close rate improved by ~14 points from 27% (~50% relative). "No silver bullets, many golden BBs."
- Multiples cited: Netflix 44×, Microsoft 29×, Amazon 310× earnings (thin margins by design); S&P 500 historically 12–18×, now 23× ($100M EBITDA → $2.3B).
- Sold 6 gyms for ~$250–300K total (fire sale) after reinvesting all profit.
- Teeth-whitening chain, 32 locations: grew ~30% during a ~4-month deal process; cheap build-outs pay back fast, so they can compound several times a year per location (transcript partly garbled here).
- Brick and mortar example: $100K to open → $500K/year profit.
- Portfolio company went from a couple million/year to a couple million/month in 3 years, then the CEO didn't know where profit went → missing finance function.
- Portfolio averages: 12 months → revenue 1.8×, profit 3.01× ($2M → he says $6.03M; 3.01× = $6.02M); 24 months → revenue 2.8×, profit 4.7× ($2M → $9.4M EBITDA). He values it at "9" × 7 = $63M vs ~$6M entry [note: 9.4 × 7 ≈ $65.8M; he rounded to 9]. Targets companies at $1–2M+ profit, typically $2–6M.
