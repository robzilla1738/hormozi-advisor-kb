## Core thesis
Simplicity, from strategy through marketing, product and delivery, is the most profitable discipline. It takes more work than complexity, because complexity is "the child of lazy thinking." Find the one thing that, if true 12 months from now, would change everything. Point every department at it, let small fires burn, and perfect fewer details. Drawn largely from Ken Segall's *Insanely Simple* (Steve Jobs) and Frank Slootman's book (caption says "Snowball"; *Amp It Up* [inference]).

## Frameworks & models
- **Crumpled-paper catch**: throw four balls and people catch none; throw one and they catch it. "Find the most compelling point and communicate it in the most compelling way" ("1,000 songs in your pocket").
- **The one thing**: "What one thing, if it were true 12 months from now, would change everything?" Examples: an owned lead channel; a talent supply for an HVAC company; cash collection cut from 90 to 30 days. Achieving #1 often makes #2–5 irrelevant or changes them.
- **Sit with the pain of an unbuilt bridge**: don't start a second solution 30 days in. Parallel fixes pull resources from the one that will work. Prefer one sure high-leverage bet over many small bets ("death by a thousand paper cuts").
- **Jade vs canvas (subtraction)**: remove the excess to reveal the product ("delete everything that sucks about a phone; what remains is an iPhone"). Related to Munger's invert: how much can I remove and still have it be good, or better?
- **Perfect every detail, limit the number of details** (Jack Dorsey). "Focus is the number of amazing ideas you say no to" (Jobs).
- **Outsized returns at the tail**: 90th → 95th → 99th percentile is where you capture the market.
- **Sausage-making vs order-of-magnitude bets**: close rate, margin and lead tweaks are daily operations. Spare team capacity (the 20–30% above 70–80% utilization) funds one real bet.
- **Accelerating projects** (Elon, Brian Chesky, YC founders): big public goal, aggressive timeline, frequent check-ins, remove people who shouldn't be on it. "A plan and not enough time." Many projects can be done in 12 weeks.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you can't name the one most important thing, the CEO has failed, so think longer (from Slootman; Alex applies it to himself).
- Introduce a feature or step only when the customer needs it to get the desired value.
- One focus means fewer meetings and a small group of very smart people.
- "The easiest way to screw up a project like this is to give it too much time."

## Playbook steps
1. Ask the 12-month one-thing question. 2. Align all departments to it. 3. Kick off the meaty solution and don't add parallel solutions when impatient. 4. Subtract: list what you can remove from offer, product and onboarding. 5. Perfect the remaining few details. 6. Run it as a 12-week, aggressive, public, frequently checked project with a minimal team. 7. Market the single most compelling point.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Listing four benefits in marketing.
- Agencies tacking on services instead of perfecting the one result (enterprise clients serving the Fortune 100 are an exception: "full integration" is their one thing).
- Changing priorities constantly: "your team's 10 times more overwhelmed."
- Alex admits he gets impatient and tries a second solution after 30 days.

## Examples / case details
- Raising Cane's: about seven menu items (toast, fries, tenders, sauce, beverages, coleslaw...).
- Skool Games: the top cancel reason was overwhelm. They cut from three calls a week to one, moved the big download from monthly to quarterly, and turned off some native app features. Members need "more value per second in fewer total seconds."
- Gym Launch: gym owners on a product call cut them off: "we just need leads."
- One-sandwich shop: obsess over ordering, waiting, sourcing, temperature and order of assembly.
