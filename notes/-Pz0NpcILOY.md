## Core thesis
Uses Munger-style inversion: to earn as little as possible (as employee or business owner) you'd provide little value, ask for a small share of it, and be easily replaceable. Flip each: create more value, ask for more (negotiate/price higher), and become a "category of one." Bonus fourth lever: who you serve / the opportunity vehicle you work inside caps what you can earn. Employees and businesses are the same game — "Business serves the customer. Employee serves the business."

## Frameworks & models
- **Pay = Value × % you ask for × scarcity (+ who you serve)**: (1) value created, (2) negotiation / share captured, (3) supply-demand / replaceability, (4) bonus: who you serve and the vehicle you're in. [inference: framed as a multiplicative structure; he lists them as variables]
- **Value equation applied to employees**: dream outcome, perceived likelihood, speed, effort/sacrifice — an employee who needs constant checking and instructions is less valuable than one who brings ideas and executes quickly.
- **Car story**: car dealer $5K, scrapyard $1K, antique dealer $200K — "the value that someone will give you is ... who you are talking to." A billionaire pays more to lose weight than someone on unemployment because of indirect value.
- **Category of one**: niche down to shrink the pool you compete against; add bonuses, guarantees, features to make a one-of-a-kind offer; broaden later using depth of expertise.
- **Variable compensation**: highest earners (owners, top CEOs) are paid variably; enterprise value itself is variable comp. Employees can propose a % tied to revenue they drive; B2B services can ask for a % of results.
- **Learn or earn** (Buffett, via Alex): work should pay in learning, earning, ideally both; if neither, stop.
- **Vision big enough** (Schwarzman, "What It Takes"): the business's vision should be big enough for others' visions to fit inside; otherwise you won't attract 10/10 talent.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Don't set prices from your own wallet; what's easy for you isn't easy for others, so your perception of value is skewed — let the market set price.
- The more directly you can tie your work to revenue, the less discount is applied to your value → the more you get paid.
- "He who has the most options wins": line up other customers/job offers before negotiating.
- Your earning ceiling is a subset of the business's opportunity (hard to make $1M/yr at a dry cleaner).
- If you're 10/10 talent, seek bigger visions/companies.

## Playbook steps
1. Audit yourself (or business) on value, ask, and replaceability (plus who you serve).
2. Raise value using the value equation; serve customers for whom the problem is more valuable.
3. Ask for more: raise prices / propose variable comp tied to revenue you drive ("would it be unreasonable...").
4. Niche down and stack bonuses/guarantees into a category-of-one offer.
5. Build options (other customers/offers) before any negotiation.
6. Owners: build a vision/comp structure where employees can get wealthy.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Selling from your own wallet / underpricing what feels easy to you.
- Being commoditized with nothing differentiating you — customers pick the cheaper one.
- Staying in a job where you're neither learning nor earning.
- Working inside an opportunity vehicle too small for your goals.
