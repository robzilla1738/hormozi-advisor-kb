# Why you should quit college.
id: 5RiR6cBLkFg / url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5RiR6cBLkFg / duration: 837s (~14 min) / tags: 12-mindset-leverage, 01-what-business-to-start, 13-quit-or-pivot, 05-sales-process
Signal: medium

## Core thesis
Don't go to college because it's "expected." Treat it as a big, irreversible decision and judge it by opportunity cost. Ask: "Four years from now, given the cost, which path gives me the most potential toward my goal?" If the goal is income, learn income-generating skills that map to business departments, starting with sales. The video tells the story of his 18-year-old neighbor Jacob, who appears on camera. Jacob dropped out of Pepperdine after one semester and got into sales.

## Frameworks & models
- **Reversibility × long-term impact**: lunch choice is trivial. College means 4 years, $50–200K, and lost income. Give big irreversible decisions matching respect.
- **Opportunity cost**: "The loss of potential gain from other alternatives." College costs tuition plus forgone earnings plus the business you could have built. He calls it "the single greatest cost people do not take into consideration."
- **"What problem are you solving?"** If it's making money, will a degree make you better at it than the alternative you? If it's socializing, there are cheaper ways: work all day, go out every night, end up debt-free.
- **Majors as business departments**: marketing (making and distributing materials), sales, delivery (physical product, customer success, or technical trades), and support (finance, HR, legal).
- **Map interests to departments**: like cameras → content/commercials. Like writing → selling, delivering, or legal. Aggressive with people → sales. More passive → HR or recruiting.
- **Branch-pivot check**: "Have I learned everything I'm going to learn from this branch?" If yes, pivot.
- **Single-statement hack**: chew on a big decision until it reduces to one sentence.
- **Bolt-on skill**: go deep technically, then add dealing with, managing and leading people. That's how you get promoted. You only need to know the technical skill well enough to teach it.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Degrees are required for doctors, lawyers and similar fields. For the "99%" outside those ~six careers, they aren't. He says Google and big tech dropped degree requirements.
- "If you're gonna do it eventually, you might as well do it now." He started at 23 and wishes he'd started at 18.
- You won't know what you like until you start. Context comes from doing.
- You start out sucking at sales and get good after thousands of reps.
- Live cheap (Jacob lived with four guys) and save to build capital plus skills.

## Playbook steps
1. Name the problem you're solving. 2. Compare the 4-year paths, including opportunity cost. 3. Pick a department skill (default: sales). 4. Get a job, do reps, save aggressively. 5. To start a business: cold calling is your first acquisition channel and sales is your conversion. Learn to teach the skill (delivery), then finance and contracts ("Googleable in a couple days"). 6. Bolt on people and leadership skills.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Going because it's the "next natural step" or from fear of disappointing parents. He stayed at Vanderbilt (graduated a year early) because he was afraid of disappointing his father.
- Believing general education adds value to the marketplace.
- Using school as a social safety net or excuse for failing at entrepreneurship. Jacob called this the line that hit hardest.
