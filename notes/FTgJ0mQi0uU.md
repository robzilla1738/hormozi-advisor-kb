## Core thesis
Learning the wrong lesson from a failure *or a success* is as dangerous as the failure, because experience then backs up a false belief. Alex thinks his early inbound success (paid ads, money "tomorrow") taught him that success should come big and fast. His lasting wins (fitness, marriage) came from slow, sustainable, low-volatility habits. So he now builds for sustained growth and for businesses that grow without him. A short reflective vlog, with no tactical frameworks.

## Frameworks & models
- **Wrong-lesson trap**: one bad hire → "employees all suck" is like swearing off dating after one heartbreak. The story you tell yourself then reinforces the wrong lesson.
- **Finite → infinite horizon test**: "Can I do this until I die?" Do only what's worthwhile on an endless horizon. This lowers the urge to hurry.
- **Time between thought and action**: early on, shrink it (you're paralyzed). Once you're good at acting, you break things, so widen it so decisions are rational, not emotional.
- **Reverse attribution exercise**: assume your successes came from outside forces and your failures were your fault. Ask, "What if this wasn't because of me?" to find new patterns.
- **Extrapolate steady growth**: if the current growth rate holds for 10 years, it reaches $100M–$200M+ (his business). Steady quarterly growth (he says "10 every quarter", [inference] ~10%) compounds hugely.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- When a competitor runs an ad or an employee leaves with clients, "most times no reaction is the best decision."
- Prefer reliability and low volatility over the big win. He says the richest people look for low volatility.
- Build things that don't need you or your skill. The goal is a business that grows without you, with you only guiding direction and acting as a sounding board for leadership.

## Playbook steps
1. After any failure or success, ask whether the lesson you took is the right one, not just a generalization from one case.
2. Run the reverse-attribution exercise on your wins and losses.
3. Check your current growth rate; extrapolate it over 5–10 years; aim to sustain it rather than chase spikes.
4. Add a pause before reacting to competitive or staffing shocks.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Generalizing from one bad outcome ("employees aren't for me").
- Taking credit for wins and blaming outside circumstances for losses.
- Over-learning from early fast success, so you expect everything to be big and fast.
- Over-acting once you're good at action, which breaks things.

## Examples / case details
- Healthy for ~20 years by choosing sustainable fitness/eating.
- Married Leila rationally on shared mission and values "before we were really in love"; it has gotten better every year (~5 years married at recording).
- Inbound/paid ads drove many of his successes but skewed his thinking short-term.
