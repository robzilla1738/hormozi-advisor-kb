## Core thesis
Seventeen pieces of advice from mentors and peers that changed his trajectory. The common thread: decide and commit, do far more volume of what already works, protect compounding by not switching, build leverage through people, product and brand, and use simple rules (learning budget, capital-allocation circle) so decisions get faster.

## Frameworks & models
- **Decision = "cut off"**: not deciding is itself choosing to kill the future that holds your dreams. Pick which future to kill.
- **Secret to sales**: "making an offer so good people feel stupid saying no." Start with what gets the most people to say yes and work backwards (Chick-fil-A doesn't need to sell its sandwiches).
- **Go slow to go fast**: consistency compounds; constant change resets you to ground zero. Every change has a guaranteed cost, and it isn't guaranteed to work.
- **Test size / volume**: his 300 flyers told him nothing. The mentor tested with 5,000, then did 5,000 a day (~150,000 a month). Never let too little volume or too short a run be why something failed.
- **"Level 10 skill set, level 2 opportunity"**: a low-leverage vehicle with poor capital compounding. Succeeding in a hard opportunity is training with extra resistance.
- **Fat pitches**: you get maybe 2–3 in a lifetime. When it gets easy, go harder, and trade some short term for long term.
- **Do 10× more of what works**: once you have product-market fit, put innovation into volume, not new businesses. He delayed the supplement business 18 months and tripled ad spend: 400K → 780K → 1M → 2M/month over ~6 months.
- **Learning budget**: put 10–20% of marketing spend toward new ideas and expect to lose it. The mentor said about 4 of 5 fail and 1 of 5 crushes. This lengthens the return horizon.
- **"If you don't agree, don't move forward"** (pastor's advice to him and Leila). First question: "what information are you using?"
- **Bigger TAM**: the riches are in the niches, but at some point open the aperture (Facebook started with college).
- **Stable of stallions / give a slice of the pie**: 10 people with 20 years each in their departments = 200 years of experience. One brain is the organization's limit. This took him past $30–40M toward a $500M top line, doing less.
- **Product leverage** (citing Naval): exceptional product = quadratic word of mouth (1→2→4→8); marketing and sales = linear. Don't make new products until the first one is right.
- **Brand, quantified (attributed to Buffett)**: the price premium over the commoditized version ($20 vs $100 t-shirt) is brand. That pricing power goes to the bottom line and compounds.
- **Capital-allocation circle**: allocate net worth by the share of your brain that knows each asset class (e.g. 85% real estate / 15% stocks). He invests only in businesses.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you've already done the research, stop gathering information and decide.
- Don't judge a channel on a tiny test. Flyers: 5,000 minimum, then daily volume.
- When something is working (e.g. doubling monthly), scale spend before starting a new venture.
- Learning budget: 10–20% of marketing spend, expected to be lost.
- Partners who disagree slow down. Almost nothing is truly urgent.
- Have investing rules in advance to avoid decision fatigue and diligence waste. "Not in my wheel" = an instant no.
- No new product until the current one is exceptional.

## Playbook steps
[inference] 1. Make the decision and kill the waffling. 2. Design an offer so good no one says no. 3. Pick a channel and run it at real volume for long enough. 4. When it works, pour 3–10× more into it rather than diversifying. 5. Set aside 10–20% for experiments. 6. Hire senior experts and give them equity or upside. 7. Make the product exceptional so word of mouth compounds. 8. Build brand for pricing power. 9. Invest only within your circle of knowledge.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Waffling for months after the research is done.
- Relying on manipulation and scripts instead of a better offer.
- Erratic switching that kills compounding.
- Under-volume tests (300 flyers; posting once vs a peer's 5–7 times).
- Easing off when things finally get easy.
- Chasing a second win instead of 10× on the first.
- False urgency.
- Hoarding equity and decisions.
- He calls his earlier dismissal of fame and audience-building (preferring only cold outreach and ads) a mistake. Inbound attracts talent and money, and the pros outweigh the cons.
- Diligencing every deal that comes in without rules.
