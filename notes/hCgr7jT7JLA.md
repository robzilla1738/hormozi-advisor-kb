# Answering Juicy Questions About Our Relationship

Speakers: Alex AND Leila Hormozi (couples end-of-year Q&A); many answers are Leila's. Mostly personal/relationship content. Business relevance is limited to partnership, focus, and work–life tradeoffs.

## Core thesis
For ambitious founder couples, you can't have two goals that conflict. Pick the one that matters more, set expectations that match the "season" you're in, and build the partnership on respect rather than feelings. Do effective work and stop when your output drops, instead of grinding hours.

## Frameworks & models
- **Output-drop rule (Alex)**: know your rate of progress (paragraphs edited, pages, slides per unit of time). When it drops a lot, stop, because the quality drops too and you'll have to fix it later. Spend that time on a higher-ROI activity (e.g., the first hour of marriage time has a very high ROI).
- **Minimum effective dose per goal**: what's the minimum each goal needs? Usually you can still lean heavily on work goals.
- **Seasons**: partners alternate intense work seasons. Expectations should change to fit the season.
- **Price tag of what you want (Leila)**: an ambitious spouse costs you flexibility. Don't hold two desires that conflict.
- **Low expectations, high commitment**: they say the most successful marriages have this, and it stops small irritations from turning into catastrophes.
- **Respect over love as the bedrock**: feelings come and go; respect for someone's character lasts.
- **Agreements, not "non-negotiables"**: shared commitments that change only if both people agree.
- **Conflict protocol**: change rooms, then text/write it out to remove emotion; come back later, and often just let it go.
- **First six hours on the one highest-leverage thing (Alex)**: think a long time about which one thing to work on, then give it the first ~6 hours of every day. He says he has done this for a decade-plus.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If your output rate drops noticeably, stop working and switch to something with higher ROI.
- If you feel unhappy as a "husband," either change what you expect of yourself or accept the season as the new normal.
- When you disagree with a partner, persuade them ("show your work"). Giving up quietly hands them full responsibility for the outcome.
- Partnerships: "you can count on one hand the amount of good partners you have in your life." Bad partnerships are a common reason businesses underperform, which they see in acquisition.com applications.

## Playbook steps
[inference] 1. Measure your output rate on your main work. 2. Stop when it falls; move to relationships or rest. 3. Give the first 6 hours of each day to the one highest-leverage task. 4. Write down shared agreements with your partner. 5. In conflict: separate, write, come back later.

## Mistakes he warns against
- "Productively procrastinating": sitting at the computer pretending to be productive.
- Giving in during a disagreement out of frustration (Leila's self-critique on the software business decision).
- Stating a conclusion without showing the reasoning (Leila's earlier habit; Alex needed to be "sold").
- Catastrophizing small issues, or the opposite: never confronting anything.
- Putting the business before the marriage. Alex did this for the first 3 years; both were miserable, so they flipped it, and he says the marriage "3x'd."

## Examples / case details
- Their one big fight was about the direction of the software business. They locked themselves in a room for ~2 days.
- Alex's low point: within ~60 days he had a head-on collision/DUI, his mother in the hospital, two business partner splits, and lost all his money. Leila still followed through on a gym launch despite their 30-day breakup.
- Alex said he was a "hard sell" on operations, culture, and people. Leila's persistence and evidence over years changed his view.
- Munger and Buffett are their example of a partnership that just keeps working.
- Esther Perel (Mating in Captivity): "create space to be missed."
