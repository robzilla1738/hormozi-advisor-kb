## Core thesis
You can multiply revenue without changing what you deliver, only how you sell and package it. The "diagnostic sale" moves a transactional, one-off sale toward a custom-feeling one: the customer states where they are and where they want to be, you present your product as the vehicle between the two, and you tie the price to that goal. In a 14-location brick-and-mortar chain he bought into (now 32 locations), this took average revenue per customer from ~$200 to ~$800 and grew the product line's recurring revenue more than 10×.

## Frameworks & models
- **Diagnostic sales steps:** (1) pre-sale questionnaire; (2) collect info + credit card ("complete your customer profile") before selling; (3) current state; (4) desired state; (5) obstacle; (6) present the desired state + your vehicle, price tied to the goal; (7) incentive to prepay; (8) transition to recurring.
- **How he finds the opportunity (5 steps):** secret shop → gather information / find the constraint (here offer/packaging) → pick the fix → "kill zombies" (handle objections before the price is shown) → implement (ongoing).
- **Sales spectrum:** transactional (fast, low-ticket, fit the customer to one product) ↔ custom ↔ enterprise/relational. The magic is a single standardized product sold in a way that *feels* custom.
- **Pre-sale questionnaire:** asks about the problem in several ways (what brought you in, how long, what has it cost you, how much worse in 5 years) to raise awareness and importance of the pain.
- **Customer self-selects before/after** (filler-density chart, scale, "yogurt-store" self-serve): when they pick the goal, "the price came from them." Use a third party to deliver bad news ("the scale called you fat, not me").
- **Customers are fractal (80/20):** the top 20% have ~5× the spending power, so price an upsell ~5× the core offer. If 20% take a $5K upsell on a $1K core, average ticket doubles to ~$2K. A $20 upsell on a $200 customer is not worth the effort.
- **Price presentation / anchor:** present the full payment-plan price first, then discounts: e.g. $4,500 full; 20% off prepay = $3,600; 10% off half-down = $4,050. You are the good guy giving a discount, not the bad guy adding interest.
- **Sales step-downs:** prepay → third-party financing → partial payment with smaller discount → payment plan/continuity (match payments to delivery) → at minimum, book the next visit. Mantra: "everyone buys something."
- **Payment plan → recurring:** people churn less out of a payment plan than a monthly membership; add automatic recurring at the end of the plan at the same price.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you can 4× a business without opening new locations, do that first.
- Upsell price ≈ 5× the current price.
- Prepay discount of 10–20%; offer the highest-discount option first and step down.
- Say "prepayment discount," never "paid in full" (a salesman term, not a customer benefit).
- Handle objections *before* presenting price; after the price, you look like a salesperson.
- "If you win the argument in a sale, you lose the sale." Ask with childlike curiosity (head tilt) rather than confronting.
- For low-skill sales teams, make the process simple and force adherence by building checkboxes into the point of sale; drill the script until they say it without thinking.

## Playbook steps
1. Secret shop your own team. 2. Act as your own consultant: find the constraint. 3. Write the new sales process (his took 4 hours with the sales director). 4. Questionnaire → card on file → current/desired/obstacle. 5. Explain the path in the customer's own words, price it to the goal (with a guarantee if possible). 6. Anchor at the full price, offer the prepay discount, step down. 7. Make payment plans auto-recur. 8. Roll out across all locations.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Selling one-off transactions with "call us when you want it again."
- Assuming your team follows your script ("if they remember half of it you'll be stoked").
- Low-priced upsells (~10% of ticket) that barely move average value.
- Presenting the discounted price first and then adding interest for payment plans (the bank approach).
- Telling the prospect they are wrong or arguing with them.
