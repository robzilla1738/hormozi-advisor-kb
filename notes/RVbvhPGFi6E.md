## Core thesis
Reframing is the 1–3 sentences you say after a prospect says anything but "yes." It raises the odds that your next line moves them toward buying. The best closers ask for the sale the most times, and reframing lets you keep asking without losing rapport. His method is the **3A framework**: Acknowledge → Associate → Ask. He gives five rules for using it ethically. Context: he has 100+ salespeople across a portfolio doing $250M+/yr. He says the method has sold offers from $100 to $1M+ in person, by phone, on Zoom and from stage.

## Frameworks & models
- **3A**:
  1. *Acknowledge*: restate what they said. This builds rapport, shows you're listening, and buys you time to think.
  2. *Associate*: tie their question to how your best customers behave. "Great question, a lot of our best customers ask that." A step away from buying becomes a sign they're more likely to buy.
  3. *Ask*: ask your next question, often a question about their question ("which certifications specifically?").
- **Whoever asks the questions is in control.** Once they're asking, you're on defense.
- **Ask only questions you know the answers to**, like a lawyer. Answer only when you know you have the right answer.
- **Three straw-man associations**, for delivering hard truths without insulting the prospect:
  - (a) "Someone asked me the same thing earlier today. Can I share what I told them?"
  - (b) "That makes you just like Sarah, who had a similar plumbing business and crushed it."
  - (c) Appeal to an edified authority: "Alex just told me this earlier today, can I share it?"
- **Labels to live up to**: an association ("smart question," "family man") gives the prospect a label you can bring back at the close.
- **Time-traveler frame**: imagine convincing your past self to buy Nvidia, Apple or Bitcoin while they don't know who you are. Have total empathy and focus on the prospect, not the product.
- **Angry-boat parallel** (customer service): never invalidate someone's upset. Validate it.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- **Rule 1**: a prospect believes almost nothing you say and almost everything they say. Ask questions so *they* conclude they're a fit.
- **Rule 2**: "Be like smoke." No one can disagree with a question. Never voice disagreement: "you never win a sale by winning an argument."
- **Rule 3**: tell them what their question means. This is the association step.
- **Rule 4**: use straw men for tough truths. A 22-year-old saying "let me put my coach hat on" won't land with a 45-year-old $10M plumber.
- **Rule 5**: keep childlike curiosity. Say "huh, that's so interesting…", which also buys 2–3 seconds. Sticky note: "keep the human number one." Seek to understand, not win.
- First objection: "What's your main concern?" / "What variables are you using?" Later in the sale: "What are you most afraid of having happen?" / "What would make this a no?" / "What would it take for you to say yes?"
- Hard questions like "what are you afraid of" only work if you have built rapport.
- **Ethics**: bad selling means selling an unqualified prospect. "State the facts and tell the truth." If they're qualified, you're obliged to keep asking.
- Recruiting aside: he wants salespeople with 2+ years of experience.

## Playbook steps
1. When you hear a non-yes, acknowledge by restating it.
2. Associate it positively with your best customers' behavior.
3. Ask a question about their question, or a next-step question.
4. If you must answer, deliver it through a straw man or authority.
5. Ask for the sale again, and repeat.
6. At the close, bring back the labels they earned earlier.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Firing back an objection-handler straight away. It sounds combative.
- "Do you have any questions?" He calls it "the worst question ever asked in sales": it hands over control and invites objections.
- Answering vague questions directly. The prospect becomes "judge, jury and executioner," especially when sellers know less than the people delivering.
- Treating statements like "I hate this feature" as deal-enders.
- Getting caught off guard. "You've had a thousand of these conversations." Practice.
- Being uncomfortable with resistance or conflict.

## Examples / case details
- "How many certifications do your trainers have?" → "Which certifications specifically?" → "Why those?" → "Great ones. Here's why we chose ours."
- "I need to think about it" → "What are the main things you're considering?"
- "Timing's off" → "Smart that you're already thinking about implementation; our biggest success stories think the same way. What would make it a good time?"
- "Talk to my spouse" → "What parts do you think they'd disagree with?"
- If they ask why you aren't answering: "It's like asking the secretary about your heart surgery," or the mechanic version, "it'd be unethical to answer without looking under the hood. Anyone who answers on the first call without an assessment, run the other way."
- A wealth advisor used it on him: "90% of clients who shift over already have a wealth advisor." He didn't buy, but adopted the technique.
- He took over a sales-floor call and asked, "Dude, what are you afraid of?"
