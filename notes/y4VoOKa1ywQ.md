## Core thesis
Every business is arbitrage: buy in one market (labor, materials, attention) and sell in another, and profit equals the size of the gap. Judge opportunities by **economic** profit (after opportunity cost), not just accounting profit. Ask which arbitrage, given your resources, skills and team, is biggest. Price should be set by market demand, never by your cost.

## Frameworks & models
- **Arbitrage**: exploiting a market inefficiency (buy on eBay for $5, sell on Amazon for $50). A service business buys labor in market 1 and sells it in market 2. A supply chain is a series of markets, each player arbitraging.
- **Accounting profit vs economic profit**: accounting = revenue − all costs. Economic = revenue − explicit costs − opportunity cost of using your resources here rather than elsewhere. Example: a paper mill that owns a forest should ask whether selling timber, wood or sawdust would make more.
- **Opportunity board**: list all the businesses you could run (gym, massage parlor, supplements…) and pick the best fit for your skills, talent, team and equipment. That answers "what can we be best in the world at?"
- **Growing markets** (e.g., online fitness) tend to have bigger arbitrage because of rising demand.
- **Information advantage**: the seller usually has it. Big businesses run huge arbitrage (iPhone's ~$17 manufacturing cost, Facebook selling eyeballs, Verizon's near-zero marginal user cost).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Choose opportunity B over A when the same resources buy cheaper or sell higher elsewhere.
- Pricing has "no limit"; it should "never be in any way related to what it costs you and only related to what the market will allow."
- Prefer markets with growing demand.

## Playbook steps
1. List your resources (people, skills, equipment, software). 2. List the arbitrage opportunities they could pursue. 3. Compare economic profit, including the opportunity cost of your current path. 4. Favor growing markets with the widest buy/sell gap that fits your skills. 5. Price to the market, not to cost.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Moral hang-ups about charging far above cost; he had them early in his career.
- Only counting accounting profit and ignoring better uses of the same resources.
- Being embarrassed to ask what a term means.

## Examples / case details
