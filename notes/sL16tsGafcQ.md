## Core thesis
Poor people stay poor chasing a fast way to get rich. The richest people pick one of four paths and play it for a decade. The paths form a 2×2 of whose money and whose business:
- **Your money + your business**: bootstrapping.
- **Other people's money (OPM) + your business**: raising capital.
- **Your money + other people's businesses (OPB)**: investing.
- **OPM + OPB**: fund management.

Each has its own trade-offs and fits a different stage. Hormozi says he has done all four, in roughly that order. "No president, no economy is going to make you rich."

## Frameworks & models
- **The four paths**, with Forbes top-11 proof points:
  - Raised capital: Musk, Ellison, Zuckerberg, Bezos, Page, Brin, Jensen Huang.
  - Bootstrapped: Ballmer/Microsoft, Michael Dell, the Waltons.
  - Investing: Buffett.
  - Fund managers appear about six names deeper.
- **Bootstrapping**:
  - You fund from savings and cash flow and reinvest profits.
  - Fits low-cost models: services, agencies, home services, B2B and professional services, education, e-com, local businesses, and now software too.
  - It is the slowest path, "a car factory built inside of the car."
  - Pros: you keep control and equity, set the pace, and exit when you want or never. You also survive longer on a low cost basis.
  - Cons: you take on "every other type of debt": management/leadership, technical and data debt. You can't afford star talent, and capital caps which opportunities you can chase (e.g. global AI robotics).
  - Goal: build a compounding vehicle with recurring or reoccurring revenue.
- **Raising capital**:
  - Fits big upfront costs with expanding margins later, or winner-take-all and network effects. Examples: tech platforms, marketplaces, manufacturing, pharma, Amazon and Facebook losing money for years.
  - Pros: top talent, outspending competitors, negative acquisition cost, fewer well-funded competitors ("count them on two hands"), less personal debt.
  - Cons: two customers (end users and investors), dilution, liquidation preferences and ratchets, board seats so you can be voted out (Steve Jobs). VC is "grand slam money": investors can absorb losses, but for the founder n=1, a "sea of tombstones."
  - Your ability to raise depends on founder track record, the size of the opportunity, and how likely investors think you are to hit it.
- **Investing**:
  - Diversification costs upside. Carnegie: "put all your eggs in one basket and watch the basket." The best investors are concentrated, with about 5–8 significant bets.
  - It's the most relaxed lifestyle (his family-office period was "the most chill") but by far the slowest. "Almost no one makes their money this way": they had a high active income first.
  - Buffett bought his first stock at about 7, was barely known before 60, and made most of his wealth from about 80 to 95. Real estate is the #1 Main Street millionaire maker but not the billionaire maker. It's a way to build and *store* wealth.
- **Fund management**: GP puts in about 5% and raises LP capital, uses debt, and takes a split after a hurdle (6–8% typical in PE) plus fees. It offers the most leverage with the smallest personal check, and the GP often ends up richer than any one LP.
- **"There is no lack of capital in the world, only a lack of good deals."** (from a mentor) If you have a good deal, capital appears.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- First business: bootstrap, to pay off "ignorance debt" before risking friends' and family's money.
- Raise capital only when there's no way to make the thing profitable without OPM, i.e. years of planned losses.
- Invest once you have meaningful excess cash and want upside without operating. The goal is passive income above your costs.
- Fund management: build a track record, proprietary deal flow and a real edge in picking and improving companies. Funds usually organize around one thesis.
- In a pitch, never say "guaranteed." Say "high likelihood of 5×, and here are the 6 ways I've mitigated risk."
- More niche and private deals with higher returns earn better GP/LP splits. First-time funds should charge lower fees for alignment.
- Don't copy outliers' paths (Buffett, Mozart) if you're not them.

## Playbook steps
1. Bootstrap a first business and learn.
2. Reinvest profits into further bootstrapped ventures.
3. Invest excess cash in other companies.
4. For capital-heavy, winner-take-all opportunities, raise capital and read the terms closely.
5. With a track record and proprietary deal flow, raise a fund.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Wanting shortcuts.
- Taking friends' and family's money on your first business.
- Ignoring term sheets. Chiron's first exit was "many tens of millions," but liquidation preferences and ratchets paid investors first, so the founders got less than expected.
- Expecting 50–100%+ annual returns from investing.
- Becoming "rich on paper but… a slave" in fund management. You have many masters: LPs, regulators, entrepreneurs, customers.

## Examples / case details
- His path:
  - Bootstrapped: gym → Prestige Labs (supplements) → Alan (software).
  - Invested: ACQ, through HQ Ventures (SMB tech) and PE-style deals with a service layer.
  - Raised capital: Skool, venture-backed. That funds $9/mo pricing.
  - Fund management: GP in ACQ real-estate buildings, raised privately from clients.
- Fund math example:
  - $100M fund: GP $5M plus $95M LP capital, plus $200M of debt, buys $300M of businesses.
  - At a 10% base case it doubles in about 7 years to $600M.
  - At 20%/yr for 6 years it's 2.98× ≈ $900M.
  - Out of that comes $200M of debt, about $100M of interest, $95M of LP capital back and about $40M of 6% pref, leaving $465M.
  - He then says 10% of that is $46.5M and 20% is about $90M on a $5M investment.
  - [inference: he describes a 50/50 GP/LP split of the $465M, which would be about $232M to the GP. His 10–20% figures look like conservative illustrations of the GP's own share. The arithmetic checks: 1.2^6 ≈ 2.99; 900 − 200 − 100 − 95 − 40 = 465.]
- Walnut-tree fund: black walnuts take 30 years to mature but cash-flow from year 3, then sell as lumber. The fund staggers annual cohorts. Worth his money but not his time.
