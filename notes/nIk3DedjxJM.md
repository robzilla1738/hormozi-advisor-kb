## Core thesis
If you're broke and don't know what to start, use a 5-step process: What (3 Ps) → Who (avatar) → How part 1 (good stuff) → How part 2 (bad stuff) → Combine into one sentence. Two bonuses: a unique mechanism and a warm-outreach script for your first 5 customers. Pick your "best bad idea" fast and make it less wrong through feedback. You won't pick it perfectly the first time. Includes Skool Games promos: he says more than 1 in 2 people who start a paid Skool community make their first dollar.

## Frameworks & models
- **3 Ps (the what)**: Pain you overcame yourself (e.g., a mom of 9 kids with a system for making lunches); Profession (e.g., a hospital dietitian who now teaches RDs to bill insurance, has 5,800 IG followers and takes home almost $1M/yr); Passion (his T Nation obsession led to a gym).
- **3 Whos**: people like you (his recommended pick); people you've helped before (paid or unpaid); underserved or emerging markets (the more formal, trend-analysis route). People like you → "missionaries"; market analysis → "mercenaries."
- **Avatar narrowing**: choose at least 3 of age / gender / profession / pains / interests, e.g., "35-year-old male accountants who are bored at their jobs." Test: if you could fill a room with just these people, would you get excited to go in and help them?
- **Specificity → price ladder**: a time management PDF sells for ~$19; for salespeople ~$199; for outbound salespeople ~$1,999; for outbound reps selling garden and power tools ~$10,000. Fewer competitors means a "blue ocean."
- **How part 1 (upside)**: the value equation written in the positive: easier, guaranteed, faster, the dream outcome, delivered exactly the way they want it.
- **How part 2 (downside)**: the risks avoided, the slowness avoided, and two kinds of sacrifice: bad things they'd normally have to *start* doing, and good things they'd normally have to *give up*. "What makes things hard is doing things we don't like and giving up things we like."
- **Motivation has only two levers**: give them more good stuff, or take away bad stuff.
- **Mad Lib**: "I help [who] get [good stuff] without [bad stuff] (through [unique mechanism])."
- **Unique mechanism**: a checklist (do all of these, in any order) or steps (do these in sequence). Wrap it and name it, using rhyme or alliteration. Examples: P90X "muscle confusion," Weight Watchers points.
- **Content structure**: promise, proof, plan.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Don't wait for the perfect pick; picking starts the feedback loop.
- The avatar needs 3+ descriptors.
- Narrower niche = higher price and fewer competitors.
- Leave out the unique mechanism until you have reps and a real process.
- First 5 customers: message people 4 hrs/day or until you reach 100 contacts per day.

## Playbook steps
1. Pick one P.
2. Pick the who, preferably people like you, and narrow it with 3+ descriptors.
3. If the avatar isn't you, interview prospects ("ask people who you want to sell"). Ask what they struggle with, for how long, what makes it painful, what their ideal experience looks like, what they hate having to start doing, and what they don't want to give up.
4. List the good stuff and the bad stuff using specific pains ("stop feeling your thighs chafing").
5. Write the sentence "I help X get Y without Z."
6. Warm outreach: greet them, give a specific compliment, insert your sentence, and ask if they know anyone who could benefit. Do this 4 hrs/day or 100/day. (Covered in the Leads book, chapter on warm outreach.)

## Mistakes he warns against
- Believing you must pick perfectly on the first try.
- Believing a narrow niche means less money.
- Vague claims ("lose weight fast"); people no longer believe generic promises.
- Starting purely from market analysis (mercenary), so you don't care about the customer.

## Examples / case details
- His first dollar: Sandy Smith asked for food help at a pizza shop, and after ~1.5 hrs she wrote him a $100 check without being asked.
- Gym Launch: the average gym made $30K in its first 30 days vs. owners' average take-home of $36K/yr.
- Spanx (he says it sold for ~$1B) and Facebook as examples of solving a personal problem.
- Sample sentences: "45-year-old women who just had kids get back into their high school jeans without giving up time with their family"; "non-fiction business authors get on the WSJ bestseller list without looking cringe."
