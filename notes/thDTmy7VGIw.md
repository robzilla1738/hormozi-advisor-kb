## Core thesis
Three steps to an "unstoppable" business: (1) stop selling small customers, because much of your churn is structural (customers go out of business or have volatile income) and no product fix solves it; (2) ask, don't tell, your customers, and iterate the product around what your best customers do; (3) don't pretend to be something you're not. Valuation comes from revenue quality and unit economics, not from labels like "software" or "platform."

## Frameworks & models
- **Structural churn**: churn built into the market you serve. A gym CRM had high churn because about a third of gyms go out of business every year. Choosing that market was your decision.
- **"Lost in the draft"** (credited to Chick-fil-A's head of people): teams lose championships in the draft, not the game. Picking the customer is the draft.
- **Volatility trap**: small business revenue goes up and down. You get them a bump, they get more customers than they can handle, they ease off, revenue drops, and they cancel. Every month it's "what have you done for me lately." The value you created doesn't carry forward: make them $10K at $1K/mo and the next month still starts at zero (actually -$1K).
- **Market pyramid by spending power**: consumers → prosumers → very small → SMB → low mid-market → mid-market → Fortune 100. He prefers to **start at the top** (less operational drag, more spending power, they stick, they know their numbers, they honor contracts) and then work down with cheaper, lower-service tiers. Revenue from the top pays for the near-zero-cost automated product needed to serve the bottom.
- **To serve beginners you need either** (a) a big one-time emotional "smash and grab" ticket (e.g. solar) or (b) an absurd price-to-value gap with a near-zero cost to serve (Netflix, Spotify, Walmart, software). The price-to-value gap has to be *bigger* for poorer customers.
- **Same product, narrower avatar, higher price**: time management $19 (mass) → $99 (salespeople) → $500 (outbound B2B sales) → $2,000 (outbound B2B power tools & gardening sales reps). The product barely changes.
- **Rank-and-build (PC) vs elegant (Mac) product iteration**: customers vote and you build the top request (gets you feature-rich "Frankenstein" products), or you reverse-engineer the ideal experience and deliberately remove things. Both work (Apple, Amazon), but "you just can't do both." Skool follows the Mac approach.
- **Two deletion questions**: "If only one thing remained, what would you keep?" and "If I kept everything but one thing, what wouldn't you care about?" Then quietly delete it and watch who complains. Ask whether the people complaining are customers you want.
- **Best-customer behavior loop**: survey to find the customers with the best results → interview them (demographics, user path/promise, actions only they took) → make new customers do those actions on purpose → measure the average customer's improvement → tie guarantee conditions to those actions (e.g. import all contacts into the CRM within 30 days).
- **Before / During / After**: map what happens immediately before purchase, before use, during use, and after. That's where you have the most leverage over results.
- **Lipstick on a pig**: sprinkling software on a service business still leaves a service business. You get valued on bottom-line multiples unless the economics actually match software.
- **What actually drives multiples**: revenue retention, incremental margin, logo retention, and the ability to let lower-skilled staff deliver high value (tech-enabled: one person serves 50 vs 5 → higher gross margin, lower talent cost, better retention).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If churn comes from customers failing, change who you serve. Don't keep rebuilding offers.
- Serving small customers requires a near-zero cost to serve. Otherwise price to their *worst* month. One agency charged $299/mo against the ~$1,500 industry standard, about a fifth of the price, so clients never cancelled.
- Big contracts from big companies are more reliable than small promises from beginners ("corporate debt is an entire investable asset class").
- Pay people who said no ~$100 for a call about why. The information is worth more, and sometimes you get a second shot at the sale.
- Ad problems: show ads to customers and ask what's missing. Sales problems: ask customers and non-buyers what wasn't compelling. Product problems: watch users use it.
- Build for ultimate enterprise value, not for a label. Treatment center example: an insurance-based model might get ~10x vs ~5–6x for cash-pay, but cash-pay charges 2–3x more, so return on effort can match or beat it.

## Playbook steps
1. Check your churn sources and remove structural churn by moving upmarket or to customers with steadier revenue.
2. Start with high-end customers, then add cheaper, lower-service tiers going down; automate to serve the bottom.
3. Niche the avatar to raise price without changing the product.
4. Pick one iteration philosophy (rank-and-build or elegant).
5. Use the deletion questions and silent deletes.
6. Run the best-customer behavior loop and tie the guarantee to those behaviors.
7. Interview non-buyers (paid) and test ads/sales with customers.
8. Stay what you are, improve the real metrics, and keep at it for a long time.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Blaming the product when the customer selection is the problem.
- Selling "out of your own wallet" to people like you who never become reliable ("foundation of sand").
- Assuming past results buy future months of retention.
- Targeting beginners with expensive products (e.g. beginner-only supplement lines, since beginners start and stop going to the gym).
- Trying to do both the PC and Mac product strategies.
- Rebranding as software/platform/tech-enabled to chase multiples; giving service away "free with the software" (the revenue still looks like service).
- Expecting it to be fast: "it always takes longer, costs more, and it's harder than you expected… it's the expectation that was the problem, not your plan."

## Examples / case details
- Gym CRM investment pitch: about a third of its customers went out of business every year.
- Big ad agencies serve the biggest clients, not small ones.
- Shopify (prosumers): he cites about 60% staying per year and 40% leaving as "the best in the world" for that segment (his stat).
- Alan (his software, third company after 2016): spent $1M+ building for gyms, who then asked for leads. He pivoted to agencies, which stay even as their own clients churn ("selling shovels").
- Solar company with 400 reps wanted to license its recruiting software for a software multiple. He told them it was a terrible idea: lower revenue per customer, a far smaller buyer pool, and founders who weren't software people.
- B2B software is hard: getting 5,000 customers to pay $1,000/mo is really tough, so valuations "equalize" against effort.
