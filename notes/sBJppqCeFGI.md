# Building a $5,000,000 Business for a Stranger in 42 Mins

## Core thesis
A live teardown of Raymond's chiropractic chain: 6 brick-and-mortar locations, all acquired, $5.2M TTM revenue, ~$1.2M profit (~23% net), 75% insurance. His goal is $5M EBITDA in 3 years and maybe a sale to an institutional buyer. The business is already sound: high-ticket packages ($2,400–3,600 over 60–90 days, about 1 in 7 cases worth up to $10K), an 80% show rate, a 71% close rate, CAC ~$700 and LTV ~$3,400 (~4.8:1). So Alex hunts for low-operational-risk levers instead of a new strategy. The main ones are: pour money into the high-intent Google channel (9:1 to 24:1 ROAS), add a paid low-ticket front-end offer, sell on day one at the point of greatest pain instead of day two, and machine the sales process so every location sells like the best one. He estimates these could roughly double revenue per store, so Raymond could hit his goal without opening new locations.

## Frameworks & models
- **Build vs buy expansion**: the two ways to add locations. Which one a founder chose tells you what problems to look for.
- **Build to sell vs build to keep**: Alex treats them as the same path. "All paths lead to building a better business."
- **Founder/owner magic**: the first location carries the founder's thumbprint, so later locations, especially bought ones, underperform it. That's why lead flow and standards slip when you scale.
- **Intent > interruption**: willingness to pay is proportional to spending power × pain. Google searchers are in pain right now, so they buy faster and bigger than people scrolling Facebook.
- **Sell at the point of greatest pain, not greatest satisfaction** (steak analogy). After you fix someone's pain, they no longer want the package. An upsell at the point of satisfaction has to open the pain loop for the next problem (more money → tax problem).
- **Expand the gap first**: in his gyms, every new member got on the scale at the start. Measure where they are against where they want to be.
- **Value equation** ($100M Offers): dream outcome × likelihood ÷ (time delay × effort). Making patients wait a day for the plan adds delay and friction.
- **Paid ad structure** ($100M Leads): the call-out is the most important part and the only thing most people read. Use a "yes question," e.g. "Attention Everett residents with back pain." Use the "what-who-when" framework for copy, put the offer up front, then proof, then a clear call to action. Compress the copy into bullets: struggle → what you've tried → better way → proof → limited availability.
- **Document → Demonstrate → Duplicate** ($100M Leads p.207): turn the best seller's process into a checklist, run it in front of the team, then have them run it in front of you.
- **Core Four**: he's doing paid ads. Local SEO / free content is the open lane, and long-tail local keywords are winnable.
- **Absolute return > efficiency**: spending $10K to make $50K (5:1, $40K net) beats a 10:1 that nets $9K. Expect efficiency to drop as you scale.
- **Scaling roadmap level 6** (20–49 employees): close efficiency on the core product becomes inconsistent. To graduate, install sales training, individual coaching and a KPI review cadence. (He plugs a free roadmap at acquisition.com/roadmap.)
- **Prioritize light work / high impact first**, then heavy work that other steps depend on. Check whether the people can execute before you change strategy: "the team's just not good enough" is often the real issue, not the model.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- A healthy close rate is about 30–40%, usually the sweet spot of price vs units. A close rate around 71% suggests the market will pay more, so consider raising prices.
- An 80% show rate means the leads are being worked well, so don't hunt for gaps there.
- If a channel returns $24 for every $1, put in as much as it will take. Tell the agency to double spend and find more keywords. Keep scaling down to about 5:1 ROAS. Doubling or tripling the allowable CPA can grow lead flow far more than proportionally.
- With an agency, speak in CPA/CAC/CPL targets. Give them a stated acceptable range that sits below your true maximum, as padding. As trust grows, move the two numbers closer together.
- Don't bring ads in-house while an agency is producing 9–24:1. It's not the priority.
- Agencies may base their spend recommendations on typical visit-to-visit chiropractors. If your LTV is higher, outspend everybody.
- Low-ticket front end: $19–29 for an X-ray + assessment, card taken over the phone, pushes show rates toward ~100%. Take the insurance card on the phone too, so the plan is ready when the patient arrives. If the $29 gets in the way, use a free offer but still collect the cards.
- When someone questions a process step ("why do you need my card/insurance?"), say "this is just how we've always done it." Appealing to policy stops the objection.
- Test a change at one location, then roll it out.

## Playbook steps
1. Raise Google spend as high as it will profitably go (stop at ~5:1).
2. Facebook: a $29 lead-magnet offer.
3. Tighter ad copy. Spy on the clients of high-ticket chiro agencies through the Ads Library and copy what works for all of them.
4. The setter collects the credit card and insurance card on the phone and verifies benefits ahead of time (some checks take 30–60 min).
5. Each morning, print the insurance-specific plan for every patient so the doctor has it on a clipboard.
6. Sell the same day: assessment → X-rays → prescriptive close → treatment afterward or the next day. The sales manager takes over the process.
7. Make the sales process a machine: a laptop deck turned toward the patient, or a clipboard script checked off and stapled to the contract. Model it on the top performer.
8. Local SEO on long-tail keywords for each location. Use reviews.
Estimated impact (his guesses): Google spend/front end = easy 2× lead flow; same-day sale = +10–15%; per-store efficiency could double; local SEO = +30–50% in about a year, almost all margin; sales process = ~20% lift in close rate. Roughly 6 months of work.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Selling after the pain is relieved ("I'm good, you fixed it").
- Not working leads quickly, often, personally and in a timely way. He calls this a huge hidden revenue hole for most small businesses.
- Ads where the offer is buried or missing ("let's just try this thing").
- Over-optimizing ROAS instead of growing absolute profit.
- Changing strategy when the real problem is the team's execution.
- Locations that get many 100%-covered patients stop practicing their sales skills and then blame "bad leads."
- Chiropractors selling one adjustment at a time instead of packages.
