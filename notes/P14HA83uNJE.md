## Core thesis
"Play dumb games, win dumb prizes." Many people live mediocre lives because they play games other people designed, where even winning brings a prize they don't want, often because staying in a familiar game avoids the discomfort of being a beginner somewhere else. Choose dominant games of your own design, where you want the prize and accept its price.

## Frameworks & models
- **Life as game design**: we play many games at once (business, fitness, relationships), some finite and some infinite.
- **Three questions for any game you're in**: 1) Did I design this game? 2) If not, do I want the prize? 3) Am I willing to pay the price, and is it globally worth it?
- **Local vs global (subset/superset)**: don't win a local game at the cost of the global one (your life).
- **Dominant game wins** (David Sacks on Founders Podcast): MySpace optimized for total users and Facebook for monthly active users. Win the dominant metric and the spoils of the inferior game come too.
- **Finite vs infinite games**: an infinite player (goal: keep playing) beats a finite player. His example is Vietnam, given explicitly as "not a political thing."
- **Comfort trap**: "just because you know how to win, doesn't mean you should win, and it doesn't mean the game is worth playing."

## Decision rules & thresholds
- If you're losing a game, first ask whether you'd even want to win it when played out to its natural end.
- If you compete, control the controllables and judge yourself by your own standard.
- If the goal is money, pick a game that pays (fitness competitions are "a terrible way of making more money").

## Playbook steps
1. List the games you're playing. 2. Run the three questions on each. 3. Exit games with prizes you don't want or prices too high. 4. Pick the dominant metric or game in your field. 5. Accept being a beginner in the better game.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Sacrificing for a trophy judged by people you don't care about.
- Assuming success in one arena (bodybuilding titles) transfers to business. Many champions go broke when their sponsorships end.
- Spending 5–10+ years in a familiar game to avoid sucking at something new.
