## Core thesis
"My business isn't scalable" usually means "it's hard and I want it to be easy." Every business can scale; the real question is how hard it is and which problems you have to solve. Difficulty is a feature, not a bug. The killer is expectations about *timelines*: founders break what already works (model, price, deliverable) while waiting for a slow fix they've already started to kick in. Change your timelines, not your dreams. Stick with one thing and say no to everything else.

## Frameworks & models
- **Scalability spectrum by category**: service (easy and cheap to start, profitable, harder to scale because quality depends on training humans) → e-commerce (middle: costs a bit more, scales faster, but runs into supply-chain and logistics problems) → SaaS/software (most time and money to start, much easier once at scale). Switching from one service business to another doesn't change your growth trajectory, because you're still constrained by humans. He says 78% of US businesses are service-based.
- **Demand-constrained vs supply-constrained**: a gym is demand-constrained (members are hard to find, trainers are easy). An accounting firm is supply-constrained (everyone needs accounting, but good accountants are scarce). Both are solved the same way: **promote**, to customers or to recruits. Businesses can swing between the two.
- **Features vs bugs** (credited to his CFO Suzanne): a feature comes standard with the business model and can't be removed (people problems in services, scarce and expensive developers in software). Some problems you *manage* rather than solve; treat them as overhead.
- **Fast fixes vs slow fixes**: fast fixes are solved and gone. Slow fixes (hiring that takes 3–6 months to ramp) make up most of a business career. You have to "sit with the fire."
- **Brick analogy**: of all the places a working brick could go, only one holds the building up. Moving something that works is unlikely to improve it.
- **Problem-trading / problem pile**: you never get "no problems," you only trade them. Before switching, list everything that sucks about the alternative path and ask whether you're better equipped to solve those problems. Usually you aren't, because you've never solved them. "The devil you know."
- **Pain 1 vs pain 2 vs pain 3**: changing course mid-fix gives you the original problem, a new problem, and the cost of change.
- **Decade timeline**: new idea to massive global company takes about 10 years (Microsoft IPO'd at ~7 years; OpenAI has been working since ~2017).
- **Old bull / young bull**, inverted for business: you can't pursue every opportunity. Pick one and compound on it for 30–40 years if it works.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Once you've diagnosed a constraint and started fixing it, don't change the model, price or deliverable while you wait. Example: going from 10 to 20 clients per account manager lowers delivery quality and raises churn.
- If you feel you *must* do something, put that energy into speeding up the solution you already chose (take interviews yourself, push the promotion cycle). Don't start a new initiative.
- When the supply fix arrives, don't lower your customer bar to fill capacity. Worse customers lead to churn and lower margins on top of new fixed payroll.
- Say no to all other opportunities. Add divisions only if they truly make sense, which is usually not the case. Usually "do more of the thing that's already working."
- The target state is no FOMO: "I know my game."

## Playbook steps
1. Diagnose whether you're supply- or demand-constrained.
2. Start the promotion cycle for that side: ads, outreach, network, then screening, interviews, offers and training, which takes 3–6 months to ramp.
3. Tell the team "we're already working on it" and refuse stopgap changes.
4. Speed up the chosen solution where you can.
5. Expect the next constraint to appear once this one is fixed, and repeat.
6. Reset your timeline expectations (think in decades).

## Mistakes he warns against
- Calling the business "not scalable" and switching to the "woman in the red dress."
- Breaking a working model while waiting for a slow fix.
- Arbitrary deadlines ("we have to double this year") that lead to cut corners and the wrong talent and customers.
- Thinking there shouldn't be problems ("you have a problem with your problems").
- Chasing every idea and getting none.

## Examples / case details
- Supply-constrained accounting firm: builds an accounting school and 20 school partnerships, then has too many accountants with empty calendars, overpays payroll, markets harder, takes worse customers, and churn and margins worsen. Payroll and revenue never line up neatly.
- Quotes Sam Altman: the idea or product is ~10% of the work and execution is ~90%, and you can't delegate the execution.
