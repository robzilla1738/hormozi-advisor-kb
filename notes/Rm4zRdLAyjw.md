## Core thesis
Education/coaching/course/membership businesses are the fastest, easiest way to $1–3M/yr (no inventory, almost no ops infrastructure, a team of ~10). For the same reasons, they are among the hardest to scale to $100M or sell: knowledge isn't sticky (low recurring retention), and there's triple key-man risk. Traditional businesses (chains, services) are slower and more capital-intensive early, but they build a sellable asset that compounds late. Decide on two things: (1) your goal (income vs a $10M+ exit) and (2) which problems you'd rather solve. Then commit and don't straddle.

## Frameworks & models
- **Growth curves**: info business = fast early, caps (a massive band of founders at $0.5–3M/yr). Traditional = slow early, "goes off the chart" late. Of founders who started from scratch without a brand: those at $100M he "can count on one hand" (all with deep brands, 30+ years), and those at $30M on two hands.
- **Why info caps**: (1) not sticky ("that's why four-year degrees are four years, not forever"), so retention is low; (2) three key-man risks: you bring the customers (ads/brand), you make the product and keep it current, and the team is loyal to you personally. Most founders also cap at the ~10-person team because they can't lead the next leadership layer.
- **Traditional business cap is the same leadership layer**: 1 city of 3–5 locations → 5 cities of 3–5 needs another layer of leadership. Going from 55 to 60 locations makes more money for almost no time; most quit "right when they reach a boss that they don't know how to beat."
- **Problems in each model**: Info = transfer what makes you special without them leaving with your customers, the talent/training business, and needing a brand because CAC rises into colder audiences (requires a consistently great product). Traditional = slower, more capital (new locations), but fewer succeed, so it becomes an asset.
- **Commitment = eliminating alternatives** (the Acquisition.com definition). "Focus is the measure of the amount of things that you say no to."
- **Representative test cohort instead of a "test kitchen"**: when switching to education, don't keep the old business to test plays. Test the way you'd roll out: from 100 customers pick 10, two from each 20% performance band across markets (Gym Launch). Top-10-only gives false positives; bottom-only gives false negatives.

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Goal of $1–3M/yr income (and willing to keep working) → info/education is the fastest path.
- Goal of a $10M+ exit / an asset that runs without you → stick with the traditional business (accept slower growth and reinvesting cash).
- If you're already well along the traditional curve (e.g., a $3–4M/yr business), stick with it; the best returns come at the end.
- Don't switch to coaching others in your industry because "impact" feels novel. The novelty wears off (he went numb after his 100th/500th/1000th weight-loss testimonial).
- When switching in either direction, cut or sell the old business completely. "I've yet to see anyone succeed big" straddling. A side business taking 10% of income will take 50–100% of mental bandwidth when it has a crisis.
- Expect goals to change (e.g., Alex Becker info → Hyros software; Sam Ovens consulting.com → Skool).

## Playbook steps
1. Write down your goal (income vs exit). 2. Choose which problem set you'd rather solve. 3. Commit by eliminating the alternative. 4. If going info: package niche knowledge where no "school" exists; build a brand and a great product; test new plays on a stratified 10-customer sample. 5. If going traditional: push through the next leadership layer; consider owning all locations vs franchising.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Straddling two businesses ("you can't ride two horses").
- Quitting a traditional business at the leadership "boss" for a shiny info business.
- Keeping the old business as a "test kitchen."
- Mediocre products that are only a little better than average, so the brand compounds against you.
- Testing on only your top performers.

## Examples / case details
- Three owners considering switching: a $4M/yr painting business, a 6-location lash salon at $3–4M, and a backyard-garden setup micro-business.
- Gym Launch: no textbook for running a gym (membership scripts, lobby, empty hours, trainer pay).
- Acquisition.com bought out the franchisees of a recently acquired chain, taking 30+ locations corporate. Why make 8% royalties when opening a franchise location takes ~50% of the work of an owned one? Also, franchisees can't be fired.
- Panda Express: ~2,600 company-owned locations, nearly $4B in sales, 27% net margin (~$1B/yr). The founder didn't start coaching other restaurant owners at 5 locations.
- Skool is in year 5–6 with less cash flow than consulting.com but scaling faster as an asset. Skool Games: 54.1% of paid-community starters make their first dollar online.
- Sold his last education business for $46.2M after $100M+ in sales.
