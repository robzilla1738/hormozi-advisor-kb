## Core thesis
"More" (more volume of what already works) is the highest risk-adjusted return move in business. Change has a fixed cost and a variable reward, so most tweaks lose money. Maximize absolute returns, not relative ones; diminishing returns are still returns. Pick very few big bets per year, and otherwise do a "violent, unreasonable amount" of the thing that works, for a long time. The transcript ends mid-sentence ("what's stopping…").

## Frameworks & models
- **More / Better / New**: this video focuses on More. The fundamental question is "Why can't I do more?" The big unlocks come after the point where you think you can't do more. "Quantity has a quality unto itself" (attributed to Napoleon).
- **Why more is the highest risk-adjusted return**: finding something that works took many tries, so changing it is statistically unlikely to beat it. Jam more resources into the working machine.
- **Change: fixed cost, variable reward**: by his observation, a change involving people typically causes a ~20% drop in performance (retraining, side effects). It may recover to a ~5% higher baseline or not at all. Serial changers live permanently below their potential.
- **Leaving things alone compounds**: with no changes, people get better at their jobs, giving ~1–3% month-over-month gains. "Repetition is the father of skill." His most profitable periods were when the business was "really boring… blocking and tackling."
- **ICE**: Impact × Confidence × Ease. Only take bets where the upside clearly beats the guaranteed ~20% hit. His minimum is >20%, ideally something that could double the business.
- **Strategy = "what do I want to eat," not "what's in the fridge"** (from Chiron). Decide the goal, then get the ingredients.
- **Maximizers vs optimizers**: optimizers seek the most from the least; maximizers seek the most, period. Absolute beats relative. $100→$1,000 (net $900) vs $200→$1,200 (net $1,000): scaling still made more. He'd take $1M→$2M (2:1) over a small high-ratio campaign "every day of the week." [inference: the transcript's "$10,000 and making a hundred back, 10 to one" is a verbal slip; 10:1 on $10K would be $100K back. The point stands either way.]
- **Optimization ceiling**: a 30% opt-in page will never reach 300%, but inputs can be 10×'d.
- **Core Four, scaled in reverse order of risk (paid)**: (1) more creative → more winners → more markets/avatars/segments; (2) more spend (from $100/day to $1,000/day: what stops you?); (3) more platforms (IG, TikTok, X). Content scales the same way, by adding editors.
- **Fixed-volume forcing function**: commit to fixed volume (100 calls, 100 minutes of content). The pain drives efficiency (best pickup times, data).
- **Volume you can't see**: to beat someone doing 3× your visible volume, do 10–30×, since they also do work you can't see.
- **Rooms test**: top 1% = win a room of 100; 0.1% = a room of 1,000; 0.01% = a stadium of 10,000. "You cannot make yourself exceptional and live a normal life." "Get better, never stop" (from the ACQ sales handbook).

## Decision rules & thresholds
- Small businesses (≤10–20 employees) should pick about one big change per year. Resource scarcity forces prioritization.
- Don't take a change whose likely upside is under the ~20% performance dip.
- If one move could double the business, don't do three.
- Prefer larger absolute profit even at a lower ratio, while keeping LTV:CAC healthy.
- Plan backward from the goal: volume needed → resources required → is it worth it? Then what's stopping you (usually nothing)?
- Don't judge a volume test after 1–2 days.
- Don't "throw rocks" at people ahead of you; they're better in some way, so learn from them.

## Playbook steps
1. Identify what currently works.
2. Ask "Why can't I do more?" and list the constraints.
3. Ads: increase creative volume first, then spend, then platforms.
4. Calculate the resources needed for the goal volume (e.g., editors), and hire or contract them.
5. Hold fixed daily volume for months; optimize inside that volume.
6. Limit new experiments to ~1 high-ICE bet a year.

## Mistakes he warns against
- Using the business to feed your own ADD and need for novelty; "the business thrives on same."
- Endlessly optimizing ratios (opt-in %) instead of scaling inputs.
- Thinking small: not asking how to 10× traffic from $1M to $10M to $100M.
- Underestimating the volume required and your own capacity.
- Explaining yourself to people who don't support you.
- Being an "optimizer" who coasts (his own high school story).
